Status: Provisional
Domain(s): Medicine — acute pain management and analgesic-trial methodology (target field). Adjacent: PRO psychometrics (response shift, longitudinal measurement invariance); health econometrics (reporting heterogeneity); psychophysics (range–frequency context effects).

Question (canonical): In adult inpatients with an acute pain episode scored serially for >= 48 h, with a then-test at a randomly assigned day and a pre-registered external anchor set (opioid-equivalent consumption/PCA demands, QST, a functional milestone such as time to first ambulation): (a) does a longitudinal measurement-invariance model of the serial 0–10 NRS, anchors as reference, reject intercept (scalar) invariance across the episode; (b) under a pre-declared operationalisation of recalibration (then-test difference, or fitted intercept drift), what fraction of the mean observed within-episode NRS reduction is recalibration rather than change in anchored pain; and (c) at the NRS <= 4 titration target and the 2-point/30% MCID, what misclassification rate of "target attained"/"MCID achieved" does that recalibration induce? Both estimates must be shown not to be recall error, via randomised then-test timing and an anchor-only refit.
(V2; the single Gate 1 reformulation, spent to pin population, operationalisation, anchor set and estimands. V1 and full text in batches/2026-10-17-0742/UQ-20261017-17-record.md.)

Alternative formulations:
- When a post-operative patient's score falls from 8 to 4 over two days, how much of the fall is the pain changing and how much is the patient's internal "10" changing?
- Is the acute-pain NRS longitudinally invariant over hours to days within one episode, and if not, what bias does that put into NRS-change endpoints and into protocols that titrate opioids to a fixed NRS threshold?

Motivating gap:
Source method: presupposition inversion. The inverted settled claim is that a falling NRS means improving pain, which presupposes a fixed sensation-to-number mapping over the episode. Recalibration response shift is established for bodily pain in surgical patients at the 3-month scale (P1) and is large and common in surgical PROMs at 1–2 years (P2), but has not been examined on the hours-to-days timescale, on the single-item NRS, or with a dosing decision attached to the number. Two adjacent findings sharpen rather than close the gap: the acute field ran a 2,661-patient serial-PROM study in exactly this window and asked only responsiveness (PROMPT NIT-1, BJA 2024; median sensitivity-to-change 0.22), and it has documented cross-sectional misclassification by a fixed NRS cut-off (P3: 17% over-/5% under-treatment at NRS > 4). Neither asks whether the scale itself moves during the episode.

Presuppositions:
1. Experienced pain and its rating are separable in principle. CONTESTED; principal dissolution risk. Mitigated, not removed, by declaring the estimand anchor-relative.
2. A then-test (or equivalent invariance model) can distinguish recalibration from recall error. CONTESTED; hence randomised then-test timing plus anchor-only refit.
3. A non-recalibrating anchor exists over the episode. CONTESTED — and the gate-limiting item: see Strongest remaining objection.
4. The NRS is used as if interval-scaled with stable anchoring in titration protocols and trial endpoints. ESTABLISHED.
5. Episodes with >= 24–72 h of serial scoring are common. ESTABLISHED (routine 4-hourly charting).
6. A then-test rating is obtainable without the clinical context cueing a socially desirable answer. CONTESTED — and now evidenced against: van Dijk 2016, Bosdet 2021 (below).
7. (Gate 1) Floor/ceiling and the "worst pain imaginable" anchor do not themselves produce the whole effect; a mechanism of recalibration, but modelled separately.

What would count as an answer:
A within-episode then-test plus a longitudinal invariance analysis of serial NRS against a pre-registered external anchor: a recalibration share of observed NRS change, under at least two pre-declared operationalisations, plus the misclassification rate of "titration target attained" and "MCID achieved". The estimate must be shown not to be recall error by design (randomised then-test timing; anchor-only refit). A null — scalar invariance holds within the episode — is a clean answer and retires the concern for both titration protocols and NRS-change endpoints.

Significance — consequence:
If yes and the share is material, two things read as measurements become partly measurements of the instrument. (i) NRS-change endpoints in acute analgesic trials carry a bias whose sign depends on whether recalibration runs with or against true improvement; if common to both arms it attenuates contrasts and would partly explain PROMPT NIT-1's median sensitivity-to-change of 0.22, and if it differs by arm (plausible where arms differ in opioid exposure or in severe-pain experience) it biases the treatment effect. Note P4's range–frequency prediction runs the other way for early scores, so the sign is not derivable a priori. (ii) Protocols titrating opioids to a fixed threshold would be acting on a moving target — a direct opioid-stewardship consequence. If no (scalar invariance holds), the concern is retired for both uses and the response-shift literature's reach into acute endpoints is bounded.

Significance — audience:
Acute pain services and authors of inpatient pain-assessment/opioid-titration protocols; acute analgesic-trial methodologists and core-outcome groups (IMMPACT/ACTTION, the PROMPT/standardised-endpoint collaborations); regulators assessing acute analgesic endpoints; the response-shift/PRO psychometrics community. Specifiable actions: add an anchor or then-test to the acute protocol, re-derive the MCID on an invariance-corrected scale, add an anchored co-primary endpoint, or stop worrying. Adversary #2's counter stands partly: P3 published a cleaner, non-anchor-relative indictment of the NRS > 4 threshold fourteen years ago and the convention persists, so the trial-methodology audience is the more responsive one and the opioid-stewardship consequence is the weakest limb of the case.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens whether anchored/calibrated acute scales (vignette-anchored, QST-referenced, forced-choice) track change better than the free NRS; whether the 2-point/30% MCID is partly a recalibration artefact; whether recalibration is itself a worthwhile treatment effect rather than a bias; whether it differs by opioid exposure, by whether the index event reset the patient's worst-ever pain, and by first vs repeat episode; and whether the same drift affects the other bedside single-item scales on the same cadence (dyspnoea, nausea, sedation).

Significance — anchoring:
Three standing anomalies rather than an invented one: the documented mismatch between NRS thresholds and patients' own bearability (P3); the unexplained low sensitivity-to-change of acute postsurgical PROMs in a large multicentre study (PROMPT NIT-1); and the open methodological dispute over whether then-test recalibration estimates are recall artefacts, which a short-recall design would inform.
