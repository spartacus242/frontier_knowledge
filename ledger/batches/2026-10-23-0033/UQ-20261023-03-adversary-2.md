## 1. Restatement in my own words (not the generator's)

Take an engineer's question about a metal strut — *how many load cycles until it breaks, and does the damage from different-sized loads simply add up?* — and point it at the wall of a single bacterium. Concretely: trap one *E. coli*, hit it over and over with the same dilution shock, each one gentle enough that it would survive it once, and ask whether the chance of bursting on the n-th hit depends on **n** at all once you have accounted for the biggest hit it has ever had. If it does, ask three sub-questions: does the number of hits to bursting fall off as a straight line on log–log axes against hit size (an S–N curve); is there a hit size small enough that the cell never bursts however many you give it (an endurance limit); and if you give it a block of big hits then a block of small ones, do the two blocks' used-up fractions add to 1 at the moment it bursts (Miner's rule). Run the whole thing twice, once with the cell growing and rebuilding its wall, once with wall building switched off, because a self-repairing strut is not what fatigue theory was built for — and that repair-versus-damage race is the actual content of the question.

My reading of what the question is *for*: it is a bid to install a **state variable** (accumulated damage) in a field that currently models the sacculus as a shell with a *strength*. Everything else is scaffolding.
