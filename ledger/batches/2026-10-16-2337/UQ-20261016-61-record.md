Domain(s): Medicine — rare-disease diagnostics; clinical epidemiology; health-systems performance measurement. Method-owning field: biostatistics of truncated time-to-event data / infectious-disease back-calculation and nowcasting.

Question (canonical, as intaken):
For a rare-disease population with datable symptom onset, does the reported fall in time-to-diagnosis survive estimation from an incident symptom-onset frame with right truncation and delay-dependent inclusion modelled explicitly — i.e. is the diagnostic odyssey measurably shortening, or is the apparent improvement an artefact of counting only patients who were eventually diagnosed?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — the one permitted reformulation, for well-posedness only; both versions retained):
In one named data source that enumerates symptom-onset cohorts rather than diagnosed patients (candidates: a national genomic-medicine service referral spine, an undiagnosed-diseases programme intake, or EHR/claims first-symptom coding for a pre-specified set of rare conditions), estimate for successive onset cohorts c (i) the onset-to-diagnosis distribution F_c under explicit right truncation at the data-cut date, with a pre-stated inclusion model allowing inclusion probability to depend on delay, and (ii) the naive estimate computed on the same records restricted to the already-diagnosed; then test whether the sign and magnitude of the published trend in mean/median delay are preserved, and decompose any difference into truncation artefact, case-mix change (which diseases get diagnosed as sequencing diffuses) and change in the diagnosis hazard itself. "Rare-disease population" and "the diagnostic odyssey" as an aggregate are replaced by a named frame and a pre-registered disease list, because the aggregate indicator has no incidence denominator.

Alternative formulations:
- Is the headline rare-disease diagnostic-delay figure (~5 years) and its reported downward trend identified at all from prevalent diagnosed cohorts, and what delay distribution does a truncation-corrected back-calculation estimator give for recent symptom-onset cohorts?
- Everyone reports that rare-disease patients wait about five years for a diagnosis and that the wait is falling, but the number is computed only from people who were eventually diagnosed — if the still-undiagnosed are accounted for, is the wait actually falling?

Motivating gap (as intaken; see caveat under Novelty):
Faye et al., Eur J Hum Genet 2024;32:1116-1126 (Rare Barometer; 6,507 respondents, 41 countries, restricted to respondents with a confirmed diagnosis) reports mean total diagnostic time 4.7 y, states the indicator "does not include RD patients who are still waiting for a confirmed diagnosis", states that "this study does not allow to monitor the evolution of TDT over time", says recruitment bias "cannot be estimated precisely", and scores 48% waiting >1 y against the IRDiRC goal. Benito-Lozano et al., Orphanet J Rare Dis 2022 (10.1186/s13023-022-02530-3; Spanish Rare Diseases Patient Registry, 3,304 patients) reports mean 6.18 y / median 2 y, fits joinpoint trends (−5.1%/yr 1974–2021; a −55.03%/yr segment 2018–2021, p=0.014), cautions that the post-2018 drop "may be affected by the fact that all the study participants" were already diagnosed because "those with recent symptom onset have not yet had the opportunity to be diagnosed and included in this study", and discards its own most recent segment. Progress against an explicit international target (IRDiRC: diagnosis within one year; Rare 2030: six months) is therefore judged on an estimator whose identification nobody has examined, and the two biases act in opposite directions — right truncation makes recent onset cohorts look fast, advocacy-panel recruitment over-represents long odysseys — so even the sign of the published trend is unestablished.

Presuppositions:
- P1 Published headline delay levels and trends are computed from already-diagnosed (prevalent) samples — ESTABLISHED. Independently confirmed here for a third source: Perez-Alamino et al., RMD Open 2025;11(1):e004756 groups 3,984 SpA patients by decade of disease onset and states patients "must have been diagnosed with SpA, otherwise they would not be included in the study."
- P2 Patients still awaiting diagnosis are excluded from those frames — ESTABLISHED (quoted in intake from Faye et al.; independently confirmed for the rheumatology analogue by Feldtkeller & Rudwaleit, who write that the target study "neglected in their analysis and interpretation all the patients with recent symptom onset who were not yet diagnosed").
- P3 Symptom-onset and diagnosis dates are recoverable accurately enough to estimate a delay distribution — CONTESTED. Perez-Alamino et al. list recall bias among their limitations; Benito-Lozano et al. (intake) call onset/validity limits "usual in these types of studies" and "further aggravated" for diagnoses made decades ago.
- P4 "Date of diagnosis" is a single well-defined event — CONTESTED. Ferrandiz-Espadin et al., J Rheumatol 2025 (jrheum.2024-0574) had to define the clock's start twice (first documented back pain; or first documented associated condition) and dropped 761 → 404 patients to get a usable start date, which shows the event boundary is an analyst's choice, not a fact in the record.
- P5 A data source with an incidence-anchored frame exists or can be constructed — CONTESTED AND LIMITING. Unverified in this run: I found no rare-disease source that enumerates onset cohorts including the undiagnosed. Without one the question degenerates into a demand for such a frame. This is the candidate's load-bearing weakness and the reformulation only names candidate frames, it does not establish any.
- P6 The delay distribution is identifiable under right truncation given a stated inclusion model — PARTLY ESTABLISHED, with a named caveat. Seaman, Presanis & Jackson, Stat Methods Med Res 2022;31(9):1641-1655 review exactly this estimation problem and, per the publication record I opened, "discuss at length whether the distribution is identifiable, which is a particular concern at the start of" the observation window. So identification is a studied, conditional property, not an assumption the candidate may help itself to.
- P7 Recruitment probability is correlated with delay — CONTESTED and unestimated. Independently corroborated in kind: Perez-Alamino et al. name channelling bias ("Patients with very mild and very severe disease would be less likely to be included").
- P8 Case mix is not held fixed across periods — flagged; must be modelled, not assumed away.
- P9 Someone acts on the number — ESTABLISHED in kind (IRDiRC one-year and Rare 2030 six-month goals are scored against it).

What would count as an answer:
A pre-registered estimation, in at least one population with datable onset and an incidence-anchored frame, of (i) the delay distribution for successive symptom-onset cohorts with right truncation handled (back-calculation / reporting-delay deconvolution, with a sensitivity band over inclusion models and worst-case delay-dependent-inclusion bounds), reported beside (ii) the naive prevalent-frame estimate computed on the same data, so the artefact is measured rather than argued; plus (iii) a decomposition of the published trend into truncation artefact, case-mix change and genuine change in the diagnosis hazard. Both directions are informative: agreement vindicates the field's indicator and makes the IRDiRC target assessable; divergence in sign means every published claim of improvement, and the targets set against it, rest on an artefact.

PRIOR 1 (near-duplicate of the question itself). Feldtkeller E, Rudwaleit M. "Comment on: Diagnostic delay in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis: results from the Danish nationwide DANBIO registry." - Unit of analysis: individual ankylosing-spondylitis patients in a national registry (DANBIO), grouped by symptom-onset period; the letter's own figure uses a 1996 survey of 1,380 patients.
- Endpoint: diagnostic delay, symptom onset to AS diagnosis, in years.
- Reference/comparator: earlier symptom-onset decades vs later; the calendar-time trend since 2000 claimed by the target paper.
- Estimand: mean delay by onset period and its calendar trend, among the diagnosed — which the letter argues is not the quantity of interest and is not estimable from the register for recent onset periods.
- What it claims: the claimed post-2000 drop is unsupported because the analysis "neglected ... all the patients with recent symptom onset who were not yet diagnosed"; the registry means are "a realistic approximation only for former disease-onset decades"; "additional diagnoses have to be expected mainly for patients with recent disease onset"; the letter allows the delay may genuinely have fallen (better imaging, awareness) but says these data cannot show it, and its only remedy is to wait — "we need another 20 years passing by."

PRIOR 2 (the naive estimator the candidate wants to beat, with the bias named and uncorrected). Perez-Alamino R, Maldonado-Ficco H, Moltó A, Waimann C, Maldonado-Cocco J, Dougados M, Landewé R, van der Heijde D, Van den Bosch F. "Trends to shorter diagnostic delay in spondyloarthritis patients during the last decades and association with clinical presentation: data from ASAS-COMOSPA study." RMD Open 2025;11(1):e004756. https://rmdopen.bmj.com/content/11/1/e004756 (opened in full).
- Unit of analysis: individual SpA patients meeting ASAS criteria, N=3,984, 22 countries, enrolled Feb 2013–Sep 2014, grouped by decade of disease onset.
- Endpoint: time from disease onset to SpA diagnosis; also binarised as early diagnosis (≤2 years).
- Reference/comparator: onset decade (and initial clinical presentation: axial / peripheral / extra-musculoskeletal / multiple).

- What it claims: delay has shortened across onset decades. The truncation is acknowledged only in limitations ("the cohort recruitment finished already a decade ago"; patients "must have been diagnosed with SpA, otherwise they would not be included in the study"), alongside channelling bias and recall bias. (Noted while reading: the abstract's early-diagnosis proportions and odds ratios do not match the Discussion's, so even the uncorrected trend is reported inconsistently.)

PRIOR 3 (the method, aimed at a different object). Seaman SR, Presanis A, Jackson C. "Estimating a time-to-event distribution from right-truncated data in an epidemic: A review of methods." Statistical Methods in Medical Research 2022;31(9):1641-1655, DOI 10.1177/09622802211023955. https://researchportal.ukhsa.gov.uk/en/publications/estimating-a-time-to-event-distribution-from-right-truncated-data/ (record + abstract opened; full text not fetched).
- Unit of analysis: individuals who have already experienced the event by the sampling date (illustration: people who had died with COVID-19 by 5 April 2020).
- Endpoint: time from symptom onset to death.
- Reference/comparator: rival estimators of the marginal distribution, compared on statistical efficiency; no substantive comparator group.
- Estimand: the marginal time-to-event distribution under right truncation, covariate effects on it, and the conditions under which it is identifiable.
- What it claims: right truncation biases observed times toward shorter durations and needs specialised estimators; identifiability is a live concern early in the observation window. The review names no diagnosis-delay or health-indicator application, and the page states no application outside infectious disease.

A near-duplicate of the canonical question exists in print in the same indicator literature (Prior 1), the data-side papers name the bias themselves (Prior 2 verified; Faye and Benito-Lozano per intake), and the structurally identical question has been asked and answered with the candidate's own estimator in HIV surveillance. ASKED BUT OPEN: the estimation limb — a truncation-corrected delay distribution for rare-disease onset cohorts reported beside the naive estimate, with a delay-dependent-inclusion sensitivity band and a truncation/case-mix/hazard decomposition — I could not find anywhere in 11 queries across the data field, two neighbouring clinical fields, the lay register, and the method-owning field. Source establishing it is open: Feldtkeller & Rudwaleit, ARD 73(12):e77, which poses the question and explicitly declines to answer it ("we need another 20 years passing by"), together with Perez-Alamino et al. Significance — consequence:
If the trend survives correction: the field's indicator is vindicated at the level of sign, the IRDiRC one-year and Rare 2030 six-month targets become assessable against an estimator with stated identification conditions, and a correction factor becomes available so registries can keep publishing comparable numbers. If it does not survive — in particular if the sign flips — then the published improvements computed on prevalent diagnosed cohorts (the Spanish registry's −5.1%/yr and the −55%/yr 2018–2021 segment, the ASAS-COMOSPA onset-decade trend, the Danish DANBIO decline, and the national rare-disease action-plan dashboards built on them) are artefacts of the sampling frame, and the targets scored against them cannot be scored at all; the field would need a replacement indicator stated on an estimable quantity (diagnosis hazard per onset cohort, or undiagnosed prevalence). The decomposition is informative even if the sign holds, because it separates "sequencing diffused and now diagnoses easier diseases faster" from "the odyssey got shorter for the same patients."

Significance — audience:
Concretely: (1) IRDiRC's and Rare 2030's indicator groups, who would have to restate a target currently written against a quantity their own cited studies say "does not allow to monitor the evolution ... over time"; (2) registry epidemiologists at ISCIII (Spanish Rare Diseases Patient Registry), EURORDIS Rare Barometer, Orphanet and national genomic-medicine services, who would change what they print beside the delay figure and whether they report recent onset cohorts at all; (3) the ASAS/rheumatology delay literature, where the same design is still in use and the same objection was lodged in 2014 and never settled; (4) the truncated-data biostatistics community (MRC Biostatistics Unit / UKHSA lineage of Prior 3 and the HIV analogue), for whom a delay-dependent-inclusion extension is a tractable methods target.

Significance — fecundity:
Makes askable: how many people are living with an undiagnosed rare disease, as an estimated quantity with an interval rather than a slogan; whether delay-dependent recruitment can be bounded empirically by overlapping an advocacy panel with a registry or health-system frame; whether genomic-service diffusion moves the diagnosis hazard or only the case mix; and whether the same artefact inflates improvement claims in the adjacent delay literatures that use the identical onset-cohort design (axSpA, endometriosis, cancer diagnostic intervals, TB patient delay).

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the identifiability problem for right-truncated distributions (Prior 3), the HIV undiagnosed-prevalence estimation programme (Birrell 2012; van Sighem 2015), the REST guideline for reporting time-to-diagnosis studies (BMC Med 2016, 10.1186/s12916-016-0690-7 — lead, not opened), the stock-versus-flow sampling problem in duration econometrics, and the unresolved ARD correspondence (73(12):e77 / 74(3):e12).

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary boundary plus an incentive gap — and the silence is partial, not total, which is itself the finding. The estimator belongs to infectious-disease biostatistics, where right-truncation correction and back-calculation were developed for epidemics with a biomarker clock (CD4) and an incidence-anchored surveillance frame. The rare-disease delay indicator is produced by advocacy survey teams and registry epidemiologists whose analytic repertoire is means, medians and joinpoint regression on diagnosed patients. Both sides can see the bias: the data side names it in limitations paragraphs, the methods side has the estimators on CRAN, and neither owns the join. On top of that, the data owners cannot fix it with their own data — a patient panel has no denominator of undiagnosed people (P5), so naming the bias and discounting a segment is the most its holders can do, and they do exactly that. The incentive runs the same way: the five-year figure and its decline are advocacy instruments and the scorecard for an international target, so an estimate that might show no improvement is one nobody is funded to compute. That is why the question appears as a comment letter and a limitations sentence rather than as a paper.
