## Restatement (my words, before attacking)

Take human-written formal libraries (mathlib, Lean blueprint projects) and the subset of theorem declarations that explicitly cite a specific published theorem. Compare each formal statement's hypotheses and axiom usage against the paper's statement (after fixing a rule for how the paper's standing conventions count). Of these, what share is logically weaker than the paper (hypotheses dropped or generalized, fewer axioms) versus strictly stronger (the formalizer had to assume more), split into what a machine detects (unused binders, typeclass weakening, `#print axioms`) and what a human judge decides on a sample — and does the weaker share track the paper's age, length or subfield? In one line: at corpus scale, how often does formalization show that published theorems were stated with more hypotheses than they need, signed against the opposite case?

Note on the record's sign convention, which I keep below: "formalized weaker" = fewer/weaker hypotheses = the formal THEOREM is more general; "formalized stronger" = more hypotheses = the formal theorem is less general.
