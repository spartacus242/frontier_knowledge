Domain(s): Medicine — clinical microbiology, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and breakpoint standard-setting; donor field: veterinary/aquaculture AST (CLSI VET03/VET04 species-appropriate incubation temperatures)

Question (canonical):
When a standard broth-microdilution panel is incubated at the measured temperature of the infection site rather than at the standard 35 ± 2 °C, what fraction of susceptible/intermediate/resistant calls flips, and does the flip rate concentrate in particular agent classes?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — the version to carry forward; one reformulation used, for well-posedness only):
For a pre-specified isolate x agent panel (isolates from cool-site infections — chronic wound, diabetic-foot, burn eschar, CF airway — plus a bacteraemia set), tested by ISO 20776-1 broth microdilution in replicate and in parallel at 35 °C and at fixed non-standard temperatures (31 °C, 33 °C for the cool arm; 39.5 °C for the febrile arm), with reading rules and reading time pre-specified and a reading-time-only control arm at 35 °C: what is the paired within-isolate S/I/R discordance rate versus the 35 °C arm under current EUCAST/CLSI breakpoints, is it larger than the replicate discordance rate measured at 35 °C in the same experiment, and is the excess concentrated in identifiable agent classes?
(Reformulation rationale: "the measured temperature of the infection site" is not an estimand — site temperature is heterogeneous in space and time, as the intake's own presupposition concedes — and "what fraction flips" is undefined without a fixed isolate x agent universe and a comparator. Fixing discrete temperatures, a fixed panel and the method's own replicate discordance as the comparator makes the quantity estimable. Both versions recorded; the original is retained above.)

Alternative formulations:
1. Are EUCAST/CLSI breakpoints, all established at 35 ± 2 °C, transportable to infections at cool tissue temperatures (chronic wound and diabetic-foot surfaces, burn eschar, CF airway at roughly 31-34 °C) and to febrile bacteraemia at 39-40 °C?
2. Is the incubation temperature written into the AST standard a hidden determinant of reported resistance rates for cool-site infections, in excess of the method's own reproducibility limit?

Motivating gap:
Aquatic-animal AST cannot assume a single incubation temperature: CLSI VET03/VET04 set species-appropriate temperatures (roughly 22-28 °C) and the field has had to study temperature explicitly when deriving epidemiological cut-off values (ECVs). Human clinical AST fixes 35 ± 2 °C for standardization and then applies the resulting breakpoints to infection sites that are demonstrably not at 35-37 °C. The transplant asks whether the standard's own thermal condition is a hidden classifier input.
Gate 2 outcome against this gap: the gap as stated is largely false. Human clinical microbiology did ask this, repeatedly, in the 1980s-90s, and answered the febrile half of it at panel scale in 1982. The intake's SEARCH NOTE (one query returning only aquaculture/veterinary ECV work) was an artefact of query vocabulary: dropping "infection-site temperature" and "breakpoint" from the query and using the plain own-field title form ("influence of incubation temperature on antimicrobial susceptibility testing", "effect of temperature on antimicrobial susceptibilities of <organism>") surfaces the own-field literature immediately.

Presuppositions:
1. Infection-site temperatures at the named sites differ from 35 ± 2 °C by enough to matter. Partly established — thermography and airway-temperature work exist and were touched in search (wound-bed temperature scoping review and infection-status study; neutrophil-elastase-mediated airway temperature rise). Not quantified here against the one-doubling-dilution relevance threshold.
2. Site temperature is heterogeneous in space and time, so "the infection-site temperature" is itself an abstraction. CONCEDED in the intake and handled by the Gate 1 reformulation (fixed discrete temperatures, not a measured per-patient value).
3. In-vitro incubation temperature is a defensible surrogate for the in-vivo thermal condition's effect on the drug-organism interaction. CONTESTED — temperature also changes host immune effectors, perfusion and local drug concentration, none of which the in-vitro arm represents. The question is scoped to the measurement's internal validity as a classifier input, not to clinical outcome. Note the 1982 prior already breached this boundary by pairing MICs with serum bactericidal activity at 35 vs 40 °C.
4. Current breakpoints are intended as organism-drug properties transportable across sites. True as deployed, but PK/PD-derived breakpoints already embed site-specific exposure assumptions, so the inversion is partial and must be stated per agent.
5. Growth at the lower temperatures is adequate for a readable panel within the standard incubation window. CONTESTED — slower growth may require re-specified reading times, and reading time is a confounder the design must separate from temperature (hence the reading-time-only control arm). An existing precision study of incubation temperature *and* time (Galway record, below) suggests this confound is recognised in the field.
6. Flip rates are comparable against the method's published reproducibility bound (essential agreement within one doubling dilution). Defensible, and this comparator is the one genuine methodological addition the candidate makes over the priors.
7. (Added at Gate 1) Current breakpoints applied to a non-35 °C MIC is a meaningful operation at all — i.e. the "flip" is defined by holding the breakpoint fixed while moving the measurement condition. If a respondent instead re-derives breakpoints per temperature, the question changes shape.

What would count as an answer:
A paired within-isolate experiment as specified in the reformulation. The answer is the matrix of MIC shifts in doubling dilutions and the S/I/R discordance rate versus the 35 °C result under current breakpoints, broken out by agent class, with the same experiment's replicate discordance at 35 °C as the comparator and the reading-time-only arm subtracted.
- "Temperature is a hidden determinant" = discordance for one or more agent classes materially exceeds replicate discordance at 35 °C.
- "No" = discordance lies within the reproducibility bound across the panel, which would positively establish the transportability the standard currently assumes.
This is specifiable, finite and falsifiable; the reformulated version has no evidence state that leaves it unanswerable.

vs prior 1 (1982, physiologic range): this is the candidate's febrile arm, already executed at panel scale (17 agents, 432 strains) with a larger temperature excursion than the candidate proposes. Differences are design-level only: the 1982 work reports the distribution of MIC fold-shifts, not the S/I/R discordance rate under today's breakpoints; it has no replicate-discordance comparator from the same experiment; it has no cool arm; its strains are not drawn from named cool-site infections; and it has no reading-time control. The candidate's febrile arm is therefore a re-analysis in category terms of a question already asked and answered, not a new question.
vs prior 2 (1984, 30 vs 35 °C, S. aureus/methicillin): this is a cool-arm category-flip rate at large isolate scale — 6.8% MIC discordance, 2.5% R-at-30/S-at-35 — i.e. the candidate's headline quantity, for one organism-drug pair. Differences: one agent, so it says nothing about concentration across agent classes; 30 °C is used as a detection-sensitivity device for heteroresistant MRSA rather than as a site-temperature surrogate, so the *motivation* is inverted; and no comparison to method reproducibility.

Significance — consequence:
If yes (excess discordance in one or more agent classes beyond replicate discordance): the 35 ± 2 °C clause in ISO 20776-1 / CLSI M07 stops being a pure standardization convention and becomes a reported determinant of the S/I/R label for a named patient population. Concretely, EUCAST and CLSI would face a choice between (a) a reporting caveat for cool-site specimens in the affected classes, (b) site-qualified breakpoints, or (c) explicit documentation that the shift is accepted and why. There is precedent that the field acts on exactly this kind of finding at organism-drug granularity: the temperature-dependent aminoglycoside behaviour of S. maltophilia (priors 3 and the 1996 mechanism paper) sits alongside subsequent CLSI revision of S. maltophilia breakpoints, which is why aminoglycosides are no longer treated as reportable for that organism. The candidate's contribution would be to turn that case-by-case history into a panel-level rate.
If no (all within the reproducibility bound): this is the more valuable outcome and the one the priors do not supply. It would convert an untested assumption of the standard into a positively established property, and would close off temperature as an explanation for the cool-site/standard-site resistance-rate discrepancies that wound and CF microbiology periodically report.
Caveat weakening both branches: because prior 1 found activity increasing monotonically from 35 to 41.5 °C across 17 agents, a "yes" for the febrile arm is close to predictable from existing knowledge, and a reader could reach it from the 1982 abstract. The genuinely informative content is confined to the cool arm and to the class-concentration pattern.

Significance — audience:
(i) EUCAST Steering Committee / CLSI Subcommittee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing — the bodies that write the incubation clause and the reporting notes; they already issue organism-specific reporting suppressions, so the action is within their established repertoire. (ii) Clinical laboratories serving diabetic-foot, burn and CF services, which would gain or lose a reason to annotate reports. (iii) CF and wound-care clinical research groups that have long argued routine AST predicts their patients' outcomes poorly — this names one specific, cheap, measurable candidate mechanism among many. (iv) Veterinary/aquaculture AST standard-setters, who would get a human-clinical reference point for their own temperature-stratified ECVs. Audience (i) is real and identifiable; what is weakened is the claim that it would be *surprised* — the 1984/1985 papers show the committees have had partial evidence for forty years and the standard still says 35 ± 2 °C, which is itself informative about how they would respond.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: whether PK/PD breakpoint derivation should carry a temperature term for cool-site targets; whether the same transportability test should be run on the standard's other fixed conditions (pH, CO2, cation-adjusted broth composition, inoculum density) — i.e. a general programme of "which clauses of the standard are classifier inputs"; whether a measured flip rate predicts anything about outcome in cool-site infection (the clinical question this one deliberately brackets); whether the temperature-sensitive classes share a mechanism (membrane transport and uptake energetics, consistent with aminoglycosides and polymyxin both appearing in prior 3) and whether that predicts the class list a priori; and whether surveillance resistance rates for cool-site specimen types carry a systematic, correctable bias.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches (a) the long-standing in-vitro/in-vivo discordance problem in AST, particularly the poor predictive value of routine AST in CF and chronic-wound infection; (b) the sessile/biofilm-versus-planktonic critique of standard AST, of which this is a thermal analogue; (c) ECV/breakpoint derivation methodology in veterinary and aquaculture AST, where temperature is already an explicit stratifier; (d) the metrological question of cut-off transportability across measurement conditions, which clinical chemistry handles under commutability and AST does not currently frame at all.

Reason for silence:
The candidate mechanism is not silence — Gate 2 shows the question was asked. The better-supported mechanism is *answer attrition inside a standards regime*: the findings exist (1982, 1984, 1985, 1996) but were absorbed as organism-drug-specific caveats rather than as a challenge to the standard's thermal clause, because the standards' purpose is reproducibility between laboratories, and any move toward site-specific incubation temperatures trades inter-laboratory comparability — the thing the standard exists to deliver — for in-vivo fidelity, which the standard does not claim. That is an incentive structure that converts a general question into a series of local exceptions and loses the general form. A secondary mechanism: the 1984 result arrived framed as an MRSA detection trick, so the cool-temperature data entered the literature under "detection sensitivity" rather than "breakpoint transportability", and later searchers in the transportability vocabulary do not find it — the same vocabulary trap that produced this candidate's intake SEARCH NOTE. If a Gate 4 adversary accepts the question as un-asked, that acceptance should itself be treated as suspect.
