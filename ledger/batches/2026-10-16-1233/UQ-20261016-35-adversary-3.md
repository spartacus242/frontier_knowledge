## 1. Restatement in my own words (before adopting any of the record's framing)

Some biobanks now let a participant revoke consent and, when they do, oblige everyone holding the data to erase that participant's records — not merely to stop collecting new data, but to remove what was already contributed and already analysed. The candidate asks three empirical things about that regime. First: are the people who revoke consent different, on the specific variables the cohort exists to study (the exposures, the covariates, the outcomes), from the people who stay? Second: when their records are actually removed, tranche by tranche, how far do the cohort's flagship numbers — a handful of genetic associations, a few hazard ratios, one risk score's discrimination — actually move, judged against simulation noise and against thresholds where a decision would change? Third: does the movement pile up in the direction the first limb predicts, which would make it drift rather than jitter?

The sting in the tail is a claim about who can ever know: because compliance means destroying the earlier version of the dataset, the only party holding both the "before" and "after" pictures is the custodian, at the single moment it is carrying out the erasure. So the measurement is one-shot and, if the withdrawal terms forbid even an anonymous aggregate description of the people leaving, may be impossible for anyone — which the record declares in advance to be an answer rather than a failure.

Two things I note about my own restatement that the record's framing softens. (i) The canonical V0 wording asserts irreversibility ("nobody can measure") rather than testing it; V1 demotes it, but V0 is what I am handed as canonical, so I will attack it there. (ii) "How far do headline estimates move" is, at the rates in play, a question whose rough answer is dictated by arithmetic before any data are touched — which is where I started attacking.

## 3. The eight attacks

### Attack 1 — Triviality: can it be answered in a page from existing knowledge?

**Objection.** Largely yes, for the limb that carries the headline. Removing a fraction *p* of a cohort inflates a standard error by 1/sqrt(1−p): at p = 0.019 that is a factor of 1.0096, i.e. about **a 1.0% widening of the interval** (my own arithmetic, exact given p, no source uncertainty to carry). For a cohort of n ~= 500,000 this is nothing, and the record concedes the point in its own Significance section. The one opened source that quantifies anything in this space does the page already: F1's simulation gives power **80% -> 78% at 5%** removal and **-> 71% at 20%**, and an illustrative null odds ratio of **0.9996 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.02)** — so a reader wanting "how much does a few per cent of removal matter" has a published answer with numbers. And limb (a)'s *sign* is guessable from the attrition literature's standing result that leavers are less educated, poorer and sicker. A page of scaling plus a page of attrition priors gets you: mildly non-random, no material movement, direction as expected.

**Best response.** The page answers the variance question and the *random*-removal question; the candidate's question is the bias question under *non-random* removal, and F1 is explicit that its own simulation does not cover it — the paper's stated reason for not going further is that *"further information on clinical and sociodemographic characteristics of patients opting out is not available."* The 1/sqrt(1−p) scaling bounds dispersion, not displacement: a *p*-fraction removal with a standardised difference δ on the exposure or outcome displaces an estimate by an amount governed by δ and p and **not** damped by sqrt(n), so the large-n reassurance is a non-sequitur for the bias term. The regime where it bites is the one the record already isolates: small effective n. My own arithmetic, with its assumption stated: for a variant at MAF 0.001 in n = 500,000 under Hardy–Weinberg, expected heterozygotes ~= 2n·MAF·(1−MAF) ~= 999; a 1.9% removal takes about 19 of them (binomial SD ~= 4.3) — and whether those 19 are a random 19 is exactly what the deletion makes unanswerable. (The 1.9% HUNT figure is the record's reading of Ursin et al., which I did **not** open; my own opened rate evidence is F1's 5.40%, for a different mechanism.) Finally, limb (a) is not answerable from the attrition page, because F4 shows the two regimes are legally opposite: the literature with a mature position on withdrawal is the trial literature, and it never faced deletion because already-collected data *"may not be removed."*

**Status: Partial.** The headline-estimate limb is close to pre-answerable by arithmetic plus F1's simulation; the covariate limb, the direction test, and the one-shot identifiability claim are not.

### Attack 2 — Disguise: is it a known question renamed? (6 new vocabularies, 12 searches, 5 fetches)

**Objection.** Two renamings come very close, and both were outside the record's five vocabularies.

(i) **Opt-out-from-secondary-use vocabulary.** F1 asks whether a consent-revocation mechanism biases English health-data research, publishes the rate (5.40% as of January 2024 — three times the record's HUNT figure), simulates the impact on power and on an association, reasons explicitly that bias arises if opt-out propensity relates to exposure or outcome, and reaches the candidate's governance conclusion: the characteristics are *"not available"* and the bias cannot be reliably corrected because *"once an individual has opted out, their data is no longer available to researchers."* That is the candidate's limb (a) declared unidentifiable and the candidate's P3 outcome already in print, for the largest health-data system in the UK, two years before this candidate was formulated.

(ii) **Data-version reproducibility vocabulary.** F2 already recomputes an entire headline analysis of a real cohort on an earlier and a later frame, with conventional movement metrics, and **names consent withdrawal in its Introduction** as one of the reasons the frames differ. The machinery the candidate proposes to build exists and has been run.

**Best response.** I opened both and checked all four attributes; neither is the candidate's question renamed.

F1's unit is a weekly aggregate count of NHS numbers, not a participant record; its endpoint is the opt-out proportion, not the movement of an estimate; its comparator is calendar time, not a fixed analysis before and after a tranche; and its only impact figures are hypothetical. Decisively, its **mechanism is different**: opt-out withholds future data flows, it does not delete data already contributed and already analysed — the paper does not discuss retroactive deletion at all. So F1 is the sibling case with the *entry/flow* mechanism, and its "not available" sentence is a statement that it **could not run** limb (a) from the researcher side. The candidate's design is the one under which limb (a) becomes runnable exactly once, custodian-side, which is the thing F1 says is missing rather than the thing F1 supplies.

F2's unit is the CpG site; its comparator is pipeline-2015 versus pipeline-2018, not pre- versus post-tranche; and its handling of the candidate's mechanism is to **eliminate** it — restrict to the 946 overlapping participants, then attribute what remains to preprocessing (*"must therefore be caused by preprocessing differences"*). It reports no withdrawal-attributable movement, no withdrawer profile, no direction test, and does not state per-version counts.

F3 confirms the ML renaming is not it either: unit = model, deletions = simulated with the admission that *"personal details are unavailable in our datasets"*, endpoint = fairness metrics. The question "is non-random deletion harmful" is asked there, but about a classifier's parity, under an assumed deletion distribution.

Searches 5 and 12 independently establish that the worst-case-dropping family has not been pointed at cohort or biobank estimates; the 2024 follow-up (arXiv 2408.09008) is still about approximating the worst case.

**Status: Partial.** Not a renaming — each near neighbour differs in mechanism, unit, comparator, or in how the deleted set is generated, and the two closest ones explicitly remove or declare unavailable the quantity the candidate wants. But I will not grade this Resolved: a reader of F1 can fairly say the *worry*, the *rate*, a *simulated* magnitude and the *irreversibility verdict* are already on record for a sibling mechanism, which leaves the candidate's distinctive remainder narrower than the record claims — the realised measurement on a true retroactive-deletion stream, and limbs (a) and (c).

### Attack 4 — Presupposition failure

**Objection.** Two.

(i) The canonical V0 asserts that after deletion *"nobody can measure"* the drift. In the regime V0 names, that is false as a factual claim. F2 is the proof: its authors held a 2015 frame and a 2018 frame simultaneously and could see which participants were in one and not the other (*"only 948 participants overlapped"*) — the older frame, with the departed individuals still in it, was in researchers' hands. The record's own P6 concedes compliance is unverifiable. So the irreversibility claim is **de jure, not de facto**: it describes what one is *permitted* to interrogate, not what exists.

(ii) The quantity presupposition. The record's P1 rests on a rate I did not verify and which the record itself calls possibly atypical; my own opened rate evidence (F1, 5.40%) is for a different mechanism, and search 8 found that no custodian in that result set publishes a withdrawal count. The limb that needs a number is standing on an unestablished one.

**Best response.** On (i): V1 already demotes the clause to P3/P6 and pre-declares the legally-blocked outcome as an answer, so the operative formulation does not assert what I falsified — and the de jure/de facto gap is itself the finding rather than a defect, since an erosion term measurable only by unlawfully interrogating an archived frame is unmeasurable by every party acting lawfully. F1 is independent confirmation that this is how the situation actually resolves in practice: a published team treated the information as *"not available"* and the bias as not reliably correctable, although somewhere in NHS systems the opt-out flags exist. On (ii): the question is conditional on the realised tranches rather than on an assumed rate — the rate is an *output* of limb (a), not an input — and N3's enforced-withdrawal date (1 April 2026) means the tranches are being generated now. The one presupposition I could falsify is the one V1 had already removed.

**Status: Partial.** The canonical V0 wording does embed a claim I can show is false in the strict sense, and P1's rate is unestablished; neither amounts to a false premise smuggled into V1, so this is not fatal. **Amendment required:** V0 should not be carried as canonical; the irreversibility clause must read "unmeasurable by any party acting within the withdrawal terms", which is what F1 documents.

### Attack 5 — Vagueness: answerable no matter what the evidence shows, or never?

**Objection.** V0's "headline estimates" is undefined, and the decision boundaries in V1 are chosen by the analyst, so "crossed a boundary" can be manufactured by picking a marginal estimate and "within Monte Carlo error" by picking a well-powered one. Worse, the record pre-declares all three outcomes — movement, no movement, and legally blocked — as substantive findings, which is the signature of a question that cannot come back empty.

**Best response.** Multiple informative outcomes is not vagueness; it is a question whose value does not depend on the direction of the answer. Vagueness would mean the outcomes are not distinguishable by measurement, and they are: a standardised difference on a pre-named variable set, movement scaled by Monte Carlo error, and sign agreement between cumulative movement and the contrast in (a). Limb (c) can fail cleanly and kills the claim that carries the significance if it does — if the cumulative shift does not track (a)'s predicted sign, "irreversible drift" is dead and "sampling jitter" is established. The endpoint metrics are not invented for the occasion either: F2 shows the conventional panel in use on a real cohort (FDR-set overlap, top-k rank overlap, Spearman of p-ranks, direction concordance, hypothesis selection). The genuine residue is V0's "headline estimates", which V1 pins to k GWAS associations, k' hazard ratios and one PGS AUC; pre-registration of that panel is the operative safeguard against boundary-shopping.

**Status: Resolved** for V1 (Partial if V0 is graded alone, since "headline estimates" is undefined there).

### Attack 6 — Significance inflation: is the audience real, and would it act on either outcome?

**Objection.** I now have the natural experiment the record lacked. F1 is this exact argument, made to this exact audience, in a peer-reviewed journal in 2024: it reports the opt-out rate rising to 4.97%, warns that bias is possible if opt-out propensity relates to exposure or outcome, and states that the correction is impossible. The reported outcome: **5.40% as of January 2024** and no policy response in the paper's own account. So the strongest available evidence on uptake is that the named audience, given the argument in print, did not act. Audience (1), custodians, is self-indicted and has already answered rhetorically in its own favour — N3 asserts removal *"supports the accuracy and validity of research outputs"* with nothing measured. Audience (4), machine unlearning, is content with simulated deletions by its own statement (*"personal details are unavailable in our datasets"*) and is not waiting for a real stream.

**Best response.** Two things cut the other way, and both are from sources I opened. First, the ask is far smaller than F1's. F1's implied remedy is a change to a national consent policy; the candidate's is "compute one aggregate table and version the release identifier", a step added to a workflow the custodian is already executing at the moment of deletion — and search 11 found that nobody has proposed it, so it is not a remedy already declined. Second, F4 shows a regulator that has already acted on precisely this reasoning in the adjacent regime: data *"may not be removed"* because the study *"must remain ... scientifically valid"*, with the bias potential judged *"immense"*. The argument is therefore demonstrably actionable by regulators; it has simply never been put in the biobank regime, where the ruling went the other way. Third, there is one audience whose live interest I can evidence rather than posit: the data-version reproducibility community, because F2 exists, is published, had the withdrawal channel in its hands, and designed it away — an uptake-ready gap with a named group already working on the failure mode.

**Status: Partial.** The audience is real and one regulator has acted on the identical reasoning elsewhere; but the closest published instance of the argument produced no visible policy movement, which is direct negative evidence the record does not have.

### Attack 7 — Reason for silence

**Objection.** The record's second mechanism is refuted by a source I opened. It claims a disciplinary boundary such that "neither field's search terms reach the other's". F2 is a methods paper about the stability of estimates across data frames that names *"withdraw consent to share their data"* in its **Introduction** and *"withdrawal of consent for certain individuals"* in its **Discussion**. The fields have met. And once they met, the plainer explanation takes over: the people who encountered the phenomenon judged it small, controlled it away by restricting to overlapping samples, and got on with the real question. "Not worth measuring" needs no silence mechanism at all.

**Best response.** F2 is the strongest available evidence for a *sharper* mechanism than the record's, not against the existence of one. A team that held both frames of a real cohort, named the mechanism twice, and then removed it by design is not a team that failed to think of the question; it is a team with no method for a *specific, exogenously selected, governance-generated* deleted subset, whose only available tool bounds the worst case over hypothetical subsets (searches 5 and 12: that family has never been applied to cohort data). That is a method-availability gap at a disciplinary boundary, which the ledger admits, and it is now evidenced in a primary source instead of inferred from a query log. The authority limb survives independently and is also now evidenced: F1 is a competent team wanting the covariate contrast and reporting it *"not available"*, which is what an authority gap looks like from the outside. And the "not worth it" story is contested rather than settled: F4 shows a regulator facing the same magnitude judgement in trials and calling the bias potential *"immense"*.

**Status: Partial.** A legitimate mechanism survives in sharper form than the record states, but the record's specific claim that the two literatures' vocabularies never meet is refuted by F2, and the record must be amended.

### Attack 8 — Dissolution: would it dissolve under analysis rather than be answered?

**Objection.** Split it and it evaporates into three things that are not one question: (i) an arithmetic exercise with a known answer (the variance cost of removing a *p*-fraction, plus F1's simulation); (ii) a legal question about what a custodian may retain; and (iii) a definitional confusion. On (iii): an estimate is only ever defined relative to a frame. The pre-deletion number was the correct answer for the pre-deletion population and the post-deletion number is the correct answer for the post-deletion population. Nothing has drifted and nothing is biased — the estimand changed. "Irreversible drift" is then a category error dressed as a finding.

**Best response.** The estimand-redefinition move fails on the science and has already been rejected by the one regulator that ruled on it. No one intends the target population to be "people who did not later revoke consent"; the target is the source population, and F4 makes exactly that argument and acts on it: already-collected data *"must remain in the trial database in order for the study to be scientifically valid"*, because otherwise *"the bias potential would be immense."* That is the bias framing, chosen over the redefinition framing, by a regulator with the incentive to choose the cheaper one. Nor do limbs (a) and (c) dissolve into arithmetic plus law: whether withdrawers differ on the cohort's own exposures and outcomes is a fact about people, and whether cumulative movement tracks (a)'s predicted sign across real tranches is a fact about a real deletion stream. Neither has an analytic answer. The honest concession is that limb (b) *read in isolation* does substantially dissolve into scaling plus the legal question.

**Status: Resolved** for the three-limb question, with the stated concession about limb (b) alone.

## 4. Closing: the single strongest objection that survives

The candidate survives. Nothing graded Unresolved, and nothing on attacks 1, 2 or 4 came close to it on evidence I could source.

The strongest surviving objection is a **narrowing**, not a kill, and it is the one assembled from attacks 1, 2 and 6 together. **Tazare et al. 2024 (opened) has already published, for a sibling consent mechanism in the largest health-data system in the UK, the rate (5.40% as of January 2024), the reasoning that bias follows if the exclusion propensity relates to exposure or outcome, a simulated magnitude (power 80% -> 78% at 5%), and — verbatim — the candidate's irreversibility verdict: the characteristics of those who opted out are "not available" and the bias cannot be reliably corrected. The governance half of this candidate's payload is therefore already in print, for a neighbouring mechanism, and it moved nothing: opt-outs stand at 5.40%.** What remains genuinely unperformed is the realised measurement itself on a true retroactive-deletion stream — limb (a) on the cohort's own exposure/outcome set, and limb (c)'s direction test — and the record overstates how much of the rest is new. Two further amendments are compelled by sources I opened and the record did not: (1) the record's claim that the robustness literature and the biobank literature never meet in vocabulary is **refuted** — the ALSPAC/ARIES EWAS paper names consent withdrawal in its Introduction and Discussion as a cause of frame change, then designs it away by restricting to the 946 overlapping participants, which is a better-evidenced and sharper silence mechanism (encountered, no method, avoided) than the one on file; and (2) the record's load-bearing unverified residue PMC6287151 is now **resolved** — it is a four-variable table (54 declined versus 809 continued; age, sex, primary education, stroke/severe disability; every p > 0.05, DOI 10.1155/2018/8267948), so a withdrawer-versus-retained comparison does exist in miniature, in a regime where it was only possible because the data were kept. That last point is the candidate's best argument and its sharpest limitation in one object: the table proves the comparison is routine where data persist, and proves it is unavailable where they do not.
