## Gate 0 — Intake

Question (as submitted, V0): In hospitalised adults, is a clinician's test-ordering and monitoring behaviour a calibrated forecast of near-term deterioration — that is, at the margin of ordering does the realised event rate equal the probability implied by the ordering decision under a pre-declared threshold (cost-loss) model — and in which patient strata is it miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Question (canonical, V1 — the one Gate 1 reformulation, used for well-posedness only): In hospitalised adults on general wards, treating each **discretionary** order for an index test declared in advance (e.g. lactate, troponin, blood culture, repeat CBC/chemistry) as a revealed forecast of near-term deterioration (rapid-response activation, unplanned ICU transfer, or death within 24-48 h, ascertained from systems independent of the index order):
(i) estimate the **clinician-specific** ordering-threshold distribution on the deterioration-probability scale from observed decisions, and at the ordering margin compare the realised deterioration rate among the lowest-propensity ordered patients against both (a) that revealed threshold and (b) a pre-declared cost-loss threshold;
(ii) report calibration intercept and slope, with intervals, of the implied forecast against realised deterioration, overall and in strata declared in advance (night vs day, staffing level, admitting service, age, language, race, weekday vs weekend), under an explicitly declared intention-to-forecast estimand plus a treatment-adjustment arm;
(iii) run a falsification arm on protocol/order-set-mandated orders, where no calibration is expected.

Why reformulated: V0 presupposes that one pre-declared cost-loss ratio pins a single implied probability. First-hand evidence opened at Gate 2 (Abaluck et al., below) puts the between-physician SD of the recovered testing threshold at ~0.054 against a mean of ~0.056 — i.e. threshold heterogeneity as large as the threshold — so a single population-level implied probability is not identified. V1 therefore estimates the threshold *distribution* from decisions and keeps the pre-declared cost-loss number as a normative comparator rather than as the definition of the forecast. V1 also makes the forecast-vs-consequence problem explicit (declared estimand + treatment-adjustment arm) instead of leaving it in the presupposition list.

Alternative formulations:
- Treating the decision to order a test as a revealed probability statement rather than as a data-generating nuisance, how well calibrated are clinicians' implicit forecasts of deterioration, and where do they fail?
- If a record's measurement pattern carries more prognostic information than its measured values, what probability is the ordering clinician implicitly asserting, and does the patient's realised outcome match it?
- (method field's words) Can the testing-threshold model be inverted on inpatient order streams to recover a clinician's subjective deterioration risk, and does the marginal ordered patient's realised event rate equal the recovered threshold?

Motivating gap (as submitted, with two corrections established first-hand this run): the anchoring anomaly is that in EHR data the presence and timing of a laboratory test predict survival better than the test's value — Agniel, Kohane & Weber, BMJ 2018;361:k1479, doi 10.1136/bmj.k1479, **opened in full this run** (author attribution now verified; it was recalled and unverified at intake). Corrections to the intake framing, both from the opened text:
1. The anomaly's endpoint is **three-year survival**, not near-term deterioration, and the unit is one randomly selected test observation per patient per test code (669,452 patients, 272 tests; timing beat the result in **118/174 (68%)** of tests in which either dimension improved on the age/sex/race baseline, out of 210 tests with consecutive pairs). So the candidate's presupposition that "the anchoring anomaly replicates in the chosen systems and era" has **never been tested for a 24-48 h deterioration outcome at all** — that limb is not a replication check, it is unexamined ground.
2. The intake's claim that the anomaly "is universally cited as a warning (orders are 'leakage', to be excluded)" is **not** the source paper's position. Agniel et al. write that "The effects of healthcare processes on EHR data should not be viewed as data quality problems or noise" and recommend modelling process explicitly; they do not say order features should be excluded. They do state that the timing "captures the physician's experience, intuition, and assessment of the patient's main complaint" — i.e. the interpretive content the candidate wants is asserted in the anchor paper — but they nowhere frame the ordering decision as a risk estimate or a forecast, and nowhere test it against outcomes. The gap the candidate names survives in a narrower and more defensible form: the anchor says the order stream carries the clinician's judgement, and nobody then measures whether that judgement is right.

## Gate 1 — Well-posedness

What would count as an answer: a pre-registered analysis in >=2 health systems with order-level timestamps and a declared deterioration outcome, reporting (i) the first-stage threshold distribution plus the marginal-patient realised event rate against both the revealed and the pre-declared cost-loss threshold; (ii) calibration intercept and slope with intervals, overall and in the pre-declared strata, under the declared intention-to-forecast estimand with the treatment-adjustment arm; (iii) the protocol-order falsification arm. A clear answer is a signed, interval-estimated miscalibration naming the strata whose intervals exclude calibration. A finding of calibration answers in the opposite direction and is equally informative: it would mean the order stream is the best available prior and that excluding it from deterioration models discards human information rather than removing leakage. If P2 fails even in V1's clinician-specific form, the honest answer is that the quantity is estimable only clinician-by-clinician and not as a population forecast — which would itself close the question.

It is a question, not a thesis: both directions are stated and both are informative, and a third outcome (not estimable) is specified.

Residual well-posedness defects, both load-bearing: (a) P3 — the intention-to-forecast estimand is *declared* rather than identified, and the treatment that follows an order genuinely moves the event rate, so a well-calibrated clinician who then treats effectively reads as over-pessimistic; (b) no source opened supplies a defensible cost-loss ratio for a near-term deterioration endpoint, unlike the PE/ACS settings where treatment benefit can at least be assumed from trial data, so limb (i)(b) rests on a number the field has not agreed. Score 2/3.

Published as the AER 2016 "Determinants of Productivity in Medical Testing" paper (PubMed 29553217) — journal version NOT opened.

## Gate 3 — Significance

Consequence: Three outcomes, all informative, which is the strongest feature of the candidate. (1) **Miscalibrated with a sign** — the direction and the strata whose intervals exclude calibration say where inpatient surveillance is systematically too thin or too heavy. Thin at night or at low staffing is directly actionable (staffing, automated escalation where the human prior is weakest); heavy in a stratum is a stewardship target with an outcome-anchored justification rather than a cost one. (2) **Calibrated** — then the order stream is the best available human prior, and the modelling practice of stripping order features to remove "leakage" discards clinician information rather than removing bias; deterioration models would need an explicit observation-process term with a declared target, which is a change in how a large class of models is built. Note the asymmetry the anchor creates: Agniel et al. already recommend modelling process rather than discarding it, so outcome (2) would *support* an existing recommendation with a measurement rather than overturn a settled practice. (3) **Not estimable** — if P2 fails even clinician-by-clinician, the answer is that "revealed clinician risk" has no population referent in inpatient ordering, which closes the question and also constrains every downstream use of order features as a proxy for clinician concern.

Audience: deterioration-model developers and the clinical-informatics groups that write feature-exclusion rules — the people who operationalise the Agniel anomaly, and the only group whose practice changes either way; rapid-response and deterioration committees, who would learn whether an order-intensity signal belongs in the afferent limb where no score is charted; diagnostic-safety researchers working on afferent-limb and failure-to-rescue; laboratory stewardship programmes, who currently argue from cost and would gain an outcome-anchored margin; and the health-economics group that owns the estimator (N1/N2's authors), for whom an inpatient prognostic endpoint is a new application of machinery they already have. Inflation risk, recorded honestly: the keep-or-drop-order-features decision does not require a calibration intercept to be made, and N2's audience already has the misalignment answer for ED/ACS, so part of this audience can act without the candidate.

Fecundity: whether the ordering stream can serve as a free-standing early-warning signal on wards where no score is charted; whether a deterioration model's discrimination should be decomposed into physiology credit and surveillance-decision credit (this is the question UQ-20261017-39 left standing, and the calibration framing gives it a scale); whether miscalibration strata coincide with documented disparity strata, which would convert an equity claim about testing into a measured forecast error; whether an order-intensity calibration statistic could function as a ward-level quality measure; whether the same revealed-forecast reading transfers to monitoring frequency, imaging and consults; and what happens to a model that silently used a covariate the order stream supplied when ordering practice changes.

Anchoring: the EHR healthcare-process bias anomaly (Agniel 2018, opened); the testing-threshold model and its unsettled empirical status (Pauker & Kassirer 1980 — **recalled only, NOT opened**; Djulbegovic PMID 24903517 — COULD NOT READ); the over/under-testing economics programme (N1, N2); informative missingness and clinical-presence shift in ML-for-health; afferent-limb failure and failure-to-rescue in rapid-response research; the elicited-subjective-probability literature of the 1980s-90s (stated, never revealed); and inside this ledger, UQ-20261017-39's timing limb (whose own Suggested-first-step this candidate partially executes) and UQ-20261015-21's performativity cluster.

## Reason for silence

Candidate mechanism: a **disciplinary boundary that runs between the method and the endpoint, reinforced by data access and by a modelling norm** — not "nobody thought of it".

The machinery that recovers an implied probability from a binary choice and tests it against the realised rate at the margin lives in health economics and runs on claims data. Claims make test positivity and 30-day claims events cheap to ascertain and make near-term inpatient deterioration (RRT activation, unplanned ICU transfer within 24-48 h) essentially unascertainable. So economics took the estimator to the endpoints its data could see: N1's endpoint is PE positivity, N2's is revascularisation and 30-day MACE — both confirmed first-hand, and neither paper uses an inpatient deterioration endpoint anywhere. Conversely, near-term deterioration is only ascertainable inside an operational EHR, and the people with that access are prediction-model builders, whose professional reflex toward order features is exclusion-as-hygiene rather than estimation. One field has the estimator and the wrong endpoint; the other has the endpoint and a norm that treats the signal as contamination. The question sits in the seam.

The mechanism is testable and it half-fails on inspection, which is recorded rather than smoothed over. Its first prediction holds first-hand (neither economics paper touches an inpatient deterioration endpoint; N1 does not estimate a prognostic probability at all, and N2 explicitly declines to estimate the physician's subjective risk). Its second prediction — that the informatics side treats order features as leakage to be excluded — is **not** supported by the anchor paper, which says the opposite ("should not be viewed as data quality problems or noise") and recommends explicit modelling. So the norm half of the mechanism rests on the downstream practice of modellers rather than on the anchor, and **no source documenting that practice was opened this run**. A second, weaker mechanism that does survive: the elicitation tradition in medical decision making spent two decades asking clinicians to *state* probabilities (Med Decis Making 1986, 1995; N3's worry factor is its descendant) and never crossed to inferring them from behaviour, because stated probabilities are what a questionnaire can collect and revealed ones need linked order-level and outcome data that did not exist when that tradition was active. That is a new-data mechanism, and it is the stronger of the two.
