## 1. Restatement in my own words

Take every deployed GNSS-disciplined clock or timing receiver whose operator keeps a per-unit log (time error, phase offset, holdover state or alarm) at least four times an hour for at least a month, where the log says what model (or what holdover-oscillator class) the unit is, and where the unit feeds an application whose standard (one of the ITU-T G.827x family, the IEEE/IEC synchrophasor standards, NIST SP 1500-08, or the substation PTP profiles) states a numeric time-error limit. Now take the periods when GNSS was being jammed, spoofed or was itself broken, dated ONLY from evidence that does not come from those clocks (regulator bulletins, NOTAMs, ADS-B-derived interference maps, NAGUs). The question asks: during each such period, how large was the worst time error each unit actually handed to its application, as a distribution per model; and what fraction of units went past their standard's limit, with a binomial interval and a count of units whose logs were missing.

So the question needs, before anything is measured: (i) that such logs exist with model keys for several models and several operators; (ii) that disturbance periods can be dated from outside at the place where a unit sits; (iii) that each named standard states a limit in time units; and (iv) — the clause nobody in the record wrote down — that what a unit LOGS during a GNSS disturbance is the time error it DELIVERED, i.e. that the unit's own record is a measurement of the estimand rather than something else.

C4. A unit's own time-error / phase-offset record during a GNSS disturbance epoch is a MEASUREMENT of the time error the unit DELIVERED to its application. — CONTESTED on a source I opened (S3, full text), and the question cannot survive without it, because its primary estimand is "maximum |time error| delivered to the dependent application" and the ONLY instrument the question declares is the unit's own record. S3 states that what a GNSS clock knows about its own error after loss of lock is "a statistical estimate", that "holdover error can't be predicted exactly", that the figure such a clock places in its time codes and protocols is its "estimated actual holdover error", and that "Customers have no practical way to verify actual performance". Under jamming — the modal epoch class the candidate's exposure instrument (ADS-B interference maps, regulator bulletins) is built for — a GNSS-only clock loses lock and is in exactly S3's regime; its logged time error during the epoch is therefore an estimate produced by the unit, not a measurement of delivered error, unless an INDEPENDENT reference is present at the unit. S3's regime is one vendor's substation clocks, so I grade CONTESTED rather than FALSE: units that carry an independent reference (ePRTC with caesium, a PTP/SyncE secondary input, or an operator monitoring probe) can measure delivered TE, and the record's P1 shows SSU2000s producing MTIE readings of their GPS input against their internal references. But note what P1's number is, on the record's own quotations: "The MTIE values were showing an error of 13 microseconds across all the affected SSU2000s", after which "downstream switches ... reject the timing inputs" and "Roughly 40 elements entered holdover". That 13 us is the error of the GPS INPUT as seen by the SSU, and the SSU's protective action was to reject that input and hold over — so the time error DELIVERED by those elements during the epoch is not that figure and is not printed. The record calls P1's figure a "delivered-error figure"; on the record's own quotations it is an input-error figure. (This reading of P1 is MY OWN INFERENCE from the record's quotations; I did not open P1.) So even the record's best prior does not show the delivered quantity being observed by the unit's own record; it shows the protective mechanism hiding it.
C5.
