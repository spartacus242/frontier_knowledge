## 1. Restatement in my own words

Take the published animal-respirometry literature and restrict it, by a frame fixed before any data is pulled, to studies that print both an oxygen-consumption and a carbon-dioxide-production figure (or an RQ/RER computed from them) for the same animals or the same declared group. Aerobic substrate chemistry pins the ratio of those two printed numbers into a narrow band, so a pair outside the band is a paper contradicting its own arithmetic. The question then asks three things. (i) How often does that happen — the share of printed value-pairs outside a window fixed in advance at 0.70-1.00, with a confidence interval, broken out by taxon, respirometry mode and decade, against a denominator (how many studies report both gases at all) that nobody has counted, plus pre-declared arms at the clinical 0.67-1.30 window and for taxa/states where an extreme ratio is real physiology. (ii) Mode by mode, what a failed ratio can actually fingerprint: derived from each mode's own computation, which error terms the ratio is sensitive to and which it is blind to, and only after that, whether a deviation puts any bound on the reported mass-specific rate. (iii) If the self-contradicting records are dropped or corrected under rules fixed in advance, does the fitted interspecific mass-scaling exponent move outside its published confidence interval.

What the question is NOT: it is not "is this one RQ plausible" (that is a written clinical criterion), and it is not "does data selection move the scaling exponent" (that is a published result). It is the retrospective, corpus-level, strata-resolved version with an identifiability derivation in the middle.
