## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Two different instruments now exist for the same number — "how many chromosomes does one
haploid set of species X have". The old instrument is a human at a microscope counting
condensed chromosomes, whose results sit in 20th-century compilations (CCDB for plants, the
coleoguy/karyotypes files for animals). The new instrument is a genome assembly pipeline that
emits some number of chromosome-scale scaffolds. The candidate asks two things. (i) For the
species that carry both, how often do the two numbers differ, which way does the difference
point, and is the pattern clade-structured? (ii) Chromosome-number evolution models (ChromEvol
and descendants) swallow the old instrument's numbers as if exact; if you re-fit one such model
on a fixed tree after swapping in the assembly-derived numbers, how far do the fitted
polyploidization / demi-polyploidization / fission / fusion rates move — compared with how far
they move if you merely jitter the cytogenetic counts at the error rate and skew that limb (i)
measured? Said plainly: is the input data of chromosome-evolution modelling error-free, and if
not, does the error matter to the published rates?

  It "never compares chromosome number or karyotype between methods". I do not carry the 12%

  paper's average over a handful of species-populations, not a rate for the candidate's frame.

  210 chromosome-complete lepidopteran genomes, 32 ancestral Merian elements intact ~250 My,

  abstract reports **no** assembly-vs-cytogenetic comparison and **no** agreement percentage,
  confirming the record's reading of the preprint rather than contradicting it.
