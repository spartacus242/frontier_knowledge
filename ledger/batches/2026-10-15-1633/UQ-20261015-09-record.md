Question (canonical):
  [v0, intake] Using Lean blueprint projects, which link each informal lemma and proof to its formal counterpart, does the per-lemma de Bruijn factor (compressed formal size over compressed informal size) fall as the mathlib library available at formalisation time grows, after controlling for mathematical field and the lemma's dependency depth?
  [R1, Gate 1 reformulation, adopted] In human-written Lean blueprint projects (blueprint text written by people, not generated from Lean code or by AI; roughly 2020-2025), is a node's intrinsic de Bruijn factor (compressed size of the Lean declarations attributed to the node, including project-local helpers used only by it, divided by compressed size of its blueprint statement and proof) lower when a larger share of its formal prerequisites were already in mathlib at the commit where the node was first formalised, holding fixed project, field, dependency depth and toolchain era (Lean 3 or Lean 4)? Secondary and descriptive only: does any association with total mathlib size remain once coverage is included?

Alternative formulations: (a) Has formalising mathematics in Lean become measurably cheaper per unit of informal proof as mathlib grew from 2017 to 2026, and does the decline differ by field? (b) Do paired informal and formal proofs bear out the folk claim that large libraries shrink formalisation overhead, or does the overhead stay roughly constant, as de Bruijn's "constant loss factor" and Wiedijk's small-sample estimates suggest?

Motivating gap: Wiedijk's de Bruijn factor (cs.ru.nl/~freek/factor) rests on about 9 hand-picked passages (Automath, Mizar, HOL; factors 0.44-8.8, typically about 4), with no library dimension. De Bruijn's own claim, which Wiedijk quotes, is that the loss factor is "constant: it does not increase if we go further in the book". In the January 2018 FOM thread, Blanqui gives 4-5 for the odd-order theorem and asserts that new formalisations "should take less time" now that libraries exist. Kontorovich (arXiv:2510.15924, 2025, sec. 9.2-9.3) cites 4-10 and predicts that a time-based "formalization factor" will fall below 1, a drop that "depends primarily on ... comprehensive libraries". Both are assertions without data. Paulson's ALEXANDRIA lessons paper (arXiv:2305.14407) reports no ratios and says "effort remains the main obstacle". Mathlib growth and structure studies (arXiv:2605.23983, file and commit counts; arXiv:2604.24797, "The Network Structure of Mathlib"; arXiv:2508.21593, "Growing Mathlib") do not measure overhead. Software verification has size and effort regressions (Staples et al. 2014; Matichuk et al. 2015) but not for mathematics libraries. The new instrument is the blueprint: a node-by-node pairing of informal LaTeX with Lean declarations, with git-dated mathlib pins.

Gate 1 — reason for reformulation (one allowed; both versions recorded above): in v0 the regressor "mathlib size at formalisation time" is almost collinear with calendar time, and so with the Lean 3 to 4 port (2023), better tactics, author experience and AI-assisted proving since 2025. Field and depth controls cannot separate these, so a slope in v0 could not be attributed to the library. R1 makes per-node library coverage the main regressor. Coverage varies across nodes within a project and year, so project fixed effects absorb author and era effects. R1 also restricts the corpus to human-written blueprint text, because LeanArchitect (Zhu, Monticone, Avigad, Welleck; ITP 2026, LIPIcs.ITP.2026.25 / arXiv:2601.22554) generates blueprint text from Lean code, which makes the informal denominator non-independent in projects that adopt it. R1 is a question, not a thesis: both signs and a null are informative.

Presuppositions:
  P1. Blueprint informal text is a fair proxy for informal proof size. CONTESTED: blueprints are written to guide formalisation and are more detailed than papers. Their detail may itself depend on coverage, because authors expand steps where mathlib is thin, which moves the denominator with the regressor. Mitigation: a second denominator from source-paper passages, wherever a node maps to one.
  P2. The compressed-size ratio is a meaningful proxy for formalisation overhead. CONTESTED: Staples et al. 2014 (ESEM) find effort linear in proof size for software verification, but Kontorovich 2025 writes that "lines of code are no longer a meaningful proxy for human effort". R1 asks about the size ratio itself; effort is part of the significance claim only.
  P3. The library state at formalisation time can be dated from lake-manifest mathlib pins and git history. ESTABLISHED, but there is a reverse-causality trap: projects upstream their own helpers to mathlib, so coverage must be measured at the node's first-formalised commit, not at the latest pin.
  P4. Field labels can be assigned (MSC codes of the source paper, or mathlib directories). LARGELY ESTABLISHED.
  P5. Attribution of formal code to nodes: declarations named by \lean{...}, plus helpers reachable only from that node. ANALYST CHOICE; shared helpers need a split rule, flagged.
  P6. Confounds are separable: the Lean 3 to 4 port, tactic and automation improvements, author experience, and AI assistance (Aristotle, Claude and others since 2025). CONTESTED: handled through project fixed effects, era strata and exclusion of AI-assisted commits. Residual confounding remains for the secondary library-size term.
  P7. There is enough data. UNVERIFIED: RLMEval used 613 theorems from 6 blueprint projects. LeanArchitect reports converting "several large" blueprint projects. No census of blueprint projects was found (Gate 2, query 28). From background knowledge (not verified this run), blueprint use began with sphere eversion and the Liquid Tensor Experiment (about 2020-21) and spread from 2023.
  P8. Projects are not selected on expected overhead. CONTESTED: projects are chosen near the mathlib frontier and by enthusiasts. Within-project analysis limits but does not remove this.

What would count as an answer: build a node-level dataset from human-written blueprint projects (pre-AI era, or AI-assisted commits excluded). Each node carries its informal statement and proof LaTeX, its linked Lean declarations with project-local helpers attributed under a fixed rule, the first commit at which it became \leanok together with the mathlib pin at that commit, coverage (the share of its transitive formal prerequisites already in that mathlib), dependency depth in the blueprint DAG, field, and era. Compute log(compressed formal / compressed informal) with fixed compressors (gzip and xz, as robustness). Fit a mixed or fixed-effects regression on coverage with project fixed effects plus depth, field and era.
  YES: the coverage coefficient is negative, its 95% CI excludes 0, and the result survives (i) the source-paper denominator on mappable nodes, (ii) both compressors, (iii) the Lean 3 and Lean 4 strata.
  NO: the CI lies inside a pre-registered equivalence band (for example, under 10% change in the factor per 25-point change in coverage). This would be consistent with de Bruijn's constancy claim at the lemma level.
  INCONCLUSIVE: anything else, reported as such.
  The secondary library-size term is reported descriptively, with its confounding stated.

Significance — consequence:
  YES (overhead falls with coverage): this is the first quantitative evidence for the library-first argument (Kontorovich's "depends primarily on comprehensive libraries"). It gives a coverage elasticity that project leads can use to estimate cost from a dependency audit before starting (FLT- or Carleson-scale planning). Field-specific slopes show where library investment pays most.
  NO (flat within the equivalence band): de Bruijn's constancy holds at the lemma level even with a mature library. Per-step overhead then comes from the informal-to-formal granularity gap, not from missing background. This argues for investing in automation, tactics or AI rather than library breadth to cut per-lemma cost, and it weakens a premise behind library-building funding pitches.
  Either way, the result fixes a number that the community currently quotes as folklore ("4", "4-10", "5x", "a week per page").

Significance — audience: mathlib maintainers and the Lean FRO (prioritising library areas), blueprint project leads (cost planning), funders of library-building and AI-for-maths programmes (whether library breadth is the bottleneck), AI-for-maths benchmark builders (MathAtlas and RLMEval already use dependency depth as a difficulty axis; coverage would be a second axis), and proof-engineering cost-estimation researchers (the Staples/Matichuk line, whose size laws have never been tested on mathematics libraries). The audience is real but small, and the size-versus-effort dispute (P2) limits how far practitioners would act on a size-only result.

Significance — fecundity: (i) The marginal value of a mathlib declaration: how much downstream formal size its presence saves, which would let library PRs be prioritised. (ii) Whether there is a field-specific floor, an irreducible factor at full coverage. (iii) Whether AI-assisted formalisation since 2025 breaks the size-coverage relation (pre- and post-2025 comparison). (iv) Whether coverage predicts autoformalisation success better than dependency depth. (v) Whether spillovers through upstreaming (one project's helpers lowering later projects' factors) are measurable, which would treat library reuse like citation flows. (vi) A direct test of the Matichuk statement-proof size law on mathematics.

Significance — anchoring: de Bruijn's constant-loss-factor claim (quoted by Wiedijk) against Blanqui's and Kontorovich's predicted decline (directly contradictory folk positions, neither tested). Rudnicki's 2000 observation that about 29% of a Mizar formalisation was facts the source used without proof, which is the component library coverage should remove. Wiedijk's own note that people pick texts that suit their system (selection bias in the "about 4" figure). Paulson 2023: "effort remains the main obstacle". The Matichuk 2015 size law.

Significance score: 2/3. The consequence is real in both directions, a concrete decision exists (library-area prioritisation, project cost estimates) and the anchoring is concrete (two contradictory folk positions). It is not 3/3 because the effort proxy is disputed by the closest neighbour, the community is small, and AI-assisted formalisation may make a pre-2025 human-era answer partly historical.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism): (1) A new instrument. Node-level pairing of informal and formal text with dated library pins exists only since blueprints (spreading from about 2023) and mathlib's git history (2017). Earlier pairings were whole paper against whole development, so per-node variation in coverage could not be observed and only one factor per project could be computed, which is exactly what the literature reports (Paulson 5.6; ordinal partitions 20+; Wiedijk's 9 passages). (2) A disciplinary boundary. The group that runs size and effort regressions (the NICTA/Data61 proof-engineering measurement work) studies software verification, while mathematics formalisers report the factor as a per-project difficulty badge. (3) Attention and incentives. Since 2024 the field's attention has moved to AI autoformalisation and time-based effort, and the closest neighbour explicitly declares size proxies obsolete, so a size-based test looks dated to insiders. Meanwhile the folk claim is believed rather than doubted. (4) A closing window. Since 2025, AI-written proofs and Lean-generated blueprint text (LeanArchitect, ITP 2026) contaminate new data, so the clean human-era corpus is fixed and finite. Weakest link: (3) is partly inferred, not evidenced.
