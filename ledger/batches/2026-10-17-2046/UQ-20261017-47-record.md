## Question (canonical)

**V1 (as received at intake).** Under a declared accounting boundary, what is the distribution across sufficiently-disclosed frontier and open models of the ratio of non-gradient-step production compute — RL rollout and verifier inference, synthetic-training-data generation, teacher inference for distillation, and discarded or failed runs — to gradient-step compute, and does ranking models by total production compute reorder them relative to ranking by their reported "training compute"?

**V2 (Gate 1 reformulation — the one permitted, for well-posedness only; scored as canonical).** For a frame F of models declared before any data pull (open-recipe releases with published RL/distillation configurations and hardware-hours, plus closed models admitted only as interval bounds), and for three accounting boundaries declared in advance — (B1) the European Commission's July 2025 GPAI boundary (¶119–122 of C(2025) 5045: parameter-update compute plus non-public synthetic-data forward passes including discarded data; excluding distillation-parent training, failed synthetic-data experiments, auxiliary reward/value models and diagnostics), (B2) that boundary widened to include RL rollout and verifier inference, distillation teacher inference and abandoned runs, and (B3) B2 with GPU-hours or board energy rather than FLOP as the denominator — what is the distribution of R = (rollout + verifier + synthetic-data + teacher + discarded)/(gradient-step), what is Spearman ρ between reported "training compute" and total production compute under each boundary, and how many models in F change threshold status at 10^25 FLOP (EU AI Act Art. 51(2)) between boundaries?

Reformulation rationale: V1 says "a declared accounting boundary" in the singular while its answer condition requires 2–3; V1 also leaves the denominator unit undeclared although its own presupposition list concedes FLOPs may not be the summable unit. V2 fixes both and names the frame-restriction the contested presupposition demands. V1 is retained in the record.

## Alternative formulations

- How much of the compute that made a model was spent on inference rather than on updating weights, and does counting it change which models are the "biggest"?
- Is "training compute" an additive quantity for models produced by RL and distillation pipelines, or does its value depend on an accounting boundary that nobody has declared?

## Motivating gap (as received, with Gate 2 corrections marked)

The settled claim being inverted is that a model is summarised by a single "training compute" figure, additive over its production — load-bearing in two places at once: scaling-law comparisons index models by compute, and compute governance makes a FLOP figure a legal trigger (EU AI Act systemic-risk presumption at 10^25 FLOP; the 10^26 figure in US policy instruments). The 2025–2026 production pipeline spends very large amounts of compute that are not gradient steps: RL rollouts and verifiers, synthetic-data generation, distillation teachers, abandoned runs.

**CORRECTED AT GATE 2 — two load-bearing claims in the intake gap are false.**

1. *"an accounting boundary that nobody has declared"* — **false.** The European Commission's GPAI scope guidelines of 18 July 2025 (C(2025) 5045 final) declare one, in detail, and declare **two different ones** for the two thresholds: ¶118 uses "the total amount of compute directly contributing to parameter updates in the model" for the indicative 10^23 FLOP GPAI criterion, and the Recital-111 *cumulative* definition for the 10^25 systemic-risk presumption. ¶119 states the general rule ("account for all compute that contributed or will contribute to the model's capabilities"); ¶120 **includes** forward passes generating non-public synthetic data "including discarded data"; ¶122 **excludes** compute to train distillation parent models, failed synthetic-data experiments whose results are discarded, auxiliary models such as value functions and reward models, diagnostics (evals, red-teaming), and activation recomputation; ¶121 includes merge compute; ¶123 requires accuracy within 30% with documented assumptions. The UNRUN check the intake flagged as "the single most likely pre-emption" was run first, and it fired.
2. *"The literature that touches this treats it as an ALLOCATION problem, not an ACCOUNTING one"* — **false.** Epoch AI's "Three challenges facing compute-based AI policies" treats it explicitly as an accounting problem, names the divergence between instruments (the Biden executive order summed pre- and post-training compute; the EU AI Act counts synthetic-data generation; Epoch's own tracker excludes "ancillary compute uses like synthetic data generation"), states that "current policies typically only count the compute from the student run, ignoring the upstream teacher compute" and that reward-model compute "remains entirely unaccounted for", and supplies spot ratios.

What survives of the gap: the *systematic* quantification — a declared frame, per-model estimates with uncertainty for all five components, the distribution of R, the rank correlation, and the threshold recount under competing boundaries — was not found published. Failed/abandoned runs are the one component no declared boundary counts and the Commission does not address at all (¶122 covers only failed *synthetic-data* experiments, not failed training runs).

## Presuppositions

1. Enough models disclose a decomposable compute figure, or enough is inferable from hardware-hours and published RL configurations, to estimate R over a non-trivial frame. **CONTESTED, and Gate 2 narrows it:** per-model decomposition has been demonstrated for one model at a time (DeepSeek-R1) from published RL hyperparameters with a stated ~2× sensitivity to the batch-size assumption alone; for closed models the published figures are explicitly labelled "best guesses, not public data". The frame is plausibly open-recipe models plus interval bounds — i.e. a bound, not a distribution, for most of the population.
2. Gradient-step and non-gradient-step production compute are separable under a stated boundary. **CONTESTED and partly the point** — a teacher's own pretraining is upstream; ¶122 resolves this by fiat (exclude), which shows the boundary is declarable, not discoverable.
3. Reported "training compute" figures are intended as totals rather than pretraining-only. **CONTESTED, and now evidenced on both sides:** Epoch's documentation says "training compute is provided as the total training compute, including pretraining, and including pretrained base models used as components", records finetuning in a separate column, and is silent on RL, rollouts, reward/verifier inference, synthetic data and discarded runs; the Commission's ¶118 uses a narrower parameter-update-only figure for one threshold and a cumulative figure for the other. So conventions genuinely differ — this presupposition holds.
4. A named regulatory threshold is in fact applied to a figure of this kind. **ESTABLISHED** (AI Act Art. 51(2) presumption, ¶28/¶118; the one-third-of-original rule at ¶60–64 makes the denominator legally operative too).
5. Discarded and failed runs belong to "what made the model" under at least one defensible boundary. **CONTESTED** — no declared boundary counts them; ¶122 excludes failed synthetic-data experiments and "compute whose expenditure contributes to enhancing model capabilities only through lessons learnt by humans". Report with and without.
6. Inference and training FLOPs are commensurable enough to be summed. **CONTESTED** — the Commission answers by fiat at ¶125 ("all operations should be counted equally, independently of floating-point precision"), which is a declaration, not a justification; Epoch's R&D piece notes R&D and RL workloads run at lower utilization, so the same FLOP costs more hardware time. V2's B3 boundary exists because of this.
7. (Implicit, added at Gate 1) The ranking question is well defined — i.e. there exists a published ranking of models by reported training compute to be reordered. **ESTABLISHED** (Epoch's frontier-models dataset and its "over 30 models above 1e25 FLOP" count).

## What would count as an answer

A frame declared before any data pull; per-model estimates with stated uncertainty of (a) gradient-step FLOPs, (b) rollout and verifier inference FLOPs, (c) synthetic-data-generation FLOPs, (d) teacher-inference FLOPs, (e) discarded-run FLOPs, each under 2–3 explicitly declared boundaries; the distribution of (b+c+d+e)/(a); Spearman ρ between reported "training compute" and total production compute; and the count of models that cross or cease to cross 10^25 FLOP under each boundary. The "not well defined" limb is answered quantitatively — by showing R or a model's threshold status changes by more than a declared margin across defensible boundaries (the natural margin is the Commission's own 30% at ¶123: a boundary choice that moves a figure by more than 30% moves it by more than the law's tolerated estimation error). A uniformly small and boundary-insensitive R would answer it the other way and positively license current practice.

## Significance — consequence

If R is large and boundary-sensitive: the 10^25 trigger is a function of a drafting choice as much as of a model, and the Commission's ¶123 30% error margin is not the binding uncertainty — the boundary is. Concretely, P1's own figures already imply this is live: if pretraining was 90–99% of total at AI Act drafting and RL:pretraining is now ≈0.2–0.4 for one open reasoning model with synthetic data adding ≈0.25 for another, then a model estimated at 7e24 FLOP under B1 can cross 10^25 under B2 without a single additional gradient step. Second, scaling-law and capability-per-compute comparisons that pool models across 2020–2026 are regressing on an x-axis whose boundary silently moved, which biases any fitted trend in the direction of understating recent compute. If R is small and boundary-insensitive, current practice is licensed and the governance debate can stop re-litigating the definition — a real and useful negative.

## Significance — audience

(i) The EU AI Office, which owns ¶119–122 and has already had to legislate the boundary once and would revise it on a sensitivity analysis showing its exclusions exceed its own 30% tolerance; the exclusions most exposed are reward/value models (now verifiers in RLVR pipelines) and failed training runs, which no instrument addresses. (ii) Third-party trackers — Epoch explicitly, whose documentation claims "total" while its practice excludes ancillary uses, and whose ≥1e25 count (33 models as of July 2025) is the number policy discussion actually cites. (iii) Scaling-law methodologists who index by reported compute. (iv) Providers preparing systemic-risk notifications, who must document assumptions under ¶123 and currently have no published reference distribution to benchmark their own decomposition against. (v) Standards bodies for model cards. These audiences are real and already arguing about this, which is also precisely why novelty is Low.

## Significance — fecundity

Opens: what denominator is defensible for a legal trigger (FLOP vs GPU-hours vs board energy) once arithmetic intensity differs by an order of magnitude between rollouts and gradient steps; whether a boundary-stable capability-per-compute metric exists; whether thresholds should be defined on auditable hardware-hours, which a cloud provider can attest, rather than on FLOP, which only the provider can compute; how to price a distillation chain when the teacher's pretraining is excluded by fiat; whether "discarded run" is even auditable after the fact.

## Significance — anchoring

The Biden-EO-vs-AI-Act boundary divergence (P1); the Commission's own split definitions at ¶118; the compute-threshold critique literature (Hooker; Heim & Koessler; arXiv 2502.00003 — none opened here); the distillation-scaling-law practice of reporting under teacher-compute-included and -excluded accounting (arXiv 2502.08606, not opened); the LCA boundary-setting tradition (arXiv 2607.05408 and the Green-AI reviews), which owns the method and has never been applied with FLOP as the functional unit.

## Reason for silence

Partial silence only, and the mechanism is an **incentive-and-purpose split between the two communities that do the counting**, not absence of thought. A tracker optimizing for comparability across a six-year span wants the narrowest boundary that stays estimable from public disclosures, so it excludes ancillary compute by construction and says so. A regulator optimizing for enforceability wants the boundary a provider can document and audit within 30%, so it excludes whatever a provider could not evidence — parent training, failed experiments, auxiliary models. Neither party gains from publishing the cross-boundary sensitivity: for the tracker it would undermine its own time series, and for the Commission it would quantify the arbitrariness of a trigger it has just defended. Added to this, a data mechanism: per-model rollout decomposition only became inferable with the 2025 open-recipe RL releases that publish step count, group size, batch size and sequence length, and remains a best guess for closed models. Both mechanisms are admissible under the ledger's list (incentive gap; new data). But because P1 shows the question *was* asked in 2025, the silence to be explained is the narrower one — why no one has run the systematic version — and that is better explained by cost and by the fact that the answer embarrasses whoever commissions it than by any conceptual gap.
