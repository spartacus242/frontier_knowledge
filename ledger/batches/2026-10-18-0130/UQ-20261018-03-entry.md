(V2; V1 — which referenced "that operator's own best performance" and left registry, outcome and margin unnamed — is in the record, one Gate 1 reformulation used.)
Alternative formulations:
- Do proceduralists lose measurable skill between cases, and how fast — is there an empirical half-life of procedural competence visible in patient outcomes?
- Is "currency" (recency of practice), the quantity aviation licenses on, an identifiable determinant of patient outcome in procedural medicine, separable from case volume?
Motivating gap: Aviation licenses on recency (landings in 90 days, rolling instrument approaches) regardless of lifetime hours; medicine privileges on cumulative/annual volume and time-limited certification. Submitted claim that medicine "contains no recency term anywhere" is FALSE as stated — Ahpra/Medical Board of Australia operate a generic *Recency of practice* registration standard (under review 2025-12-05). Surviving gap: existing recency instruments are generic, hours-based, multi-year, not procedure-specific, and not calibrated to any outcome curve.
Presuppositions:
1. A registry holds operator IDs with dated per-operator case histories — ESTABLISHED and demonstrated (PHC4 Pennsylvania, surgeon licence number + days-since-last-procedure, P1).

Operator FE + unrelated-procedure negative control identifies a procedure-specific recency effect — CONTESTED and evidenced against: in P1 the pre-declared placebo (recency of any inpatient procedure) is non-null and LARGER than the treatment (+0.067 vs +0.032 pp/day in-hospital mortality), so the design cannot interpret its own falsification test.
3. Outcome measured on a timescale over which recency could act — CONTESTED for long-horizon endpoints; fine for 1-day/in-hospital/30-day mortality.

5. Skill is the mechanism — explicitly NOT presupposed.
6. A credentialing body could act on a recency parameter — ASSUMPTION, weakly contradicted by 11 years of non-response to P1 and 9 to Van Gestel 2017.
What would count as an answer: the V2 estimation, pre-registered, giving (i) the recency coefficient and CI conditional on volume, (ii) a negative control and falsification test whose expected-null status is defensible given P1's non-null placebo, (iii) inversion of the fitted curve into the gap length at the pre-stated margin with CI, or a demonstration of no such gap within support. A CI excluding a material effect answers negatively and is usable: it tells privileging committees not to write recency rules.

P1. Hockenberry & Helmchen, "The nature of surgeon human capital depreciation," J Health Econ 2014;37:70-80, doi:10.1016/j.jhealeco.2014.06.001 — read in full as NBER WP 20017. Unit: CABG discharge (56,315 discharges, 188 surgeons, PHC4 Pennsylvania 2006Q3-2010Q4), exposure at operating surgeon. Exposure: days since surgeon's last CABG and days since last inpatient procedure of any type. Endpoint: 1-day and in-hospital mortality, log cost. Comparator: within-surgeon variation net of surgeon, hospital and year FE, trailing-365-day surgeon and hospital volume, patient covariates. Estimand: within-surgeon conditional association per additional day away (LPM, SEs clustered by surgeon; scheduling flagged endogenous). Result: in-hospital mortality +0.067 pp/day (SE 0.025) any-procedure, +0.032 pp/day (SE 0.015) CABG; emergent patients of high-volume surgeons +0.398 pp (SE 0.101), 11.4% relative.
P2. Huesch, "The impact of short breaks from cardiac surgery on mortality and stay length in California," J Healthc Qual 2014;36(5):42-49, doi:10.1111/jhq.12018 — read via pure.psu.edu record. Unit: isolated-CABG patient (56,956, California 2003-2005). Exposure: binary no-CABG in the preceding calendar month. Endpoint: LOS, in-hospital and 30-day mortality. Comparator: patients of surgeons who did operate. Estimand: adjusted mean difference, long-run monthly volume adjusted, FE not stated. Result: +0.5 day LOS (p=.037, not robust); no mortality difference.
