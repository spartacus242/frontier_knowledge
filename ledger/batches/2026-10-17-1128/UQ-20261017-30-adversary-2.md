## 1. Restatement in my own words

Take the published LLM/agent evaluations that ship per-item logs with an error field. Before
looking at the data, declare the frame and a failure taxonomy. Then ask five things.

(a) Of the items that the headline number was divided by, how many actually broke on the
scaffolding's side — the container died, the API 500'd, the call timed out, the grader crashed —
rather than the model getting the answer wrong? Broken out by class, with the classifier checked
against hand labels and the share you could not classify stated.

(b) For one fixed target quantity, how far apart are two ways of computing it: throw the broken
item out of the denominator (per-protocol), versus leave it in and call it a miss (intention-to-
treat)? Reported as score difference, rank correlation, and a count of model pairs that swap order.

(c) Separately — and explicitly not as a third way of computing the same thing — what do you get
if you keep retrying until the item completes, and how far is that from each of the other two?

(d) Is the chance of a scaffolding failure tied to which model is being run? That is what makes
throwing items out biased rather than just wasteful.

(e) How many published evaluations say which of these they did?

The claim under test is that nobody has asked this.

## 3. What I found that the record did not have

**(i) A peer-reviewed paper that poses the candidate's limb-(b) choice, decides it, and gives the
limb-(d) bias argument — in AutoML vocabulary.** AMLB (Gijsbers et al., *JMLR* 25:101, 2024),
§5.1.3, read via the JMLR PDF:

- the convention, stated: "Therefore, we impute missing values with the constant predictor, or prior."
- why not drop the item: ignoring missing values would mean "AutoML frameworks may fail on harder
  tasks or folds and consequently obtain higher average performance estimates."
- the bias ground: "Failures correlate with data set properties, such as data set size and class
  imbalance" — which the authors argue makes exclusion biased.
- a third convention they also reject: imputing with a random forest would "provide an unfair
  advantage to the AutoML frameworks that are not well-behaved."
- the choice defended as a fairness-between-systems decision: the constant predictor is "a very
  penalizing imputation strategy" and "we feel this penalization for ill-behaved systems is
  appropriate and fairer towards the well-behaved frameworks."
- the failure classes named only loosely: "There are situations where search time budgets are
  exceeded or the AutoML frameworks crash outright"; frameworks "crash irrecoverably, for example,
  due to segmentation faults."

Scope check, because this is the claim my kill rests on: AMLB's unit is framework × dataset × fold,
not LLM-eval item; its failures are framework crashes and time-budget overruns, not API/container
errors; and it reports **no** sensitivity analysis and **no** quantification of how the choice moves
the ranking ("we first impute any missing values with the constant predictor and then average the
performance over all folds"). Its correlate is with *dataset* properties, not with *system identity*
— system-dependence is presupposed in the "well-behaved / ill-behaved frameworks" framing, not
measured. So AMLB does not answer limb (b) numerically and does not answer limb (d) at all. It does
ask the candidate's question, in print, in a top ML journal, and settle it by argument.

**(ii) Limb (e)'s nearest hit, read, with a mixed result that partly favours the candidate.**
arXiv:2605.21404, read in full via the PDF. Its schema has five fields and "None has an item for
retry policy or for how errored runs enter the denominator"; the nearest items are a "Failure
breakdown: ... refusal, infinite loop, tool-use error, environment error, time-out, grader timeout,
incorrect final state" requiring papers to "report categorized counts at task granularity", and a
harness field whose sub-field is the "stopping rule (max steps, max tokens, max wall-clock)". It
reports disclosure counts for the failure breakdown — "Three of twelve papers reach 1.0 (AgentBench,
HumanEval, MBPP); four are partial; five are absent" — and for the harness field, "Seven of the eight
agent benchmarks score 0.5; one (SWE-bench...) scores 0.0. Zero score 1.0." It reports **no**
infrastructure failure rate and recomputes **no** scores or rankings. Unit of analysis: "the canonical
benchmark paper"; "We score the disclosure of an agent run, not its correctness"; twelve papers, one
auditor, single pass. (It is internally inconsistent about how many papers its own ar5iv conversion
lost — abstract says two, text says "The ar5iv HTML conversion failed for three papers".)

So: limb (e)'s *exact* object — the fraction of published evaluations stating their denominator
convention — is **unoccupied**, which upgrades the record's UNVERIFIED to verified-open. But its
immediate neighbour is occupied and quantified: the fraction disclosing categorized failure counts,
including the harness-side classes (environment error, time-out, grader timeout), is published at
3/12 full, 4 partial, 5 absent, on the same unit of analysis and the same endpoint type. Limb (e)
survives as a crevice beside a measured hole, not as an open field.

**(iii) Prior A verified at first hand, and its limits confirmed exactly as the record states them.**
matharena.ai/retry/, Dekoninck, Jovanović, Rögnvaldsson and Vechev, 16 January 2026: "We
experimentally demonstrate that frequent retries can overestimate performance by up to 5 percent on
MathArena benchmarks"; simulations "most exhibit an initial performance increase of 1-5%"; the
convention for the comparator arm, verbatim, "If all attempts fail, the solution is marked as
incorrect"; and the reversal, GPT-5.1 versus DeepSeek-v3.2, where "the ranking reverses at lower
stability under a maximum-time failure model". And confirming the record's honesty about A's gaps: it
reports **no** empirically measured failure rate (failure rates are modelled, and per-request timing
was not recorded for most historical data — only the directional finding that "longer requests, i.e.,
requests with more output tokens, are more likely to cause API errors"), and it does **not** consider
dropping the affected item (under a hard cutoff it notes that "accuracy is undefined" if all runs for
a problem fail). The record did not overstate A.

Net: the record's three load-bearing in-field priors are real and say what it says they say; one limb
it marked unverified is genuinely open; and I added a peer-reviewed cross-field prior that is
*stronger* evidence of the question having been asked than either of the two non-peer-reviewed
sources the Low grade was resting on.
