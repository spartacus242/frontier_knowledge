## 0. Restatement in my own words (before engaging the record's framing)

Benchmark questions sometimes appear verbatim-ish inside the pretraining corpus of an open-data
model. Everyone who has looked has counted *how often* a question appears; nobody has read *what
the page around it says the answer is*. So: (a) take a fixed set of items and an indexed open
corpus (Dolma, the Pile, LLM360, DCLM), pull every near-match of the stem, and sort each hit into
"says the gold answer", "says some other answer", "says nothing", "the benchmark's own key is the
wrong one" — and report those shares both per-occurrence and per-item, with CIs and a measured
extractor error rate. Then (b) for items whose answer-stating copies mostly contradict the gold
key, is the model *worse* on them than on never-seen items of the same difficulty (difficulty
pinned using models that never saw the item)? In plain terms: when a leak carries a wrong answer,
does deleting that item from the score push the score up or down, and by how many points? The
claim under attack is that the contamination correction has an empirically signed direction rather
than being inflation by assumption.

## 2. What I verified

**Prior C (arXiv:2508.13180) — read; the record's load-bearing quote is only partly confirmed.**
The paper says "we find retrieving a HuggingFace contaminated source does not improve the accuracy
relative to uncontaminated sources" (§3.1) and its Figure 3 caption reports "no improvement from
retrieving search-contaminated compared to uncontaminated samples." The record's clause "**even
lowering it to an extent**" did not come back in my read of the HTML; what I can confirm is a
**null**, not a decrease. Also confirmed: contamination is flagged by URL substring match
("huggingface" + a benchmark keyword), the authors concede the "simple, hard-coded URL detector
does not capture the full extent of search-time contamination"; the paper **never** systematically
compares the retrieved page's stated answer with gold and reports **no** wrong-answer fraction (the
only answer-content material is qualitative: "we observe instances where the model reasons to use
the retrieved ground truth label over its own calculations"); Table 1 rates are HLE 3.36/3.44/3.36%,
SimpleQA 1.20/1.11/0.99%, GPQA 2.34/1.90/4.15%. So the record's characterisation of C's *gap* (no
answer classification, no disagreement fraction) is accurate; its characterisation of C's
*anomaly* overstates it.
**My own arithmetic, flagged as mine:** the paper reports percentages, not counts, and I did not
read a GPQA denominator, so with a GPQA split in the low hundreds of items, 1.90-4.15% is a
contaminated subset of order ~4-20 items per agent. A null on a subsample that size is what a
null on a subsample that size looks like; the paper states no CI for it that I read.

**Prior B is real, with a title/author discrepancy.** arXiv:2601.04301 = Schaeffer, Kazdan, Abbasi,
Liu, Miranda, Ahmed, Puri, Mireshghallah, Koyejo, "**Quantifying the Effect of Test Set
Contamination on Generative Evaluations**" (7 Jan 2026) — found verbatim with abstract in
`lyy1994/awesome-data-contamination`'s README and in two independent arXiv-mirror repos. The record
gives the title as "Test Set Contamination of Generative Evaluations" and adds Barez,
Denisov-Blanch and Biderman to the author list; the listings I saw do not have those three. Minor,
not load-bearing, but the citation is not exact. The abstract corroborates the record's reading
(MATH replicas swept 0-3162, gold solutions in every copy, no wrong-answer arm, SFT "can either
increase or decrease performance ... depending on the amount of pretraining contamination").
I did **not** re-verify prior A (arXiv:2401.06059) — no budget left. COULD NOT READ: MMLU-Redux
(the ~6.5% figure the record itself marks as not opened), Magar & Schwartz, and neighbour D.

**One own-field paper that the record left unopened bears directly on the "nobody treats the
wrong-answer content as a variable" story.** The bibliography fragment for "Investigating Data
Contamination in Modern Benchmarks" (the TS-Guessing paper, record's query 10 hit, never opened)
reads: "This approach entails **masking a wrong answer** in a multiple-choice question and
prompting the model to fill in the gap ... in the MMLU benchmark, ChatGPT and GPT-4 demonstrated an
exact match rate of 52% and 57%, respectively, in guessing the missing options." So the candidate's
own literature already makes the *wrong-answer content of a leaked item* its central variable — as
a contamination **detector**, on the benchmark's own distractors, not on what a corpus document
asserts and not as a score correction. This does not touch limbs (a) or (b), but it does puncture
the record's silence mechanism ("the CONTENT of the occurrence never became a variable") and it is
a vocabulary a Disguise sweep would have to clear.
