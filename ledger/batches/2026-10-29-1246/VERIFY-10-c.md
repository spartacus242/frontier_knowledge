## 3. Does the Source column show the problem OPEN, or asked-and-partly-answered? (item 3)

Taking the entry's quotations as given (and as relocated by VERIFY-10-a and -b), both cited papers show the question **asked at coarse grain and partly answered observationally**, not merely open. A research run handed a bare "open" row would redo these checks:

**Barrett et al. 2017 (DOI 10.1145/3133876), §5.2** — the compilation-event coincidence check has been RUN on two VMs with a split result: Figure 7 shows "a clear correlation between a JIT compilation and a slowdown"; Figure 6 a slowdown "clearly correlated to garbage collection" (a PyPy memory leak, reported upstream); and "in many cases such as Figure 8 neither JIT compilation, nor garbage collection, are sufficient to explain odd behaviours", summarised as "Whilst some non-warmups are plausibly explained by GC or JIT compilation events, many are not, at least on HotSpot and PyPy." Unquantified ("many"), correlational ("we make no claim that they prove causation"), two VMs, instrumentation-mode runs separate from the timed runs. Their one explicit speculation about cause is not the loop: "we suspect that some of the odd results we have seen result from over-training VM heuristics on small sets of benchmarks" (§9). Direction: compilation events are implicated in SOME non-warmups and absent from MANY.

**Antoch et al. 2026 (PACMPL 10(OOPSLA1) art. 128), §3 and §8** — one recurrent change (at about 12 100 s, 3.4 h, Oracle Java 11, Renaissance 0.14.1) was dissected: "No extra dynamic compilation event and no extra garbage collection cycle occurs regularly around the time of the change" (compilation-event counts and compilation CPU time recorded per repetition); then a positive INTERVENTIONAL attribution to a non-compiler cause — "changing the maximum heap size, which duly shifts the time of the associated performance change", "caused by a combination of factors ranging from relatively high level actions (such as the use of reflection) ... down to machine code behavior (such as the sensitivity of memory access latencies to data-layout minutiae)", "the same code exhibits different performance after a change in data layout". §8 keeps the compiler mechanism live only modally ("even dynamic compilation may cause ... indefinitely repeating performance changes, for example in applications that generate code, or due to artifacts such as deopt loops") and widens the list ("memory layout changes ... can also keep triggering performance changes indefinitely"; heterogeneous-core scheduling). §1 frames the feedback loop as a PROPAGATOR of exogenous execution differences ("execution differences can also influence feedback-directed just-in-time compilation and therefore expand their impact to future workload repetitions"). The paper's stance: the lack of steady state is "an inherent property of these workloads rather than a fault". Direction: for the one change dissected, not the compiler; the compiler named as possible, not shown.

**Also partly answered, by a document the entry lists as opened by nobody — Zheng, Bulej, Binder, ECOOP 2017 (opened here, see section 4):** an INTERVENTION on the loop's deoptimisation→reprofile→recompile pathway, comparing three deoptimisation strategies ("the default strategy, which always invokes the InvalidateReprofile action"; "conservative, replaces the use of the InvalidateReprofile action with the RecompileIfTooManyDeopts", which "delays recompilation"; "adaptive, uses a custom deoptimization profile to choose a deoptimization action both during dynamic compilation and during program execution"), with the finding "the choice of a deoptimization strategy has negligible impact on steady-state performance" — where steady state is "the wall-clock time for the execution of the last benchmark iteration" over "at least 10 iterations and 10 seconds", 10 runs, geometric mean of speed-ups, **no convergence criterion** (NOT FOUND: coefficient of variation, changepoint, stopping rule), and no sentence saying any benchmark failed to reach steady state (NOT FOUND after the targeted prompt: "no steady state", "does not converge", "performance fluctuation", "instability", "oscillat", "deopt loop"). It does DOCUMENT the loop continuing to cycle past warm-up: "there are a few cases of repeated deoptimizations, especially in the scalac benchmark, where on average 10 deoptimizations per iteration are triggered even past the 15th iteration"; "The recompiled elemHashCode(Object) method will therefore repeatedly trigger deoptimizations and recompilations"; "the majority of the repeated deoptimizations originate from recompiled code"; "Of all the sites emitted for the DaCapo benchmarks, only 1.68% were actually triggered". So the mechanism the candidate hypothesises (recompilation cycling) is in print as an observed phenomenon in Graal, and an intervention on its policy moved start-up (adaptive strategy: "average speed-up of 6.4% for the DaCapo and ScalaBench workloads, and a speed-up of 5.1% for the Octane workloads" on a single core) but not steady-state mean time — at a 10-iteration horizon that cannot see Barrett's 2000-iteration non-convergence.

**Net for item (3):** the Source column as the entry's Routing line words it ("posed and unresolved ... and the second explicitly calls for additional experiments") is overstated twice — "posed" at the candidate's grain is not in either paper (VERIFY-10-a, -b), and the "additional experiments" sentence is an external-validity disclaimer (VERIFY-10-b) — and it omits the partial answers above. A row must say: asked at the grain of "JIT compilation or GC events" (Barrett H3) and "the usual culprits such as dynamic compilation" (Antoch §3); correlational checks run twice with split/negative results; one change causally attributed to heap size/layout by intervention; deoptimisation-policy intervention shows negligible steady-state-mean effect at short horizon; no freeze/pin/replay arm, no convergence endpoint under intervention, no between-arm share anywhere opened.
