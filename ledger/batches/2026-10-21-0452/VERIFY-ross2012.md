### Note on reconstruction method

The page summariser refuses bulk verbatim reproduction, so verbatim strings
below were obtained as **ordered consecutive fragments** each short enough to
return whole, then assembled only across consecutive returns, with junctions
checked by continuation of the clause. Each assembled passage is labelled with
its fragment count. No cut string was completed by inference.

## Claim (b) — "In 112/183 (61%) cases where a new medicine was prescribed, the decision maker was not the prescription writer."

**Verbatim, and cut at a sentence boundary on both sides.**

Reconstruction from **2 consecutive fragments** of the abstract's *Results*
section:

> fragment 1: `In 112/183 (61%) cases where a new medicine was prescribed, the decision maker was not the`
> fragment 2: `prescription writer.`

Assembled:

> "In 112/183 (61%) cases where a new medicine was prescribed, the decision maker was not the prescription writer."

Grammar on both sides of the cut, checked explicitly:

- **Left edge.** "In" is the *first word* of the Results section. Nothing
  precedes it inside Results; the preceding text is the section heading
  "Results". So the quotation does not begin mid-sentence and no preceding
  clause qualifies it.
- **Right edge.** The sentence ends at the full stop after "prescription
  writer." The page was asked directly whether any text follows those words
  within Results; it does not — the next thing on the page is the
  "Conclusions" heading. Word count returned for the whole Results section:
  **18 words**, which matches the assembled sentence exactly.

So the quoted string is the *entire* Results section of the abstract. There is
no surrounding clause that could change its sense, and no trailing hedge or
qualifier was dropped.

Two cautions that are about scope, not about the quotation:

1. The sentence is the **abstract's** statement of the headline result. I could
   not read the Results section of the full text, so I cannot report whether
   the paper states the same figure a second time, with or without a
   confidence interval, or breaks it down by ward or grade. Point (4) of the
   checking protocol — report every formulation and say which is hedged — is
   therefore **unanswerable at this read level** rather than answered in the
   negative.
2. The figure is stated bare: no confidence interval, no denominator
   uncertainty, no p value anywhere in the abstract.

Re-derivation at the author's own threshold: 112/183 = 0.61202…, i.e. 61.2%,
which rounds to the stated 61%. Internally consistent.

## Claim (c) — "Further research in a larger sample of hospitals is required to confirm generalisability of the results"

**Verbatim as far as it goes, but it is a MID-SENTENCE CUT at a comma, and the
cut drops a second stated purpose.**

Reconstruction of the relevant sentence from **2 consecutive fragments** of the
*Conclusions* section:

> fragment 3: `Further research in a larger sample of hospitals is required to confirm generalisability`
> fragment 4: `of the results, and to inform educational interventions to reduce error rates.`

Assembled sentence as served:

> "Further research in a larger sample of hospitals is required to confirm generalisability of the results, and to inform educational interventions to reduce error rates."

Grammar on both sides:

- **Left edge.** "Further" begins the sentence; it is the third sentence of the
  Conclusions. Clean start.
- **Right edge.** The claim stops at "of the results" — immediately before a
  comma and a coordinated second infinitive clause, "**and to inform
  educational interventions to reduce error rates**". The quoted words are
  accurate, but the sentence as written attaches a second purpose to the call
  for further research. A reader of the quotation alone would learn that the
  authors want generalisability confirmed; they would not learn that the
  authors frame the same further research as feeding educational
  interventions aimed at error reduction.

Whether that matters depends on what the quotation is doing in the ledger
entry. As a self-limitation statement it is faithful. As a characterisation of
*what the authors said further research is for*, it is incomplete.

For completeness, the whole Conclusions section, reconstructed from **4
consecutive fragments**:

> "Decision making and the writing of prescriptions are generally not undertaken by the same doctor. Moreover, communication about prescriptions is poor. Further research in a larger sample of hospitals is required to confirm generalisability of the results, and to inform educational interventions to reduce error rates."

Note that the first Conclusions sentence is itself hedged — "**generally** not
undertaken by the same doctor" — which is a weaker formulation than the bare
61% in Results.

## Claim (d) — what the paper measured

Everything here comes from the abstract's *Design of the study* and *Results*
sections. Where the claim asks for something the abstract does not state, that
is recorded as could not read, not as absent from the paper.

Reconstruction of *Design of the study* from **2 consecutive fragments**:

> "Observational data was collected in six wards, 2 weeks per ward, at a single large UK teaching hospital over a 12-week period from January to April 2011."

Reconstruction of *Purpose of the study* from **6 consecutive fragments** (see
the defect list — the summariser visibly revised one fragment boundary
mid-answer, so treat this passage as slightly less secure than the other
reconstructions; the wording was stable across the revision, only the cut
point moved):

> "Errors involving drug prescriptions are a key target for patient safety initiatives. Recent studies have focused on error rates across different grades of doctors in order to target interventions. However, many prescriptions are not instigated by the doctor who writes them. It is important to clarify how often this occurs in order to interpret these studies and create interventions. This study aimed to provisionally quantify and describe prescriptions where the identity of the decision maker and prescription writer differed."

Answering the claim item by item:

- **How many hospitals.** One. "a single large UK teaching hospital".
- **How many wards.** Six. "in six wards, 2 weeks per ward".
- **Kind of hospital and country.** A large teaching hospital in the UK. The
  abstract does not name the hospital, the city, or the UK nation; the author
  affiliations (Aberdeen, Belfast) are front matter and are not a statement
  about the study site. I did not read the Methods, so the site is
  **could not read**, not unstated-by-the-paper.
- **Period.** A 12-week period, January to April 2011, with two weeks of
  observation on each of the six wards. 6 wards x 2 weeks = 12 ward-weeks,
  consistent with the 12-week calendar span, which implies the wards were
  observed consecutively rather than in parallel. The abstract does not say
  so outright; that is my arithmetic, flagged as inference, not a quotation.
- **How the decider was ascertained as against the name on the prescription.**
  **COULD NOT READ.** The abstract says only "Observational data was
  collected" and that the study "aimed to provisionally quantify and describe
  prescriptions where the identity of the decision maker and prescription
  writer differed". It does not say who observed, whether ward rounds were
  shadowed, whether decision makers were asked directly, how a "decision"
  was operationally defined, or how the observation was matched to the
  signature on the chart. Anyone asserting a specific ascertainment method
  for this paper is going beyond the abstract.
- **How many prescriptions in the denominator.** The denominator that appears
  is **183**, and the abstract's own words restrict it: "183 (61%) cases
  **where a new medicine was prescribed**". The abstract gives **no** count of
  total prescriptions observed, no count of patients, no count of ward
  rounds, and no count of doctors. So 183 is a count of new-medicine
  prescribing events, and the total prescribing denominator is
  **could not read**.
- **Is the 61% new medicines only or all prescriptions.** **New medicines
  only**, explicitly, on the face of the sentence: the restrictive clause
  "where a new medicine was prescribed" governs the 183 cases from which the
  61% is computed. Representing 61% as a figure for prescriptions in general
  would misstate the source. The abstract says nothing about continuation,
  re-write, or drug-chart-rewriting prescriptions.
- **Unit of analysis / endpoint / comparator / estimand, in the source's own
  words.** The unit of analysis is a **case where a new medicine was
  prescribed**. The endpoint is whether, in that case, "the decision maker was
  not the prescription writer" — a binary discordance between two identities.
  The estimand is a single descriptive proportion of such discordance; the
  paper's stated ambition for it is to "**provisionally quantify and
  describe**" these prescriptions. There is **no comparator arm**: no control
  group, no before/after, no comparison between wards, grades or hospitals
  appears in the abstract. It is a one-arm descriptive prevalence estimate,
  self-described as provisional.

## Claim (e) — scope, and the three things the claim asks about

**Scope: yes, hospital inpatient prescribing — but "on ward rounds" is not
something the abstract says.**

- The title is "Who makes prescribing decisions **in hospital inpatients**? An
  observational study", and the Design places the work "in six wards, ... at a
  single large UK teaching hospital". So inpatient hospital prescribing is the
  paper's scope on its own face.
- The abstract **never uses the phrase "ward round"** or any equivalent. It
  says data were collected "in six wards", which is a place, not an activity.
  Attributing a ward-round design to this paper is an addition the abstract
  does not license. Whether the Methods describe ward-round observation is
  **COULD NOT READ**. This is exactly the shape of error the protocol warns
  about: "in six wards" is verbatim, "on ward rounds" is not.

**Electronic order records distinguishing an entering user, an authorising
prescriber and a cosigner:** the abstract says **nothing** about electronic
prescribing, order entry, computerised systems, user accounts, authorisation,
countersigning or cosigning. Its vocabulary throughout is paper-flavoured —
"the prescription writer", "the doctor who writes them", "the writing of
prescriptions". Full text **could not read**, so I cannot say the paper is
silent; I can say the abstract is.

Two cautions on this item:

1. Abstract-level silence is **not** a statement by the authors that such a
   distinction does not exist or should not be made. Silence is not a
   prohibition.
2. The paper's own decision-maker / prescription-writer split is a *different*
   distinction from the entering-user / authorising-prescriber / cosigner
   triad. A source that separates who decided from who wrote is not thereby a
   source that flags the absence of a three-way electronic-record role split.
   If the ledger entry treats this paper as evidence that the electronic-record
   gap has been noticed, that is an over-read.

**Instrumental-variable analysis:** nothing in the abstract. There is no
analysis section, no statistical method named, no estimator, no mention of
causal identification of any kind. The abstract reports one raw proportion.
Full text **could not read**. Given that the abstract's only statistic is a
percentage and the design is explicitly described as "provisional"
observational description with no comparator, an IV analysis in the body would
be surprising, but I did not read the body and am not reporting it as absent.

**Prescriber-targeted feedback:** nothing in the abstract under that
description. The nearest material is adjacent but distinct, and should not be
conflated:

- Purpose: "Recent studies have focused on error rates across different grades
  of doctors **in order to target interventions**" — this is the authors
  describing *other* studies' rationale, not their own intervention proposal.
  Per protocol point (5), this is a finding/motivation the paper attributes to
  prior literature, not to itself.
- Purpose: "It is important to clarify how often this occurs in order to
  interpret these studies **and create interventions**" — "interventions",
  unqualified. Not feedback, not prescriber-targeted.
- Conclusions: "to inform **educational interventions** to reduce error rates"
  — educational interventions, which is a different instrument from
  audit-and-feedback directed at individual prescribers.

So the abstract supports the weaker reading that the authors see their finding
as bearing on how error-rate-by-grade studies are interpreted and on the
design of *educational* interventions. It does not mention
prescriber-targeted feedback. Full text **could not read**.

**Source type.** This is a **primary observational study** (OUP issue section:
"Original Article"; Pure: "Article", peer-reviewed), not a review, protocol,
guideline, editorial or column. It is small, single-site, self-described as
"provisional", and reports one descriptive proportion with no stated
uncertainty.

## Defects

1. **(c) is a mid-sentence cut that drops a stated purpose.** The quoted
   string stops at "of the results", immediately before ", and to inform
   educational interventions to reduce error rates." Verbatim, but it
   truncates the authors' second reason for wanting further research.
2. **Read level is abstract only; three sub-questions of (d) and all three of
   (e) cannot be settled.** Specifically: how the decider was ascertained
   against the name on the prescription, the total prescription denominator,
   the hospital's identity, and whether the body mentions electronic order
   roles, instrumental variables or prescriber-targeted feedback. These are
   **COULD NOT READ**, not "the paper does not say". Any ledger wording that
   turns them into claims about the paper's silence overstates what was
   verified.
3. **No open full text exists to recover.** The Aberdeen Pure record carries no
   accepted manuscript or PDF; its only access route is the DOI. This is a
   paywall, not a tooling failure, and re-running the search will not fix it.
4. **"Ward rounds" is not in the abstract.** The abstract says data were
   collected "in six wards". If the ledger entry describes this paper's scope
   as ward-round prescribing, that phrase comes from somewhere other than the
   abstract.
5. **The 61% is new-medicine prescribing events only.** Its denominator, 183,
   is restricted by the clause "where a new medicine was prescribed". The
   abstract gives no all-prescriptions denominator, so the figure must not be
   presented as a proportion of hospital prescriptions generally.
6. **The headline figure carries no stated uncertainty.** 112/183 = 61.2%, and
   the abstract reports "61%" bare: no CI, no p value, no sensitivity
   analysis. Protocol point (8) — carry the stated uncertainty — has nothing to
   carry, and that absence should travel with the figure.
7. **Results and Conclusions are hedged differently.** Results asserts the bare
   61%; Conclusions softens it to "**generally** not undertaken by the same
   doctor". Neither abstract formulation carries a citation. Whether the body
   restates the figure differently is unknown at this read level.
8. **The paper's own decision-maker/writer split is not the electronic-record
   role triad.** Treating this source as having flagged the
   entering-user / authorising-prescriber / cosigner gap would be an
   over-read; it addresses a different two-way distinction.
9. **One reconstruction is slightly less secure than the others.** In the
   fragment pass over *Purpose of the study*, the summariser emitted a
   fragment 3, then visibly revised its own cut point mid-answer to stay under
   the character limit. The wording was identical across both versions and
   only the boundary moved, so the assembled passage is reported above, but it
   is flagged here rather than presented as equally solid. The *Results*,
   *Conclusions* and *Design* reconstructions showed no such wobble, and
   *Results* was additionally corroborated by an exact word count (18 words)
   and by a direct check that no text follows "prescription writer."
10. **Minor front-matter variance between records.** OUP serves "Christine
    Bond"; Aberdeen Pure serves "Christine Margaret Bond". Nothing turns on
    it, but the front matter is not byte-identical across the two sources, and
    the OUP form is the one the journal itself prints.
11. **No OCR damage was encountered.** The OUP article-abstract page served
    clean text on this occasion, so the routing note's OCR-damage warning did
    not bite and nothing needed flagging-as-served.
