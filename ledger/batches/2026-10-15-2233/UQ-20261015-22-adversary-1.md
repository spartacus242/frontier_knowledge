## Restatement (my own words, before reading the generator's framing back)

Regulatory PBPK platforms treat a set of numbers as "known human physiology": how much microsomal and cytosolic protein and how many hepatocytes there are per gram of liver, what fraction of cardiac output goes to liver and kidney, how big each organ is, and what each tissue is made of (lipid, water, phospholipid, albumin) for the Rodgers–Rowland / Poulin partition equations. The candidate asks two things.

(a) Go behind each of those numbers and count the bodies. For each parameter: how many genuinely separate human measurement campaigns sit underneath it (separate donors AND separate assay AND separate lab), how many actual subjects in total, from which decades, which ages/sexes/ancestries, and obtained how (autopsy, surgical resection, rejected transplant). Then build a matrix saying which pairs of parameters are, in whole or part, measurements of the *same* bodies.

(b) Take five published PBPK analyses that fed a real regulatory decision and can be re-run in an open platform. Re-run them twice: once with the vendor's default uncertainties treated as independent, once with each parameter's uncertainty-on-its-mean widened to reflect its true independent-source count and with the same-bodies correlations from (a) switched on. Ask whether the regulatory number crosses a line that was drawn in advance (an AUC ratio leaving 80–125% or the ICH M12 no-effect boundary; a paediatric exposure estimate leaving the adult matching band).

In short: *how few people are actually inside the physiology of these models, how much do the parameters share those same people, and does fixing that change any decision?* Note that (b) corrects the standard error of a mean and deliberately does not touch between-subject variability — this is the hinge I attack.

## Single strongest killing point

**Limb (b) is aimed at the wrong error term, and the record's own benchmark prior proves it.** R1 pre-commits to correcting sampling-uncertainty-on-the-mean plus shared-source correlation and to leaving population variability at platform defaults — but the boundaries it adopts (80–125% / ICH M12 no-effect; paediatric exposure-matching bands) are variability-dominated interval comparisons, and Bogdanovich et al. (PAGE 2026, verified) measure that this class of platform underpredicts between-subject variability by ~2-fold (AUC) and ~3-fold (Cmax) while total between-study imprecision is only 18–31% with mean bias of −6% to −4%. So the candidate proposes to tighten the smaller term while a larger, already-quantified, uncorrected term dominates the same decision metric. A "thin but robust" result — the most likely outcome — would then be uninformative about provenance, because robustness would follow from the choice of correction rather than from the adequacy of the parameter pedigree. Promotion requires either correcting the variability term as well, or pre-registering a power analysis showing the chosen correction is capable of crossing the named boundaries at all.
