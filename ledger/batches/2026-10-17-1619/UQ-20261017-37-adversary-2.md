## Restatement (my words, before attacking)

In trials that randomise whole clinics, practices or wards — parallel cluster or stepped-wedge — to an intervention that only works through clinicians (training, decision support, order sets, prescribing prompts), how often is it the case that the same clinician, within the same calendar window, treats patients in two clusters that are in *different* treatment states? Measure that from provider identifiers in site rosters or payer/EHR provider files rather than from what the trial report says, and report it two ways: the share of randomised clusters touched by at least one such clinician, and the share of patient-contacts those clinicians deliver. Then, in trials whose provider identifiers can actually be obtained, refit the published analysis with the clinician entered as a crossed (non-nested) random effect, or with an interference-aware estimator, and report how far the point estimate and the confidence interval move relative to the published one.

Unit of analysis the candidate claims: the randomised cluster and the patient-contact. Endpoint: realised contemporaneous cross-state clinician overlap, and the displacement of a published effect estimate. Comparator: the published analysis with a cluster (or cluster-period) random effect only. Estimand: a distribution over a pre-declared trial frame, plus a within-trial model-contrast.

## What the opened pages say (quoted, with scope checked)

**Moerbeek & Safarkhani 2018 (the kill).** A CRT design and sample-size paper for the case where "a second random factor, such as the professionals delivering the intervention, is crossed with the randomized clusters." Its taxonomy is exactly the candidate's structure: in the **complete** design "each professional delivers both conditions"; the **partial** design "restricts each professional to one condition," which the authors note "is less efficient." Its running example is a health-professional-delivered trial, and it cites a real trial (Mulligan et al. 2012) in which "one type of health professional delivered the control only and all other types delivered both control." On the empirical quantity the candidate proposes to measure, the paper is explicit that it is missing and wanted: "An intraclass correlation at the level of the professional was not given," values are assumed "without further justification," and for crossed random effects "such estimates are hardly available in the literature." SCOPE CHECK: this is a design/power paper, not a measurement — "no real outcome data are re-analyzed," and it "reports no measured proportion" of crossing. So it does **not** answer the candidate; it **asks** for the candidate's limb-(a) parameter, in a field the record never searched.

**Wilson-Aggarwal et al. 2022 (presupposition 1).** Staff were located by ward and day from Epic electronic medical records carrying "pseudonymous identifiers for the HCW" plus door-access logs with "a pseudonymous staff identifier," and the paper states that "the flow of staff between COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 wards continued throughout the pandemic." SCOPE CHECK: it reports no proportion of HCWs working on more than one ward, and it "does not mention" trials, arms, randomisation or contamination. So it is evidence that the candidate's *instrument* exists and is routine — not a prior for the candidate's object.

**Torgerson BMJ 2001 (triviality / historical asking).** The canonical statement that cluster randomisation answers contamination turns out to be entirely about participants: "people in the control group might learn about the experimental diet and adopt it themselves." It "does not discuss contamination arising from people who deliver the intervention." Its one number is a design threshold, not a prevalence: "In terms of total sample size, cluster trials are only more efficient where contamination exceeds 30%." SCOPE CHECK: this *helps* the candidate — the field's founding editorial on the topic never posed the deliverer-overlap version.

**Jenney & Lohr 2009.** Not a prior. Teachers are "nested within a single school" and randomisation is at school or teacher level, so agents are not shared across randomised clusters; the c=2 teachers per student is "a modeling assumption, not a measured quantity."

**Prior spot-check.** The record's P1 (Magill et al., BMC Med Res Methodol, doi 10.1186/s12874-018-0646-z) is real — exact title returned independently by query A9, together with its 2015 precursor. P2 (Jacobsen & Wood, UCL eprint 10108228) and P3 (Xierali, JABFM 31(2):260–269) were not re-opened inside my budget; I found nothing contradicting either, and record no independent verification of them.
