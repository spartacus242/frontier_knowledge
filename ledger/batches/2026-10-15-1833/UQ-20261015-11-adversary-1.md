## Restatement (my own words, not the generator's)

Broth-microdilution panels sometimes show a "hole": a well with no growth sitting below the MIC, with growth both one dilution lower and at least one dilution higher. Standards call this a skip and tell the reader to retest, read the top value, or void the drug. The candidate asks two things. (a) Is the hole a property of the bug rather than the plate? Concretely: test many isolates many times across plate lots and labs, read growth from machine optical density so the hole is not erased by the reading rule, and fit a mixed model to get the share of variance in "hole happened" and "how far below the MIC it happened" that belongs to the isolate — an ICC with a confidence interval, with lot, run, reader and lab as separate random effects. (b) Do isolates that tend to make holes also carry more resistant minority cells, as measured by population analysis profiling?

Reduced further: *is a discarded AST reading anomaly an isolate-level trait, and does it mark heteroresistance?* That reduction matters for Disguise, because the same sentence is true of several other AST anomalies.

### What the kill paper actually does
Same lab, same drug, same species as the candidate's lead case. Isolates are classified by whether the reading anomaly appears — "IC producers" versus "never-producers" — from testing in biological duplicate on separate days with technical triplicate, i.e. the anomaly is already treated as a repeatable isolate-level trait. The anomaly is then associated with heteroresistance: IC producers had 1.71 greater odds (95% CI 1.24-2.34; P < 0.01) of screening heteroresistant; 41/43 (95.3%) of IC producers screened positive versus 9/14 (64.3%) of never-producers. The companion poster asks the converse (does an HR screen predict IC?) and reports a null in 19 isolates. Heteroresistance was measured with a cheap disk-elution screen, not PAP.
