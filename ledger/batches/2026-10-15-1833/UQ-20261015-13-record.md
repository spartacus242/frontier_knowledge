## Gate 0 — Intake

Question (canonical, V0 as submitted): Do unseen-richness estimators applied to the distinct adverse-event types observed in a drug's pre-approval trial programme predict the number and identity of new adverse reactions added to that drug's label in the following five years better than exposure (patient-years) and drug-class baselines?

Question (canonical, R1, adopted at Gate 1): For new active substances approved by FDA or EMA in a fixed window (e.g. 2010–2018), does the estimated unobserved adverse-event-type count derived from the pooled pre-approval MedDRA preferred-term frequency spectrum (Chao1 asymptotic richness minus observed richness; Good-Turing coverage deficit f1/n) predict the number of distinct preferred terms added to the label's adverse-reactions section within five years of approval, with discrimination and calibration beyond a pre-specified baseline model of pre-approval patient-years, observed richness, drug class, biologic status and accelerated-approval status; and, secondarily, are label-added terms enriched among terms seen exactly once pre-approval?

Alternative formulations: (1) Can an adverse-event "species-accumulation curve" over patient exposure be extrapolated to a drug's post-marketing adverse-event repertoire? (2) Is the completeness of a pre-approval safety database estimable from the frequency spectrum of singleton and doubleton adverse-event terms, and does that estimate calibrate against what regulators later add to the label? (3) Lay: does the share of side effects seen only once in a drug's trials tell you how many more side effects will turn up after launch?

Motivating gap: Ecology, sequencing-library complexity (preseq) and software inspection all estimate what remains unobserved from the singleton/doubleton spectrum. Drug regulation sizes the pre-approval safety database by fixed exposure rules (ICH E1: ~1,500 exposed, 300–600 for six months, 100 for one year; rationale is the detectable-frequency bound, "no serious event in 100 patients → one-year incidence ≤3%") and sets post-marketing requirements without any estimate of how many reaction types the database has missed, although ~32% of 222 novel therapeutics approved 2001–2010 acquired a post-market safety event at a median 4.2 years (Downing et al., JAMA 2017) and trial sample size did not predict later boxed warnings (Schick et al., Drug Safety 2017). Capture-recapture in pharmacovigilance targets under-reporting of known reactions; structure- and label-based prediction (BMC Bioinformatics 2020) uses other drugs' data, not the drug's own frequency spectrum. MedDRA-coded AE tables (ClinicalTrials.gov results, EMA clinical-data publication, FDA reviews) supply exactly the spectrum the estimators need.

Presuppositions (each marked established / contested):
- P1 MedDRA preferred terms behave as countable, reasonably stable types — CONTESTED (coding granularity, splitting and synonymy are the direct analogue of taxonomic inflation; the MedDRA level changes counts; MedDRA versions change twice a year). Mitigation: pre-specify PT, sensitivity at HLT, version-lock.
- P2 Pre-approval AE sampling is approximately a random draw from the same type-abundance distribution that post-market use samples — CONTESTED and the most consequential premise (restricted trial populations, exclusion criteria, duration limits; post-market events partly come from new populations and longer exposure). The design must be able to attribute a NO to this premise rather than to P3 or P6; see Gate 1.
- P3 Label additions are a usable readout of realised richness — CONTESTED (label changes are regulatory decisions and depend on reporting volume, which tracks sales; non-serious additions dominate counts). Mitigation: pre-specified secondary readouts (signals in FAERS/EudraVigilance or PRAC signal lists; serious-term subset).
- P4 Term-with-count AE data exist for enough approvals — PARTLY ESTABLISHED (ClinicalTrials.gov results tables list all-cause AEs by PT with a frequency threshold; EMA Policy 0070 CSRs from 2015; FDA reviews; laborious and thresholded, so f1/f2 may be under-reported in some sources).
- P5 Post-approval detection effort can be placed on the same effort axis as trial exposure — CONTESTED (spontaneous reporting is not systematic collection; effort units differ). R1 therefore uses the asymptotic and coverage quantities as predictors and treats exposure-point extrapolation as secondary (Chao et al. 2014 advise against extrapolating beyond ~2–3× the reference sample; post-market exposure is often 100–1000× trial exposure).
- P6 Trials of one programme can be pooled as one sample — CONTESTED (solicited vs unsolicited AE collection, differing visit schedules). Mitigation: pool only non-solicited treatment-emergent AEs from controlled trials; sensitivity on phase-3-only.
- P7 The estimators are informative on these samples — ESTABLISHED in principle (Chao1 is a lower bound; Good-Turing coverage is robust), but their value as predictors is the question.

It is a question, not a thesis: both YES and NO are live, and the premise most likely to fail (P2) is the one the result would quantify.

## Gate 1 — Well-posedness

What would count as an answer: A retrospective, pre-registered cohort of approvals (e.g. all new active substances approved by FDA and EMA 2010–2018 with retrievable PT-level AE tables). For each drug: (a) reconstruct the pooled pre-approval PT frequency spectrum (f1, f2, …, n, S_obs) from controlled-trial unsolicited treatment-emergent AEs at a locked MedDRA version; (b) compute Chao1 (bias-corrected), the Good-Turing coverage deficit f1/n, and as a secondary quantity the Chao–Jost rarefaction/extrapolation estimate at 2× the reference sample; (c) read out the number of distinct PTs added to the adverse-reactions section of the label (US PI sections 6/6.2; EU SmPC 4.8) within five years, at the same MedDRA level, serious subset reported separately; (d) fit nested models: baseline (log patient-years, S_obs, drug class, biologic, accelerated approval, psychiatric indication) vs baseline plus the completeness quantities; (e) report discrimination (e.g. C-index for the count or AUC for "≥k additions"), calibration curves, and the pre-specified improvement threshold. Positive control, added at R1 to make a NO interpretable: a within-programme hold-out in which the phase-2 spectrum predicts the number of new PTs that appear in phase 3 (where P2 approximately holds). YES = the estimator improves discrimination beyond the pre-specified margin and calibrates within stated bands. NO with a passing positive control = the estimator works within the trial sampling frame but not across the trial-to-market boundary, which quantifies the sampling-frame shift that P2 denies. NO with a failing positive control = the estimators are uninformative on AE spectra at these sample sizes (P1/P7 problem). Partial = works within established drug classes but not first-in-class or biologics.

Why R1 was needed (one reformulation, both versions recorded above): V0 asked the estimators to predict the "identity" of new reactions, which richness estimators cannot do by construction (they are type-agnostic); V0 also implied extrapolation to post-market exposure, which the estimators' own guidance forbids at the relevant multiples and which P5 makes unit-incommensurable; and V0's claim that a NO is "itself usable" was ambiguous between P2 failure, P3 readout failure and P7 estimator failure. R1 drops identity from the primary question (keeping a singleton-enrichment secondary, which is the Good-Turing-consistent form of an identity claim), replaces the exposure-point extrapolation with asymptotic and coverage quantities, and adds a positive control so that NO is attributable.

Presupposition check: every presupposition is listed and marked; P2, P3, P5 contested and mitigated by design, not assumed. Well-posedness score (R1): 3/3. (V0 would score 2/3: identity clause unanswerable, NO ambiguous.)

## Gate 3 — Significance

Consequence: YES → every NDA/MAA already contains the inputs, so a drug-specific completeness index (coverage deficit, estimated unseen types) could be reported in the integrated summary of safety and used to grade post-marketing requirements, PASS obligations and RMP "missing information" sections; ICH E1's fixed exposure thresholds would gain a data-driven complement; Schick et al.'s null for sample size would be explained (size matters only through coverage). NO with a passing positive control → the first quantitative measurement of how far post-market reactions come from outside the trial sampling frame rather than from undersampling, which argues for population-broadening (pragmatic trials, expanded-access and registry data pre-approval) over larger conventional trials, and converts an assumption regulators hold implicitly into a measured quantity. NO with a failing positive control → MedDRA PT spectra are not a usable "community sample" at trial sizes, which closes the transplant and bears on any future use of term-level AE counts as a completeness measure. Partial (works for established mechanisms, not first-in-class or biologics) → maps onto Downing's novelty and biologic predictors and would justify class-dependent post-marketing intensity.

Audience: FDA CDER/OSE reviewers writing post-marketing requirements and EMA PRAC/RMP assessors (they decide surveillance intensity with no completeness figure today); ICH E1 and E19 maintainers; sponsor safety statisticians and the SAVVY/PSI/EFSPI safety-statistics community (they already standardise AE estimation and would own the computation); pharmacovigilance methodologists (ISoP, Uppsala Monitoring Centre) for the singleton-enrichment secondary; HTA bodies judging evidence completeness. Action is plausible because the index is cheap, uses existing data, and fits existing documents; whether it changes decisions depends on effect size, and regulators act on serious reaction identity more than on counts, which is the main limit on the score.

Fecundity: at which MedDRA level AE types behave as species (an empirical answer to the granularity problem); whether AE-type accumulation follows a Heaps-type law in exposure and whether its exponent differs for biologics; whether singleton terms in trials are the reactions later labelled (identity via Good-Turing logic, which would redirect signal detection toward a drug's own rare trial terms); whether subgroup spectra (age, renal function) quantify sampling-frame shift directly; extension to vaccines (largest exposure jump) and devices; whether solicited and unsolicited collection give different coverage; whether the coverage deficit predicts the timing (not only the count) of label additions.

Anchoring: Downing 2017 (one-third acquire events; novelty and biologic status predict); Schick 2017 (trial size does not predict boxed warnings, an unexplained null); ICH E1's ad hoc thresholds and the "1 in 1,000 not characterised" statement; the known limits of richness extrapolation (Chao1 as a lower bound under heterogeneity; extrapolation unreliable beyond 2–3× the sample); the MedDRA granularity debate; SAVVY's finding that standard AE estimators are biased by follow-up (the same spectrum inputs are affected); the broader "unseen" problem in genetics, linguistics and software, where the same estimators have been validated against later discovery.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism): a disciplinary boundary reinforced by an incentive gap and a data-access change. Trial biostatistics treats AEs term by term as incidence proportions or time-to-event quantities (ICH E9, SAVVY) and never as a community sample whose type count is the estimand; pharmacovigilance treats the term vocabulary as a coding problem and completeness as report-field completeness; ecologists and sequencing statisticians do not read regulatory safety summaries. Sponsors have no incentive to quantify the incompleteness of their own database, and regulators hold a coarse answer (ICH E1's rule-of-three exposure thresholds) that pre-empts the search for a better one. Finally, the inputs (PT-level all-cause AE tables across whole programmes) only became broadly retrievable with ClinicalTrials.gov results reporting (2008 onward) and EMA clinical-data publication (2015 onward), so the question was not testable at scale when the estimators were being transplanted into other fields in the 1990s–2000s. A weaker alternative mechanism, to be tested at Gate 4: the answer is presumed negative ("everyone knows trial populations differ from the market"), so no one measured it.
