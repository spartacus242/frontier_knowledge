## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Serialized track-and-trace now stamps every drug package with a history: when it was packed, who held it and for how long, and how much labelled life is left when it reaches the patient. The candidate asks (i) what those package ages, remaining shelf lives and custody/dwell histories actually look like across products and dispensing channels, and whether packages of the SAME product vary much (a pre-registered within-product ICC is the gate); (ii) if they do, whether, WITHIN a batch, older or longer-dwelling packages assay lower (potency/dissolution against the monograph); and (iii) whether those provenance variables predict a (still unnamed) potency-sensitive clinical endpoint. Put bluntly: "how old is the medicine when the patient gets it, does that vary, and does it matter?" — now that the distribution chain is observable per package.

Single strongest killing point: the candidate's novelty is thinnest exactly where its clinical claim is strongest — remaining shelf life at dispensing has been readable off every pack for decades and is already a regulated dispensing rule ("Kurzläufer"), and per-product end-of-shelf-life clinical consistency trials already answer "does aged-within-expiry product still work" by design; what serialization adds (custody path, dwell time) is a proxy for cumulative excursion exposure that is bounded a priori by stability design and measured directly, where it matters, by temperature logging — so the candidate risks being positive only for thermolabile products where cold-chain monitoring already governs, and null everywhere it would be news.
