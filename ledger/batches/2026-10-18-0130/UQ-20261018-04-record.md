Domain(s): Medicine — perioperative care, infection prevention and sterile processing; surgical patient safety. Method-owning neighbours: reliability engineering (usage-based service life, wear-out hazard) and hospital asset/clinical engineering.

Question (canonical) — V1 (as intaken):
Now that item-level instrument-tracking systems record each surgical instrument's and tray's own reprocessing history and link it to the cases it was used in, does an item's accumulated reprocessing cycle count and age predict intraoperative instrument malfunction, sterilisation-process failure, and surgical-site infection in the cases it serves?

Question (canonical) — V2 (the one Gate 1 reformulation, used for grading; V1 retained above):
In hospital systems whose instrument-tracking system records per-ITEM cumulative reprocessing-cycle counts and per-item-to-case linkage, what are the three separately-estimated associations between an item's cycle count (and calendar age) at the moment of use and (i) intraoperative malfunction or exchange of THAT item, estimated at the item-use level; (ii) sterilisation-process failure of the load or tray containing it, estimated at the load/tray level; and (iii) NHSN-defined surgical-site infection at 30 and 90 days in the case it served, estimated at the case level with the case's maximum or mean item cycle count as exposure — each adjusted for service line, surgeon, case complexity and tray type, each with a pre-declared negative-control exposure and stated minimum detectable effect?
(Reason for reformulation: V1 says "predict" of three endpoints at three different units of analysis under one verb, which leaves the estimand and the denominator undefined; V2 fixes the unit per limb. V2 is graded; the novelty finding below applies to both.)

Alternative formulations (as intaken):
- Do reusable surgical instruments have a measurable service life in clinical use, and is current practice — retire on visible damage, or at a cycle limit validated on a bench — consistent with it?
- Is the number of times an instrument set has been through decontamination a risk factor for anything that happens to the patient it is used on?

Motivating gap (as intaken, with Gate 2 correction):
Intaken claim: the newly measurable quantity is "how many times has THIS instrument been reprocessed, and in which case is it being used now"; per-item cycle histories linked to cases are a by-product of barcode/RFID tracking "nobody has used as an exposure", with attention concentrated on flexible endoscopes because outbreaks forced it there, and rigid-instrument life governed by bench-validated cycle limits and retirement on visible damage.
GATE 2 CORRECTION, load-bearing: the premise "nobody has used it as an exposure" is false. Yoshikawa et al. 2019 (BMC Health Services Research 19:695) took per-item cumulative use counts straight out of an RFID surgical-instrument-management-system log at a single centre (34,390 operations, Sep 2013–Apr 2017) and used them as the exposure in a logistic model and in Kaplan–Meier survival analysis against instrument failure, reporting a per-use odds ratio of 1.026 and predicted-failure thresholds of 169 and 224 uses. The "new instrument" of this candidate was mined for exactly this exposure seven years ago, in the candidate's own field, and the service-life limb (alternative formulation 1) was both asked and answered there. What remains unoccupied on this record is only the tray/load limb (ii) and the patient limb (iii).

Presuppositions (status after Gate 2):
1. Item-level tracking data with per-cycle timestamps AND per-case linkage are retrievable for research — CONTESTED, and the key feasibility presupposition. PARTLY SUPPORTED: Yoshikawa et al. state instruments are linked to operations through use records and set IDs, so item-to-operation linkage exists in at least one deployment; but they explicitly used no patient data, so item-to-PATIENT-OUTCOME linkage remains unevidenced. Deployments vary and many systems associate only the tray, not the instrument, with a case.
2. Reprocessing cycles are a meaningful wear exposure over practical lifetimes — SUPPORTED in direction for the instrument-failure endpoint, and by a prior rather than by assumption: Yoshikawa's per-use OR of 1.026 is positive, though on a model with R² = 0.032, i.e. cycle count explains very little of the variance in which instrument gets a repair request. Not established for cleaning/sterilisation efficacy: van der Ploeg et al. 2024 found greater scope usage associated with LOWER contamination odds (aOR 0.80, 95% CI 0.64–0.995), the opposite sign to the wear hypothesis.
3. Intraoperative instrument malfunction is recorded consistently enough to be an endpoint — CONTESTED and probably under-recorded. Note that the closest prior did not use the OR record at all: it used "issuance of a repair request" as the failure definition and did not independently verify breakage or malfunction, which is evidence that the OR-record endpoint this candidate wants is not routinely available.
4. Confounding by tray type, service line and surgeon is addressable — CONTESTED and unresolved. Yoshikawa's own finding that usage is non-uniform across nominally identical instruments is the mechanism of the entanglement, not a solution to it.
5. Manufacturers' reprocessing limits are bench-derived rather than field-validated — VERIFIED for the lifetime/biocompatibility arm: a reprocessing-validation laboratory's own service description states that "the medical device is subjected to a simulation of several reprocessing cycles" and that "through several cycle series, the maximum lifetime of the products is determined and defined", under DIN EN ISO 10993-1/-15 (CleanControlling, end-of-life simulation of reprocessing cycles). The page does not cite ISO 17664 or AAMI and says nothing about field data. So the asymmetry the question exploits is real, but it is a weaker asymmetry than claimed, because the field-side measurement has already been made once (presupposition 2).
6. Surgical-site infection is attributable at the level of an individual instrument's history — WEAK and unimproved by Gate 2. No prior found attempts it; both device-side priors stop at contamination or repair, and the pedicle-screw paper's SSI connection is explicitly speculation, not measurement.

What would count as an answer:
In one or more hospital systems with item-level tracking, a pre-registered cohort of cases linked to the specific trays and items used, estimating the association between a pre-declared exposure (cycle count at time of use; item age; cycles since last inspection) and three pre-declared endpoints — (i) intraoperative instrument malfunction or exchange recorded in the OR record, (ii) sterilisation-process failure attributable to the load or tray (biological-indicator failure, wet pack, load recall), (iii) SSI within 30 and 90 days under NHSN definitions — adjusted for service line, surgeon, case complexity and tray type, with a pre-declared negative-control exposure (cycle count of an instrument type not used in that procedure) and a stated minimum detectable effect for each endpoint. A tight null on (iii) answers the patient-safety limb; a positive on (i) or (ii) answers the service-life limb and would bear on retirement schedules and AAMI/AORN reprocessing guidance.
Gate 1 verdict: well-posed in V2. Settling evidence is specifiable, the negative control and the MDE requirement are stated, and every presupposition is flagged. Caveat carried forward: limb (iii)'s answer is pre-determined to be a null of unknown informativeness unless the MDE is stated in advance, which the answer-criterion does require.

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, all opened):
P1 (CLOSEST — near-duplicate of alternative formulation 1). Yoshikawa T, Kimura E, Akama E, Nakao H, Yorozuya T, Ishihara K. "Prediction of the service life of surgical instruments from the surgical instrument management system log using radio frequency identification." BMC Health Services Research 2019;19:695. https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s12913-019-4540-0
  - UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the individual instrument (136 Cooper scissors in analyses I–II; Cooper 35 / Metzenbaum 28 / Kelly 20 / Kocher 58 / Pean 28 in the survival analysis).
  - EXPOSURE: per-item cumulative usage count read out of the RFID instrument-management-system log — the candidate's own "newly measurable quantity".
  - ENDPOINT: instrument failure, defined as issuance of a repair request (not independently verified breakage or malfunction).
  - REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: instrument type, pairwise, by log-rank; the logistic model has no comparator arm.
  - ESTIMAND: per-use odds of failure (1.026 per additional use; model R² = 0.032; Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.117) and the usage count at which predicted failure probability exceeds 0.5 (169 uses) and 0.8 (224 uses), plus Kaplan–Meier survival against usage count with pairwise log-rank p-values (Kelly vs Kocher p = 0.017; Kelly vs Pean p = 0.004; Cooper vs Metzenbaum p = 0.036; Kocher vs Pean p = 0.106).
  - Explicitly NOT examined: any patient outcome, SSI, or sterilisation-process failure; no patient data were used and failures were not linked to cases. Weaknesses visible in the text: inconsistent Cooper n (136 vs 35), censoring handling unstated.

P2. van der Ploeg K, Vos MC, Erler NS, Bulkmans AJC, Mason-Slingerland BCGC, Severin JA, Bruno MJ. "Impact of duodenoscope reprocessing factors on duodenoscope contamination: a retrospective observational study." Journal of Hospital Infection 2024;154:88–94. https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/impact-of-duodenoscope-reprocessing-factors-on-duodenoscope-conta/
  - UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the duodenoscope surveillance culture, clustered by scope in a logistic mixed-effects model (307 cultures, 1,296 reprocessing cycles, one scope model, Feb 2022–Dec 2023).
  - EXPOSURE: reprocessing-process factors — cleaning delay >30 min, manual cleaning ≤5 min, drying <90 min, personnel reprocessing frequency, storage >7 days — plus "duodenoscope usage".
  - ENDPOINT: scope contamination, i.e. culture growth of micro-organisms of gut or oral origin.
  - REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: the opposite category of each dichotomised factor.
  - ESTIMAND: adjusted ORs. Manual cleaning ≤5 min aOR 1.61 (1.10–2.34, p = 0.01); increased usage aOR 0.80 (0.64–0.995, p = 0.045); 18.9% contaminated overall.
  - No patient outcome. Scope age and number of prior reprocessing cycles were not named exposures; "usage" is undefined on the record available.

Significance — consequence:
If limb (i) is positive at the OR-record endpoint, retirement policy changes from "retire on visible damage or at the bench-validated cycle ceiling" to a usage-indexed schedule, and the tracking systems already installed for inventory and recall acquire a second, clinical, function. If limb (ii) is positive, tray history enters sterilisation process control alongside load parameters, which is a change to AAMI ST79-governed monitoring rather than to purchasing. If limb (iii) is positive at any plausible magnitude it would be the first patient-level consequence ever attached to an individual instrument's biography, and would force per-item retirement into infection-prevention practice. If limb (iii) is null and tightly bounded, that bound is itself worth having: it closes a worry that is in print (P3) but has never been measured, and it would be evidence against the single-use-instrument advocacy that currently argues from mechanism alone (query 11 returned mostly that advocacy). The discount is that limb (i)'s direction is already known to be positive-but-weak from P1 (per-use OR 1.026, R² 0.032), so the consequence of limb (i) is now mostly about generalising and re-endpointing an existing finding rather than about establishing one.
Significance — audience:
Sterile-processing departments and their system vendors (who hold the data and set tray-assembly and retirement rules); AAMI ST79 and AORN guideline committees (who would have to say whether cycle count belongs in reprocessing guidance); instrument manufacturers and their reprocessing-validation laboratories, whose maximum-lifetime claims are set by simulated cycle series under ISO 10993 (verified above) and would acquire a field comparator; hospital infection preventioners and NHSN-reporting teams for limb (iii); value-analysis committees weighing reusable against single-use instrument sets. Each is identifiable and each has an existing decision the answer would enter.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens: is cycles-since-last-inspection a better exposure than lifetime cycles (i.e. is the hazard reset by inspection)? Does the wear hazard differ by instrument class — hinged, lumened, powered, robotic — which is where the AAMI robotic-instrument reprocessing discussion already sits? Can a usage-indexed retirement rule be cost-compared against single-use conversion on the same data? And a measurement question: does the tracking system's own definition of a "cycle" (scanned at decontamination? at assembly?) change the estimate, which is the sort of instrument-definition problem this ledger has repeatedly found to dominate.
Significance — anchoring:
Touches the single-use-versus-reusable instrument debate (currently argued on mechanism and cost, not on per-item outcome data); the duodenoscope reprocessing-failure literature, which is the acknowledged asymmetry and where usage is already a covariate with a surprising sign (P2); the unexplained residual in SSI risk models after patient and procedure factors; and the reliability-engineering question of whether a reprocessed instrument is a repairable system whose hazard is reset by maintenance.

Reason for silence:
The honest mechanism is now split, and only half of it survives. For limbs (ii) and (iii) the mechanism is a data-ownership and governance boundary rather than an absence of data: the per-item reprocessing history lives in the sterile-processing tracking system, which is procured and governed as inventory and recall infrastructure, while the case, the load record and the NHSN SSI determination live in the EHR and the infection-prevention surveillance system — separate custodians, separate IRB and vendor-contract paths, and no routine join key at the item-to-patient level (P1's authors had the item-to-operation link and still used no patient data at all). Add the outbreak-driven allocation of attention: contamination research went where outbreaks were, i.e. flexible endoscopes, and that literature now has prospective multicentre contamination studies while rigid instruments have bench cycle simulations.
