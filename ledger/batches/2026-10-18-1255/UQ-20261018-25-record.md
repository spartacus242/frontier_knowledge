Domain(s): Medicine — clinical-trial methodology; research ethics; health-services / pharmacoepidemiological measurement

Question (canonical):
- **V1 (as intake):** In the 12 months after a randomised trial of a chronic therapy closes, what happens to its participants — what fraction continue the trial drug, revert to their pre-trial therapy or are left untreated, how large is the discontinuity in treatment and in the trial's own outcome measures at the last visit, and does that discontinuity differ between arms?
- **V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, the one permitted, for well-posedness only; scored as canonical):** For a pre-declared frame of completed randomised trials of a chronic therapy whose participant rosters carry a per-participant last-visit date and are linkable to routine dispensing and clinical records (consent or regulatory permission established before any data pull), and with the exposure defined as that dated person-level closeout: (i) what is the distribution over the pre-declared treatment states — trial drug continued, pre-trial therapy resumed, switched to a third agent, nothing dispensed — at 3, 6 and 12 months after each participant's own last visit, measured against a look-back window of 12 months before randomisation; and (ii) against a declared control series (matched never-randomised patients, or the participant's own pre-randomisation series under an interrupted-time-series model with a pre-declared handling of the monitoring effect), what are the level change and slope change at the closeout date in the trial's primary outcome or a declared nearest routinely captured proxy, pooled and by randomised arm?

Alternative formulations:
- What is the measured clinical cost of a trial ending, to the people who were in it?
- Treating trial closeout as an exposure, is there a detectable treatment and outcome discontinuity in participants' routine records after their last visit, and is it arm-dependent?

Motivating gap:
The intake's reading was that the field is dense with how-answers about ending a trial (site-closeout operations, open-label-extension design, audits of whether protocols and papers *state* a post-trial access provision under Declaration of Helsinki para 34 and CIOMS guideline 6) and silent on the empirical question of what participants' own records show after the last visit; and that the enabling method — extending trial follow-up by linkage to routinely collected data — exists only to extend ascertainment of long-term *efficacy*. **Gate 2 falsified the central factual claim of this gap.** The empirical post-closeout question has been asked, participant-level, by randomised arm, from routine records: SPRINT's extended follow-up read outpatient blood pressure out of routine EHR after close-out and reports the between-arm systolic difference collapsing from 5.9 mm Hg at 5 years to -0.21 mm Hg at 10 years alongside loss of the mortality benefit; UKPDS post-trial monitoring states that "Between-group differences in glycated hemoglobin levels were lost after the first year." What Gate 2 did **not** find is the first limb of V2 — a person-level post-trial *treatment-state* distribution dated to each participant's own last visit with a pre-randomisation look-back — and the formal treatment of closeout as a dated exposure against a declared control series.

Presuppositions:
1. CONTESTED, likely binding — participants of enough completed trials can be identified in routine data with consent or regulatory permission. Trial consent often does not cover post-trial linkage, so the feasible frame may be confined to jurisdictions with registry linkage and broad consent (Nordic registries, UK, VA, large integrated US systems). Partially *established* by the priors: the SPRINT secondary analysis obtained routine outpatient EHR blood pressures for a 2,944-participant subset, and the JAMA Netw Open scoping review documents 113 trials extended by linkage, so the frame is non-empty — but note both are consented, sponsor-cooperating extensions, not third-party linkage.
2. Per-participant last-visit dates are obtainable, so closeout is a dated person-level exposure. UNVERIFIED here: the priors date closeout to a trial-level window (SPRINT: "close-out visits through July 2016"), not per participant.
3. Pre-randomisation therapy is observable in the same routine data (requires a look-back window before trial entry). Not evidenced either way by anything opened.
4. CONTESTED — arm assignment can be released for blinded trials. Without sponsor or trialist cooperation the arm-dependence limb is not estimable and only the pooled discontinuity can be reported; the entry says so rather than assuming access. (The priors are sponsor-run extensions, which is exactly how they got arm labels.)
5. CONTESTED — any discontinuity at closeout is attributable to closeout rather than to concurrent secular change or regression to the mean after an intensively monitored period. Requires the declared comparator and pre-declared monitoring-effect handling written into V2. **This is the presupposition the priors leave unaddressed**: neither SPRINT nor UKPDS-PTM declares a control series for the closeout date itself; both compare arms to each other over post-trial time.
6. The trial's primary outcome, or a declared nearest routinely captured proxy, is measurable in routine data at comparable intervals before and after closeout. Established for surrogate-type outcomes (office BP, HbA1c) by both index priors; unestablished for event or patient-reported primaries.

What would count as an answer:
Per-participant dispensing/prescribing and outcome series from 12 months before randomisation to 12 months after each participant's last visit, with pre-declared estimands: the fraction in each post-trial treatment state at 3, 6 and 12 months; the level change and slope change in the trial's primary or declared proxy outcome at the closeout date against the declared control series; and the arm difference in each, all with intervals and with the attribution design stated. A near-zero discontinuity is a substantive answer and would retire a standing ethical worry; a large one attaches a magnitude to the Helsinki para 34 obligation. Note that for limb (ii) an answer of this shape **already exists for index trials** (see priors), so only the treatment-state distribution and the closeout-as-exposure design remain unanswered.

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, all opened and read):
1. **Jaeger BC, Bress AP, Bundy JD, et al. "Longer-Term All-Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality With Intensive Blood Pressure Control: A Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA Cardiol. 2022;7(11):1138-1146. doi:10.1001/jamacardio.2022.3345** — https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/article-abstract/2797064 (abstract + results fragments served by publisher).
   - Unit of analysis: individual SPRINT participants (9,361 for mortality; a 2,944-participant subset for blood pressure).
   - Endpoint: all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (National Death Index 2016-2020) **plus outpatient systolic blood pressure taken from routine electronic health records** after close-out.
   - Reference/comparator: original randomised arm (intensive <120 mm Hg vs standard <140 mm Hg).
   - Estimand: hazard ratios in the trial phase vs over total follow-up (median 8.8 y), and mean post-trial outpatient SBP by arm; intensive-arm SBP rose from 132.8 mm Hg at 5 y to 140.4 mm Hg at 10 y, and the between-arm difference went from 5.9 mm Hg at 5 y to "-0.21 mm Hg" at 10 y. No medication-use reporting in what was served.
2. **Fitzpatrick T, Perrier L, Shakik S, Cairncross Z, Tricco AC, Lix L, Zwarenstein M, Rosella L, Henry D. "Assessment of Long-term Follow-up of Randomized Trial Participants by Linkage to Routinely Collected Data: A Scoping Review and Analysis." JAMA Netw Open. 2018;1(8):e186019. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.6019** — https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2719129 (opened; this is the paper the intake named but had not read).
   - Unit of analysis: pairings of original and extension reports — 113 extended trials, 155 outcome analyses (not participants).
   - Endpoint: long-term efficacy/safety outcomes — mortality in 77.9% of extensions, cancer 36.3%, cardiovascular 32.7%; sources were vital statistics 31.9%, health administrative data 27.4%, cancer registries 24.8%, specialised registries 11.5%.
   - Reference/comparator: original randomisation in 104/113 (92.0%); 9 (8.0%) not analysed by randomisation; ITT reported in 86.9% of the 99 giving detail.
   - Estimand: the long-term randomised treatment effect by original allocation; the review itself declares no single estimand and re-analyses nothing. Directly relevant admissions: post-trial intervention data were **noted in 18 studies (15.9%) and used in 7 (6.2%)**, and treatment switches and co-interventions were "usually undocumented"; it discusses the "so-called legacy effect".
3. **Holman RR, Paul SK, Bethel MA, Matthews DR, Neil HAW. "10-Year Follow-up of Intensive Glucose Control in Type 2 Diabetes." N Engl J Med. 2008;359:1577-1589 (UKPDS post-trial monitoring)** — read from an institutional PDF mirror of the published paper, https://www.farm.ucl.ac.be/FARM2129/2009-2010/Jonas/UKPDS_Follow_up_study.pdf (mirror, not the publisher page; flagged as such).
   - Unit of analysis: individual patients.
   - Endpoint: HbA1c and other measurements at annual clinic visits in post-trial years 1-5 (questionnaires only in years 6-10), plus clinical events and mortality (ONS / NI General Register Office).

Novelty — how this differs from each:
- **vs Jaeger 2022 (SPRINT):** the smallest gap, and the one that sets the grade. Same unit (participant), same comparator (randomised arm), same data type (routine records), and an endpoint that *is* the trial's own primary measurement — so the candidate's limbs (ii) and (iii), "how large is the discontinuity in the trial's own outcome measure" and "does it differ between arms", are asked and answered there for an index trial, with a number. Differences are design-level, not question-level: SPRINT dates close-out at trial level and reports means at 5 and 10 years rather than a level-and-slope change at each participant's own last visit; it declares no control series for the closeout date (arms are compared to each other, leaving the monitoring/secular-trend confounding of presupposition 5 untouched); and it reports no treatment-state distribution. A candidate whose novel content is a better identification strategy for an already-asked question is not an un-asked question.
- **vs Fitzpatrick 2018 (scoping review):** genuinely different object — unit is trial-report pairs, endpoint is long-term efficacy, estimand is the randomised long-term effect. It is the method inventory the candidate wants to borrow, and it concedes the candidate's gap in its own words (post-trial intervention data used in 6.2%; switches "usually undocumented"). But it also shows the enabling frame is 113 trials deep and dominated by mortality ascertainment, i.e. the infrastructure exists and has been pointed somewhere else — which supports only the treatment-state limb's novelty, not the discontinuity limb's.
- **vs Holman 2008 (UKPDS-PTM):** same unit, same comparator, same estimand shape, and the answer is given on exactly the candidate's 12-month horizon ("lost after the first year"). It differs in reporting post-trial therapy pooled rather than by arm and in using trial-clinic visits plus questionnaires rather than routine dispensing records, and it has no control series and no dated person-level exposure. So the candidate's distinct residue against this prior is again only limb (i) plus the identification design.

Grounds: two priors, both opened and read, ask the candidate's discontinuity-and-arm-dependence question at the candidate's unit of analysis with the candidate's comparator, on the candidate's 12-month-after-closeout horizon, one of them from routine records. The intake's claim that "the empirical question ... is not asked" does not survive. A narrower candidate confined to limb (i) — the person-level post-trial treatment-state distribution dated to each participant's last visit with a pre-randomisation look-back, against a declared control series — was not matched by any of 11 queries across six vocabularies and could plausibly carry Medium; that is a different question from the one at intake and would need its own Gate 0-2, including the unrun de-implementation sweep.

Significance — consequence:
If post-trial discontinuity is large: a magnitude attaches to Helsinki para 34 / CIOMS 6, which sponsors and ethics committees currently satisfy with a stated provision rather than a measured delivery; HTA bodies acquire an empirical prior for the treatment-waning assumptions that query 8 shows they currently assert; and long-term extension analyses acquire a quantified confounder, since the scoping review concedes switches are "usually undocumented" in 113 extended trials. If near-zero: a standing ethical worry is retired and open-label-extension provision can be deprioritised. The deflation is that for surrogate outcomes the answer is already partly in hand and points one way — SPRINT and UKPDS-PTM both show the trial's own measure reverting within roughly a year — so the decision-relevant increment is concentrated in the treatment-state distribution and in whether the reversion is attributable to closeout rather than to the end of monitoring.
Significance — audience:
Research ethics committees and sponsors (would have a measured rather than assumed denominator for post-trial access obligations); HTA agencies and their modellers (would calibrate waning rather than assume it); trialists designing extensions and open-label extensions (would know what the control arm reverts to); pharmacoepidemiologists holding the registries (would gain a new, dated, person-level exposure). Each is a community that already acts on this object, which is also why the question did not stay silent.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens: does the magnitude of reversion predict the decay of the "legacy effect" across trials? Is reversion larger where the trial drug was not reimbursed at closeout? Does the monitoring effect, separated from the drug effect by the declared control series, have its own measurable size — a quantity the trial-effect literature currently cannot separate? Does arm-dependent reversion bias long-term extension estimates in a signed direction?
Significance — anchoring:
Touches the legacy-effect / post-trial-monitoring anomaly directly (UKPDS-PTM's event benefit persisting while HbA1c converged; SPRINT's mortality benefit not persisting while SBP converged); the HTA treatment-waning assumption; and the long-standing gap between stated and delivered post-trial access.

Reason for silence:
Candidate mechanism — a **custody and ownership boundary**: the sponsor's data custody and the trial's ethics approval both terminate at the last visit, so no party owns the window, and the roster of per-participant last-visit dates never reaches the holders of routine dispensing data. This explains the specific residue Gate 2 found: the question *is* asked whenever a sponsor funds a post-trial monitoring programme and therefore keeps the roster and the arm labels (UKPDS-PTM, DCCT/EDIC, SPRINT extension), and is not asked as a routine, cross-trial, treatment-state measurement where it would require a third party to hold both the roster and the pharmacy records. So the silence is **selective rather than total**, which weakens the mechanism as support for the intake's framing: a disciplinary-boundary mechanism that predicts silence in the places the literature is in fact loud is evidence that the novelty finding was mis-specified, which is consistent with the Low grade rather than an excuse for it. The secondary mechanism, which survives, is a **consent-scope gap**: trial consent language typically does not authorise post-trial linkage, so the treatment-state limb is confined to broad-consent/registry jurisdictions and has no low-cost pilot in most of the world.
