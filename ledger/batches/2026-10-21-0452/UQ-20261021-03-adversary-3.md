## 1. Restatement in my own words

In hospitals where three successive medication-safety checks (a CPOE decision-support alert, a pharmacist's verification action, a bedside barcode scan) each leave an electronic trace on the same order, how correlated are their *misses*? Concretely: given that one check let a labelled error through, how much more likely is the next check to also miss it than its own baseline miss rate; package that as a reliability-engineering common-cause-failure beta factor; and by how much does the real end-to-end escape probability exceed the naive product of per-check miss rates — the arithmetic behind "another layer multiplies the risk down". (V2 adds: restrict per error class to the checks actually capable of seeing that class, bound the never-detected stratum with declared assumptions, and audit whether any published analysis at the site actually multiplies.)

Framing I refuse to inherit from the record: that medicine has "neither the words nor a measurement" for coupling between an automated check and a human check. That is a factual claim about the literature and I tested it.

Single strongest killing point: the record's novelty and silence arguments rest on medicine having neither measured nor named the coupling between an automated check and a human check, yet Lyell et al. 2017 measured exactly that under the name "automation bias" (incorrect CDS increased omission errors by 33.3%, 24.5% and 26.7% relative to no CDS, abstract-level), and a BMJ Qual Saf 2020 conceptual piece already states the common-cause mechanism in the field's own words ("both people may be affected by the same disturbances in the environment") and concludes "full independence cannot be achieved for checks" — leaving only the operational, record-linked, multi-barrier magnitude unasked.
