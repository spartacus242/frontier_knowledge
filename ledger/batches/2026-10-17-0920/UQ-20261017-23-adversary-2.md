## Restatement (my own words, before engaging the record's framing)

Steel mill certificates for a given grade almost always show yield strengths comfortably above the grade's specified minimum, and the lower tail looks clipped. Two things could produce that: the mill deliberately aims its chemistry/rolling above the minimum (production), or heats that came out weak were never called that grade — they were retested, rejected, or re-certified into the grade below (selection). The candidate asks for the split: what fraction of the mean offset and of the lower-tail shape is the grading decision rather than the metal, identified not by a single-threshold density-smoothness argument (which cannot work, since the mass below the minimum is missing by construction) but by a bookkeeping condition — heats missing from below grade G's minimum should show up as extra heats in the upper part of grade G-1 on the same mill and product line, at a rate that is consistent across the several thickness-dependent minima. Then: what are the pre-selection ("latent") 5th percentile and the reliability index beta, compared with the certificate-based values the codes actually use?

Put bluntly: is the extra strength codes credit to steel a property of steel, or of which heats got called that grade — and does correcting for it move beta?

A unimodal lognormal fitted to that inflated-CoV mixture therefore assigns probability below G-1's minimum where the accepted population has approximately none, i.e. it overstates lower-tail mass.
