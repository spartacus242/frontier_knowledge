Question (canonical, Gate 0 as intaken):
For successive shipped context-length increases in a model family, what is the realized marginal value of the last doubling — the share of real production requests whose input exceeds the previous limit, the change in task outcome on just those requests, and the serving and short-context costs incurred to support it?

Question (canonical, Gate 1 REFORMULATION — the version to carry forward; one reformulation used, for well-posedness only):
For >=2 OPEN-WEIGHT model families with >=2 announced context-length increases (so that the family supplying the thresholds also supplies the checkpoints), and for each announced step L_prev -> L_new, measured on a NAMED, DATED, PUBLIC request-length corpus C stratified by workload class (chat, coding agent, document QA, long tool-trace agent): (a) what is the exceedance share s = P(input length > L_prev | class, C) with CIs; (b) on the exceeding stratum only, what is the task-outcome difference between serving at L_new and truncating-or-compressing to L_prev (with a retrieval-augmented truncation baseline); (c) what is the serving cost per request at L_new versus L_prev; and (d) what is the short-context delta between the pre- and post-extension checkpoints of that family on one pinned harness?

WHY THE REFORMULATION (recorded per Gate 1): the intake version's estimand was "the share of real production requests" for a family whose traffic is first-party and non-public, while its proposed instrument was open corpora — an unidentified estimand measured by a proxy, and separately it ran the outcome experiment on open-weight checkpoints while taking thresholds from closed families (a unit/regime mismatch of exactly the kind this ledger has been losing entries to). Pinning the corpus by name and date makes s estimable by anyone and demotes representativeness from an identification problem to a declared external-validity limitation; restricting to open-weight families with >=2 announced increases (e.g. Qwen 32k->128k->1M-class, Llama 4k->8k->128k, Mistral, DeepSeek, Gemma) keeps all four limbs on one object. No other change: the question, its negative answer, and its four limbs are unaltered.

Alternative formulations:

2. Where is the knee in the context-length cost-benefit curve when it is measured against deployed request-length distributions rather than synthetic long-context benchmarks?

Significance — consequence:
If exceedance shares are a few percent and the outcome gain over truncation-plus-retrieval is null while the short-context delta is negative, the answer says the last doubling was realized value-destroying for the measured workloads — which reclassifies announced context length as a positional/marketing variable rather than an engineering one, and tells serving operators that max_model_len can be right-sized below the announced number with a quantified residual risk. If instead exceedance is material in the agent-trace class and the outcome gain is real, the answer relocates the long-context research frontier from million-token documents to hundred-thousand-token tool traces, and makes the doubling an evidenced decision with a measured price. Either direction changes a decision; the no-change case (shares material AND gains real AND costs negligible) is the one this question would retire, which is itself informative.
Argued, not asserted: the mechanism by which it changes behaviour is concrete and does not need a lab's cooperation — vLLM/SGLang deployments set a max length per deployment today with no published basis, and long-context benchmark suites choose their test lengths with no traffic weighting. Both are editable by third parties on this evidence.

Significance — audience:
(1) Third-party serving operators and inference-platform engineers, who would change a specific configured number (the served window and the KV budget behind it) and could act on the exceedance share alone — the opened priors show these teams already instrument per-request input tokens, so the instrument exists where the decision is made. (2) Long-context evaluation designers, who would reweight suites toward the binding regime rather than the headline one; the "effective context" literature (e.g. Paulsen 2025, title-level only) currently measures capability at a length with no traffic weight, so there is a named practice to change. (3) Release decision-makers inside labs, the weakest limb of the three: they hold better data than any public answer would, so a published figure changes their decision only by changing what outsiders can audit — real, but indirect, and this is why significance is scored 2 and not 3.

Significance — fecundity:
Makes askable: where the knee of the length cost-benefit curve sits per workload class; whether exceedance shares are growing fast enough to justify pre-provisioning (a forecasting question the opened traces could answer and do not); whether truncation-plus-retrieval dominates native long context at equal cost on real request tails rather than on benchmarks; what the right denominator for "long-context capability" is when traffic-weighted; and whether the short-context cost of extension recipes, measured per checkpoint pair, correlates with the size of the doubling.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the lost-in-the-middle / effective-versus-announced-length anomaly (announced numbers that capability measurements do not support); the long-context-versus-RAG debate, which is argued on benchmarks and not on traffic tails; the serving-systems finding that TTFT grows primarily with input length (PRIOR 3, Section 3.3), which makes the cost limb non-trivial; and the KV-memory wall that the 27 GB-per-100K-token example in PRIOR 3 illustrates.

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary boundary that coincides exactly with a data boundary, plus an incentive gap. The three limbs are owned by three communities, and NOBODY HOLDS TWO OF THEM AT ONCE. Serving-systems researchers hold per-request input lengths at billion-request scale, but their deliverable is a scheduler or a trace generator, so length is an exogenous input to be modelled, never a decision to be justified — ServeGen fits the distribution and never names a context limit; the CompanyX trace holds the tail and labels it "not shown". Long-context ML researchers own capability-at-length, and their instruments (needle probes, effective-length suites) are deliberately traffic-free, so their designs cannot see a utilisation share. The announced number itself is set by product and release decision-makers, who hold first-party request-length distributions but publish none, and for whom the most likely finding — that real traffic rarely reaches the headline — is an audit of a competitive claim, which is the incentive gap. The structural fact that keeps the join impossible from outside: every public corpus I opened is model-mixed and excludes first-party API traffic, so the party holding the thresholds does not hold the lengths and the parties holding the lengths cannot attribute them to a family's own L_prev. That is a mechanism, not "no one thought of it", and it predicts where the silence will break first — an operator of a single-family deployment who can publish both numbers at once.
