Question (canonical, Gate 1 version — the one that stands):
For a pre-specified list of the drugs most often prescribed to lactating women, (A) what fraction of drugs have published human milk concentration-time data that satisfy each design criterion a veterinary regulator imposes on a milk residue-depletion study used to set a withdrawal period (minimum number of milked subjects, number and spacing of post-dose milk time points, coverage of the terminal depletion phase, assay LOQ relative to the limit, dosing at the licensed regimen); and (B) for the qualifying subset, what breastfeeding-interruption intervals result when the veterinary tolerance-limit rule (an upper one-sided tolerance limit on the depletion curve, e.g. 95/95) is applied against each of a pre-stated SET of candidate infant-exposure limits, and how does the resulting distribution of intervals compare, drug by drug, with the interruption advice currently given to mothers?

Motivating gap:
Veterinary medicine answers a structurally identical decision — how long after dosing is milk unfit for a consumer — with a standardised, statistically explicit, auditable procedure: a residue-depletion study with specified subject and time-point counts, a maximum residue limit (MRL), a tolerance-limit rule applied to the fitted depletion curve, and public calculators. Intake-verified sources: EMA CVMP guideline on determination of withdrawal periods for milk, revision 1, overview of comments (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/comments/overview-comments-received-guideline-determination-withdrawal-periods-milk-ema-cvmp-swp-10941-2022-revision-1_en.pdf); EMA guideline on determination of withdrawal periods for edible tissues, revision 1 (https://ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/guideline-determination-withdrawal-periods-edible-tissues-revision-1_en.pdf); RIVM MILKINGS web-application manual, a public milk-withdrawal calculator (https://www.rivm.nl/sites/default/files/2024-04/Manual%20web%20application%20MILKINGS.pdf). Gate 2 added two further veterinary-side anchors returned by search: the adopted EMA guideline on determination of withdrawal periods for milk, revision 1 (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/adopted-guideline-determination-withdrawal-periods-milk-revision-1_en.pdf) and, notably, an EMA CVMP position paper on establishing MRLs in milk that considers the daily intake of children (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/position-paper-establishment-mrls-milk-considering-daily-intake-children_en.pdf) — i.e. the veterinary side has already reasoned about child milk intake as the sensitive consumer, while the human side has not borrowed the machinery back.
Human lactation advice for the same decision rests on relative-infant-dose rules of thumb, expert consensus, and interruption intervals stated in round hours or in multiples of half-lives. The one mature human precedent for a computed interruption interval is radiopharmaceuticals, where dosimetry-based milk-discard times exist against an explicit infant dose limit (intake flagged this FROM MEMORY; Gate 2 located documents consistent with it but could not open them — see Novelty sources).

Presuppositions:
1. The statistical core of the veterinary procedure (an upper one-sided tolerance limit on a between-subject depletion curve) transfers to a setting with between-subject variation in milk volume, fat content and feeding schedule. PLAUSIBLE, not contested: the veterinary method is itself a between-animal tolerance-limit method, and dairy milk yield varies comparably.
2. CONTESTED AND CENTRAL: an infant-exposure limit analogous to an MRL can be defined. None is agreed; the candidates (relative-infant-dose thresholds of 10%/5%, paediatric reference doses, NOAEL- or ADI-derived margins) are themselves consensus artefacts, and an MRL protects a diluted population intake whereas an infant is the sole consumer. Handled, not solved, by pre-stating a set of candidate limits and reporting the interval under each; the census limb (A) does not depend on it.
3. CONTESTED: published milk concentration-time data are extractable at the required resolution (subject count, sampling density, terminal-phase coverage, assay LOQ) for a non-trivial number of drugs. The census may return near-zero — which is itself the answer and specifies the missing study design.
4. CONTESTED: milk concentration is the right quantity, rather than infant serum concentration, infant systemic exposure after oral bioavailability, or a clinical outcome. Flagged because the veterinary limit protects a consumer population at steady dietary intake while an infant has immature clearance, is the sole consumer, and absorbs only a bioavailable fraction. Gate 2 note: the concePTION/Milk4baby decision tree (Monfort et al. 2025, opened) explicitly moves the endpoint to infant *systemic* exposure weighted by oral bioavailability, so this presupposition is actively contested in the candidate's own field and must be carried as a stated modelling choice, not an assumption.
5. The relevant drug list can be enumerated from prescribing or dispensing data for lactating women. Routine but non-trivial: lactation status is poorly coded in claims data, so the list will need an explicit, auditable derivation (e.g. postpartum dispensing windows in a national registry) or a published reference list.
6. NOT presupposed: that current advice is wrong. The discrepancy distribution, including a finding of broad agreement, is the result.

What would count as an answer:
A pre-registered extraction over a named drug list and named sources (LactMed, Medications & Mothers' Milk, primary milk-PK papers, FDA/EMA lactation-study submissions) reporting, per drug, whether the data satisfy each stated design criterion of the veterinary standard — with the criteria fixed in advance from the EMA CVMP milk-withdrawal guideline and its tolerance-limit rule, not chosen after seeing the data. Then, for the qualifying subset, interruption intervals computed under the tolerance-limit rule against each pre-stated candidate infant-exposure limit, tabulated beside current advice, with the discrepancy distribution (signed, in hours and in doses missed) and a sensitivity analysis over the limits. A "no" answer — essentially no drug has data meeting the standard — is complete and highly actionable, because it names the study design that would fix it, in units a regulator already uses. Not an answer: a paper that advocates the transplant, proposes a framework, or reports a decision tree without executing the census and the computation.

Significance — consequence:
If the census comes back near-zero (the outcome the retrieved per-drug literature makes likely — single-drug studies with small n, sparse sampling and truncated terminal phases dominate), the answer converts a diffuse complaint ("lactation data are poor") into a specific, costed, regulator-legible study specification: n milked subjects, this sampling grid, this terminal-phase coverage, this LOQ — the same specification a veterinary sponsor must already meet, with an existing calculator to run it through. That is directly usable by FDA's lactation-study programme, by EMA, and by the concePTION-style consortia that are currently choosing designs by decision tree rather than against an adequacy threshold. If instead a non-trivial subset qualifies and computed intervals are tabulated, the consequence depends on the discrepancy distribution: where computed intervals exceed current advice, the current advice is under-protective at the stated limit and specific LactMed/ABM entries need revisiting; where they fall short — the more likely direction for most drugs, given the "almost all drugs are compatible" consensus — the finding quantifies, for the first time in the units a regulator uses, how much conservatism is in the round-number advice, which is the argument that would let clinicians stop recommending interruption at all for named drugs. A finding of broad agreement is also consequential: it would show the consensus advice is reconstructible from data, and license its extension to drugs where it has not been stated.

Significance — audience:
Named, institutional, and already acting on this decision daily: (i) LactMed's NIH curators and the authors of Medications & Mothers' Milk, who write the interruption sentences the census would grade, and who have an existing revision process to act through; (ii) the Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine and AAP committees, who issue the round-hour advice; (iii) FDA (lactation-study guidance, already issued — https://fda.gov/media/124749/download) and EMA, who would be handed a design standard with a precedent in their own veterinary divisions, and in EMA's case a sister committee (CVMP) that has already published on children's milk intake; (iv) the concePTION / IMI lactation-PK community (Monfort et al. 2025, opened above), who are choosing designs now and would use an adequacy threshold immediately; (v) nuclear-medicine physicists, who would gain a comparison class for the one modality that already computes intervals; (vi) anaesthesia and oncology services, which are the settings where interruption is actually ordered. Counter-consideration: this audience's prevailing norm is to minimise interruption advice because it ends breastfeeding, so a finding that computed intervals are longer than current advice may be resisted rather than acted on; the claim of significance survives because the census limb is actionable regardless of direction.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: what is the human-milk analogue of an MRL, and who should set it (the question the candidate deliberately refuses to settle, and the one a negative census makes unavoidable)? Should the endpoint be milk concentration, infant systemic exposure after oral bioavailability (the concePTION choice), or an outcome? What does a one-sided tolerance limit mean when the "consumer" is a single infant with developing clearance rather than a diluted population — does the 95/95 rule need replacing with an individual-risk criterion? Can the tolerance-limit rule be run on sparse, pooled literature data via a hierarchical model, and with what loss of coverage? Does the same audit transfer to the other human secretion decisions (semen, sweat, dialysate) and to occupational re-entry intervals? And, reflexively: for which veterinary drugs is the human-side evidence actually better than the animal-side evidence — a comparison nobody has had the units to make.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the recognised and repeatedly stated open problem of the lactation evidence gap (the "therapeutic orphan" status of breastfeeding women), to which it offers a measurement instrument rather than another lament. Touches the regulatory asymmetry anomaly that a cow's milk withdrawal interval must be justified by a specified study and a statistical rule while a human mother's is given in round hours by consensus — an anomaly that is reported in passing but, on this search, never posed as a question. Touches model-informed drug development's extension to lactation (the per-drug PBPK cluster) by supplying the data-adequacy denominator those models are being fitted to. Touches the nuclear-medicine/pharmacology boundary, where the same clinical decision is made by two incompatible standards of proof inside one hospital.

Reason for silence:
Primarily a disciplinary and institutional boundary, with a reinforcing incentive asymmetry. The withdrawal-period machinery lives in veterinary regulatory science (EMA CVMP, FDA CVM, WOAH, national residue laboratories), publishes in veterinary and food-safety venues, and is taught as residue chemistry; human lactation pharmacotherapy lives in clinical pharmacology, paediatrics and lactation medicine, publishes in CPT/Clin Pharmacokinet/Breastfeeding Medicine, and is taught as risk-benefit counselling. The two share no committee, no journal, no reviewer pool, and — decisively — no legal driver: the veterinary calculation exists because a *consumer* residue limit is statutory and a food business is liable, whereas no statute sets a residue limit for human milk and no one is liable for an infant's intake, so no sponsor was ever required to produce a depletion study to a specified design. The incentive runs the other way too: the field's dominant and well-founded concern is that interruption advice ends breastfeeding, so its reflex is to shorten or eliminate interruption rather than to formalise it, and a quantified "withdrawal period" for human milk reads as a step toward more interruption, not less. A borrowed vocabulary that likens nursing infants to dairy consumers is also plainly unattractive to write, which plausibly suppressed even the analogy. Finally the enabling conditions are recent: digital extraction of milk concentration-time data at scale, public withdrawal-period calculators (RIVM MILKINGS, 2024), and explicit human lactation-study design guidance (FDA) all post-date the consensus texts that the current advice rests on.
