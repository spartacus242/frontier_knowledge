## 1. Restatement in my own words

Inside one person's body, the two sides of a paired organ (breast, lung, kidney, testis, ovary) hold the same genotype, the same age, the same bloodstream and the same lifetime of smoke, hormones and diet. They do not hold the same amount of tissue. The question: if you measure how many at-risk epithelial cells each side actually carries, does that side's cancer hazard rise in exact one-for-one proportion to that count — the exponent beta in hazard ∝ (cells)^beta equal to 1, as an independent-cell-of-origin picture requires — or is there a leftover "which side of the body you are on" effect on carcinogenesis once the cell count is taken out? Three pre-declared outcomes: beta = 1 with no residual (laterality is just bookkeeping); a surviving signed side term (an unexplained left-right asymmetry in human carcinogenesis); beta well below 1 in several organs at once (sublinearity, i.e. clonal competition or field constraint, bearing on Peto's paradox inside one body).

Gate 1's one permitted repair is in force and I read the question as repaired: among **unilateral** incident cancers in cohorts with pre-diagnostic per-side volumetry plus a measured per-side epithelial cell density, a case-only conditional likelihood for *which side* got the cancer, P(left) as a function of beta·log(C_L/C_R) plus a side intercept, fitted per organ and tested for homogeneity across organs.
