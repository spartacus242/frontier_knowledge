## 1. Restatement in my own words

Take a tissue made of many unrelated cells, sequence it so that each individual DNA strand is read separately and both its sequence and its 5mC marks are read off *that same strand*. Pick out the strands that carry a somatic point mutation. Now ignore the mutated base itself, and ignore any CpG that the mutation invented or wiped out, and look only at the *other* CpGs nearby on the same strand. Ask: is that strand's leftover methylation level different from the methylation level of other strands covering the same stretch of genome, from the same sample, same strand orientation, that have no mutation there? The answer wanted is a matched odds ratio (each mutant strand compared against its own locus-and-sample-matched mutation-free neighbours), computed separately for C>T-at-CpG, other C>T, and non-C>T mutations, with one mutation class declared in advance as a class where no effect is expected, and with a stated model for how often a per-strand 5mC call is simply wrong.

Two things the question is *not*: it is not asking whether methylated regions mutate more (that is the old regional question), and it is not asking about the mark on the base that mutated (that is arithmetic, not measurement). It is asking whether the *carrier molecule* looks epigenetically different in its surviving neighbourhood.

## 3. What I established from sources I opened

**(A) The candidate's question is named in print as an aspiration and left unexecuted.** epialleleR's introduction says the framework "allows to connect DNA methylation status with potential underlying cis-factors, such as single-nucleotide variation" — and the paper runs no such analysis: its "variant epiallele frequency" is defined from methylation thresholds and read counts, not from sequence variants ("Variant epiallele frequency calling was performed using epialleleR::generateCytosineReport"), its statistics are "t-test with Holm adjustment", and it contains no mention of excluding mutated bases or flanking CpG windows and no odds ratio or logistic regression. This is the *same pattern* fact (6) records for Akbari et al. (joint genome-and-epigenome measurement in the introduction only, no methylation analysis). Two independent read-level-methylation tool papers announce the conditioning and never run it.

**(B) Per-read 5mC accuracy is measured in the field, but only coarsely, and the benchmark authors say per-read recovery is the weak point.** Nat Commun 12:3438: all tools reached "areas under the precision–recall (PR) curve AUCPR above 0.8" per read; "Megalodon showed the highest AUC and AUCPR values, followed by DeepSignal and Nanopolish"; "Guppy had decreased precision at high recall values"; "As DeepMod did not provide any information for individual reads, we could not use it for the per-read benchmarking analyses"; and, against the candidate, "Although nanopore provides an opportunity to detect methylation at the single-molecule level, its accuracy in this context remains largely unknown" and "there is still a major limitation in the capacity to correctly recover the methylation levels from individual reads". The accepted-version PDF confirms the actual numbers live in Fig. 3 and are not stated in text; the only numeric per-read threshold given anywhere is Nanopolish's default log-likelihood ratio of ±2.5.

**(C) On the conversion-efficiency attack already on the table — it is not sourced, and the paper's own text points the other way.** From the MethylSaferSeqS preprint: "LM" is defined as the library "from original molecules for assessment of DNA methylation"; the 77% figure appears as "with average conversion efficiency of 77% (IQR: 75% to 79%)"; and the authors immediately say "This conversion efficiency was adequate for the regions of interest to us", with "Greater (>98%) conversion of the unmethylated C's could be achieved by exposure to bisulfite at 50°C for 16 hours". At the two loci actually measured, observed apparent methylation at a non-CpG cytosine is 1.3% (PIK3CA wild-type CAT trimer) and 3.1% (TP53 mutant CCA trimer), both attributed by the authors to "incomplete bisulfite conversion". **1.3–3.1% is not ~23%.** So the claim "a 77% library average means roughly a quarter of unmethylated cytosines read as methylated" does not hold in the regime the candidate occupies (targeted amplicons chosen for adequate conversion); the figure is a cross-library average over all contexts, and the IQR is across libraries. I therefore do **not** carry that attack.
Two residues of it do survive, sourced: (i) MethylSaferSeqS performs **no per-read conversion QC** — conversion efficiency "is estimated from unmethylated bases but is not used to discard reads"; the only read-level filter in the paper is a count threshold ("Only regions with more than 10 reads are included in Table S7"); and (ii) its methylation estimand is region-aggregated and background-corrected, not per-molecule: "Fractional methylation was calculated for each marker as the ratio of the total number of unconverted cytosines in CpG sites to the total number of CpG sites", then adjusted "to remove the non-converted background and to correct for run-to-run differences in conversion efficiency". So on the one instrument demonstrated to co-measure, the per-molecule error rate the candidate requires does not exist, and the standard per-read fix (conversion QC on non-CpG cytosines) was available and not used.

**(D) My own reasoning, labelled as mine, on whether conversion deficit biases or only attenuates.** In the MethylSaferSeqS design the mutation call is made on the *copies* (unconverted) and the methylation call on the *originals* (bisulfite-treated), joined by UID — so conversion failure cannot alter a molecule's arm assignment. Error in the exposure is therefore non-differential with respect to the outcome, and within a locus-and-sample-matched stratum it contaminates both arms alike. For a **binary** exposure, non-differential misclassification is guaranteed to attenuate toward the null; **that guarantee does not extend to a graded exposure** such as window-level 5mC density, where error whose magnitude depends on the true value (an unconverted molecule reads as near-fully methylated) can in principle bias either way. I did not find and do not cite a source covering the graded case, so I claim only the weaker conclusion: the conversion deficit is far more likely to cost power than to manufacture an association, and it does not by itself defeat the estimand. Anyone who asserts either direction as settled theory is overreaching — including me.
