Domain(s): STEM — computer-assisted mathematics and formal methods; computer-architecture reliability (silent data corruption, soft errors). Source method: silence mapping.

Question (canonical):
- (V0, intake) Given the silent-data-corruption rates now measured in production CPU and GPU fleets, what is the probability that a published computer-assisted mathematical result is wrong for hardware rather than logical reasons, and what fraction of faults injected into a formally verified proof checker make it report 'verified' on an invalid certificate instead of crashing or rejecting?
- (V1, Gate 1 reformulation, well-posedness only — ADOPTED) Over a defined frame of published computer-assisted mathematical results, (i) what interval on the hardware-fault exposure of each result follows from its reported core-hours, hardware and re-check history combined with a stated fleet SDC rate carried at its own order-of-magnitude uncertainty; and (ii) under a pre-registered fault model, what is the fail-silent fraction of a formally verified certificate checker — the share of injected faults under which it outputs 'verified' where no valid derivation exists — against the shares that crash or correctly reject?
  Reason for reformulation: V0's first limb says "probability that a result is wrong", but the candidate's own presupposition list concedes that hardware-caused and logical wrongness are "probably not distinguishable in the historical record", and its answer criteria ask for an exposure interval rather than a probability of wrongness. V1 states the limb as the quantity the stated deliverable actually produces. Both versions recorded; the reformulation does not rescue novelty.

Alternative formulations:
1. Does re-checking a certificate with a small verified checker reduce a computer-assisted proof's hardware-fault exposure, or does it concentrate the entire exposure into one long, unreplicated run?
2. The trusted base of a machine-checked theorem is argued down to the logic of its kernel; what does that base contain once the executing machine is admitted to have a measured, core-local, reproducible corruption rate?

Motivating gap (as intaken, and as it stands after Gate 2):
As intaken: the literature on trusting machine-checked mathematics is dense with how-answers (minimal kernels, the de Bruijn criterion, independently implemented checkers, verified certificate checkers) and near-silent on the machine those checkers execute on; meanwhile the architecture community has moved from modelling soft errors to measuring them in production fleets and reports corruptions that are frequently core-local, data-dependent and reproducible — the regime in which "run it again" and "check the certificate" do not help — and has asked the vulnerability question for one load-bearing domain after another without ever asking it of machine-checked proofs.
After Gate 2: the mapped silence is REAL for the formal-methods how-literature and FALSE for the candidate's field taken as a whole. The question is a named, repeatedly asked and twice quantitatively estimated question in the mathematics-of-trust literature: Lam (1991) computes it for one published non-existence result from a measured machine error rate, and Hales (2013) computes it from FIT rates for long computations and states explicitly that the exposure is larger inside a proof assistant. The silence mapping surveyed the kernel/TCB literature and missed the literature where the question already lives. What survives the gap claim is narrower and is stated under "Suggested narrowing" below.

Presuppositions (established / contested, with Gate 2 updates):
1. Published fleet SDC rates apply to the machines these computations ran on. CONTESTED, unchanged — rates come from particular hyperscaler fleets, silicon generations and ages; the two main rate families differ by orders of magnitude; consumer and academic-HPC rates are not established.
2. Corruption is often core-local and reproducible rather than independent-transient. Reported by fleet studies; SHARE contested and load-bearing. UPDATE: this is the one presupposition that genuinely post-dates the priors — Lam and Hales both reason inside the independent-transient model, and Lam's remedy (re-run the affected cases) and Hales' reassurance (the E8 calculation "has now been repeated about 5 times with identical results") are both invalidated in the reproducible regime. This is the candidate's only real conceptual increment over the priors.
3. A verified checker's correctness theorem is conditional on a machine model that SDC violates. Established by inspection. UPDATE: also PARTLY SAID ALOUD already — Hales §3.4: "every formal verification project is a verification of an abstract model" of the code and language; FOM 2000 (Bauer) lists CPU design bugs and cosmic-ray malfunction among the things trust in a computer-verified theorem rests on. The candidate's claim that this "is rarely said aloud" does not survive.
4. Fault injection is a valid proxy for field corruption. CONTESTED, unchanged, and now the sharpest part of the candidate: the mapping from measured field corruptions to an injection model is exactly what is unsettled, so limb (ii) is conditional on the chosen model and needs the sensitivity analysis the answer criteria specify.
5. The core-hours, hardware and replication history of published computer-assisted results are recoverable. Partly established; UPDATE: Hales demonstrates recoverability for a handful of named results (E8: 77 h, 64 GB; projective plane of order 10: never independently verified; four-colour theorem: second-generation proof plus formal verification), so the census is feasible but has only ever been done anecdotally.
6. "Wrong because of a hardware fault" is distinguishable from a logical or software error. Distinguishable inside the injection experiment; CONTESTED in the historical record. UPDATE: Lam separates them in practice — two independently written programs cross-checking selected cases for the software branch, a measured CRAY-1A error rate for the hardware branch — so the separation is not novel either, though his is a design-time separation, not a retrospective one.

What would count as an answer:
Two measurements reported together. (i) A census over a defined frame of published computer-assisted results recording, per result, reported core-hours, hardware, and whether the certificate check was ever repeated on independent hardware or a different architecture, combined with a stated fleet SDC rate to give an exposure interval carrying the rate's own order-of-magnitude uncertainty rather than a point value. (ii) A fault-injection campaign against one or more verified certificate checkers under a pre-registered fault model, reporting the fail-silent fraction (checker outputs 'verified' where no valid derivation exists) against the fractions that crash or correctly reject, with the fault model's influence shown by sensitivity analysis. A fail-silent fraction effectively zero, because corruption almost always derails control flow, settles the question in the reassuring direction and is equally admissible.

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, all opened and read):

PRIOR 1 — Hales, "Mathematics in the Age of the Turing Machine", arXiv:1302.2898 (2013; in Turing's Legacy, LMS Lecture Notes). READ, §3.5 "Soft errors".
- Unit of analysis: a single long-running computation or proof-assistant run (worked example: the E8 character table, 77 hours, 64 GB RAM).
- Endpoint: expected number of soft errors during the run, and whether the resulting mathematical claim is trustworthy.
- Reference / comparator: software bugs ("In software that has been thoroughly debugged, soft errors become the most significant source of error in computation"); and the same hardware at different elevations (Denver ~3x Boston).
- Estimand: E[N_soft errors] = FIT rate (10^3 FIT/Mbit) x memory x wall time, giving ~40 memory soft errors for the E8 run; plus the explicit claim that "the probability of a soft error during a calculation inside a proof assistant can be much higher" because soft errors scale with execution time. Conclusion quoted: "One rogue alpha particle brings all my schemes of perfection to nought."
- Also contains, anecdotally, the candidate's census variables: E8 "has now been repeated about 5 times with identical results"; the projective plane of order 10 result "has never received independent verification", with soft errors cited as a reason Lam will not call it a proof; the four-colour theorem has a second-generation proof plus a formal verification. §3.4: "every formal verification project is a verification of an abstract model" of the code and language.

PRIOR 2 — Lam, "The Search for a Finite Projective Plane of Order 10", Amer. Math. Monthly 98 (1991) 305-318; reliability section read at the CECM mirror. READ (partly: two displayed probability expressions are images in the source and did not render as text).
- Unit of analysis: one published computer-assisted non-existence result; internally, the ~500,000 non-isomorphic A2 configurations of its search tree.
- Endpoint: probability that the published conclusion (no projective plane of order 10 exists) is wrong because undetected hardware errors silently deleted the branch containing a plane.
- Reference / comparator: software error, handled separately and explicitly — two differently written programs cross-checking selected cases, plus internal consistency checks during isomorphism testing.
- Estimand: P(a plane existed AND each of its >=2 non-isomorphic A2's was hit by an undetected hardware error), computed from a measured CRAY-1A error rate of about one per thousand hours, which implies "two to three errors" over the computation; one error was in fact caught by chance and the affected cases re-run. He calls the compound probability "infinitesimal", declines to call the result a proof ("only an experimental result"), and says it "desperately needs an independent verification".

PRIOR 3 — FOM (Foundations of Mathematics) list thread "Computer-verified theorems", November 2000, cs.nyu.edu/pipermail/fom/2000-November/004532.html (and /004537.html). READ.
- Unit of analysis: a computer-verified theorem / a proof-checking run.
- Endpoint: whether such a theorem should be believed, and which error source binds that belief.
- Reference / comparator: the explicit comparator is repetition — "running programs on 100 different machines". Error sources compared: design errors (Pentium FDIV cited) vs random transient errors (bad disk sectors, corrupted network transfers, cosmic rays).
- Estimand: none numeric; a qualitative ranking, and a disagreement left unresolved. Bauer: corruption is "extremely uncommon", cosmic-ray malfunction "presumably is exceedingly rare" and undetectable in practice, random hardware errors are "a non-issue", and "running programs on 100 different machines independently does not help against design errors". Kanovei: a few runs on different computers suffice. Shipman: for a randomized primality test, machine error is a likelier explanation of a positive than the test being wrong, "even if you ran the calculation on 100 different independent machines".

Significance — consequence: If the fail-silent fraction is materially above zero, then the de Bruijn criterion delivers a guarantee conditional on a machine model that the field has never audited, and the correct hygiene for a 100 TB-scale certificate is re-checking on a different microarchitecture rather than a yet smaller kernel — a reversal of where the field spends effort. If it is effectively zero because corruption derails control flow, a worry standing since Lam 1991 retires with evidence instead of with assertion, and the FOM position that random hardware error is "a non-issue" acquires the support it has never had. Against this: Hales made essentially this argument in 2013 to a wide mathematical audience and the practice did not move, which is direct evidence about how much the answer would change.
Significance — audience: proof-assistant kernel and SAT/SMT certificate-checker maintainers (the verified-checker ecosystem: ACL2 and CakeML-based DRAT/LPR checkers, Lean/mathlib, HOL Light), the computational-mathematics groups publishing terabyte-certificate results, and the HPC centres running the checks. Real and identifiable — the existence of verified checkers is itself evidence that this community acts on trust arguments — but their revealed response to the same argument from Hales was no change in practice.
Significance — fecundity: whether architecture-diverse re-checking should become a reporting norm; whether certificate formats should carry independently checkable per-chunk digests so a check can be sharded across heterogeneous machines; whether SDC-hardened checker designs beat plain duplication on cost; and what a correctness theorem conditional on a corrupting machine model would even state.
Significance — anchoring: the de Bruijn criterion and TCB enumerations (CompCert TCB paper arXiv:2201.10280; seL4's admitted hardware assumptions); the 2021-2026 fleet-SDC literature; Lam's standing call for independent verification of the order-10 result; the ledger's own UQ-20261016-01 on SDC in LLM training.
