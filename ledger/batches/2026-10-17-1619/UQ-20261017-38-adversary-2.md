## 1. Restatement (my words, not the record's)

Take a fixed list of published clinical risk scores that each ship with a published "act above this number" threshold. For each score, collect at least three implementations written independently of one another — calculator websites, specialty-society apps, open-source packages, journal-appendix code. Before running anything, decide by a written rubric which input combinations the original publication actually pins down to one score, and which it leaves open. Then feed every implementation the same pre-specified set of synthetic patients, drawn by a rule fixed in advance that deliberately over-weights boundary values, unit edge cases and missing predictors. Report three numbers, separately for the pinned-down and the open regions: (i) how often any two implementations differ numerically at all; (ii) how often they differ enough that one implementation says "act" and another says "don't"; and (iii) inside the pinned-down region only, what share of those act/don't flips two independent raters agree to call a coding bug rather than a hole in the publication, with an agreement statistic.

In one line: *not* "do different risk models disagree" (that is the family the intake itself excluded) but "is one published score a single function, and does which copy of it you open decide the treatment decision."

## 3. Spot-check of the record's cited priors

**P1 — Rahaman, Samuel, Neamtiu, "Diagnosing Medical Score Calculator Apps", Proc. ACM IMWUT 7(3), Article 118, Sept 2023. REAL, opened in full, and the record's characterisation is accurate on the points that carry the kill, with two corrections.**

Verified as the record states: the oracle is the published reference table, not other apps — the app's score is checked *"against the reference score for that parameter combination"* (§3.3.5), and the paper's only comparison step is *"we compare the two scores: the app-calculated score and the expected correct score"* (§3.3.5). It never compares apps to each other and reports **no inter-app disagreement rate**. It reports **defect counts only** (23 coverage, 32 non-overlap, 16 calculation errors) with detection metrics against hand-labelled ground truth, and **declares no input distribution** — partition checks are solver-based over full ranges and GUI exploration is depth-first, not sampled. On the threshold limb, §5.3: *"certain errors 'straddle' the threshold, which, as discussed previously, can put the patient in a different class."* Table 4 carries a "Meets or Exceeds Threshold" column, but **no quantitative threshold-crossing rate is given** and the paper does not define the column's Y/N. None of "differential testing", "N-version programming" or "metamorphic testing" appears anywhere in the paper.

**Correction 1 to the record (line 21 / line 49).** The record says *"No inter-rater statistic is reported there, which is the candidate's only surviving methodological increment on this limb."* That is inaccurate as stated. P1 **does** report a multiple-rater agreement figure: *"The observed multiple-rater agreement across H1, H2, and H3's findings was 100%."* (§4.3, repeated for app findings in §5.4.1). It is not chance-corrected and it is not reported for the three error-source categories specifically. So the candidate's surviving increment on this limb shrinks from "an inter-rater statistic" to "a chance-corrected statistic on the error-source adjudication specifically" — a smaller increment than the record credits it with, which cuts **against** the candidate.

**Correction 2 to the record (lines 49, 74, 89).** The record repeatedly says P1 "states that EHR implementations ... are not evaluated" and that the EHR limb is "excluded in P1's limitations". The limitations section (§6.5) says nothing about EHR implementations; its only stated limitation there is *"DroidBot failed to produce proper JSON GUI data in 5 cases when apps used WebView,"* and §6.6.1 says *"While this paper focused solely on Android apps."* Web calculators appear in §6.3 as observed examples, not as a named limitation. So the routing residual's provenance is wrong: the EHR slice is **not** named as a gap by P1; it is simply outside P1's scope. This does not affect the kill, but the residual should be re-sourced before it is routed.

**P2 — Bierbrier, Lo, Wu, *J Med Internet Res* 2014;16(2):e32.** Not re-opened by me (budget); the record opened it in full and its numbers (14 apps × 13 functions × 10 cases = 1240 outputs, 98.6% accuracy, 17 errors, Child-Pugh errors not changing class) are internally consistent and consistent with how D2/D4's neighbours cite that literature. ## 4. The new prior my Disguise sweep found — the strongest single killing point

**Seiberth S, Terstegen T, Strobach D, et al. "Accuracy of freely available online GFR calculators using the CKD-EPI equation." *European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology* 2020;76:1465–1470. OPENED AT THE PUBLISHER on the first call (no PMC route attempted).**

This is a **fourth** prior, in a **fourth register** — clinical pharmacology / nephrology, "online GFR calculators" — that neither the intake's clinical-epidemiology register nor Gate 2's mHealth-safety / software-verification register surfaced. On the dimensions that matter it is **closer to the candidate's V1 design than either graded prior**:

- **Unit of analysis:** (online calculator, reference case) for **one published equation** — CKD-EPI. 49 calculators identified, 2 produced no result, **47 evaluated**. The candidate asks for I ≥ 3 independent implementations per score; this is I = 47.
- **Input sample, declared in advance:** *five hypothetical reference cases* varying age, sex, serum creatinine and ethnicity, each run **in both creatinine units where available** — i.e. the awkward regions the candidate says a grid must include (unit edge cases, the ethnicity switch) are exactly what was deliberately sampled.
- **Oracle — and this is the point:** the reference was built by **two authors independently entering the published CKD-EPI equations into two separate Excel sheets and comparing the results.** That is the candidate's own N-version differential-testing construction, executed in 2020, and used as the specification oracle.
- **Estimand and result:** *"Only 36 of 47 calculators (76.6%) produced accurate eGFR results for all reference cases."* *"Eight of 47 (17.0%) calculators were considered as faulty."* *"Overall, 28 errors were found (range 59 to 147% of the correct eGFR value)."*
- **Error-source adjudication, i.e. the candidate's limb (iii):** errors are classified by cause — ethnicity handling, eGFR unit conversion, creatinine unit conversion, and unexplained — and small deviations are set aside as rounding (*"Otherwise, rounding inaccuracies were assumed."*).
- **Specification under-determination, i.e. the candidate's spec-undetermined region:** named explicitly for CKD-EPI — the choice of BSA formula (Mosteller vs Du Bois, each calculator matched to its own choice), which equation variant to select by sex/ethnicity/creatinine category versus a single equation, the absence of an upper age limit (*"There appears to be no general recommendation on an upper age limit."*), and creatinine assay method beyond standardisation. That is a per-score under-specification audit, the limb the intake named as the candidate's most distinctive contribution.

**What Seiberth does NOT do, and this is the candidate's entire remaining content:** it reports no threshold-crossing rate in its own data. It says only that the error magnitudes *"could lead to erroneous staging of CKD and inadequate drug dosing"* and gives a metformin example where an erroneous conversion *"could lead to quite different clinical decisions in therapy with metformin."* No CKD stage change is reported as observed, and no agreement statistic is given for the error classification.

So after four opened priors across four registers, the candidate's surviving increment is: **report limb (ii) as a rate, and put a chance-corrected statistic on limb (iii).** Everything else — many independent implementations of one published equation, a declared awkward-region input set, an N-version-built specification oracle, an error-source taxonomy separating specification latitude from coding defect, a per-score under-determination audit — has been published, more than once, in registers that do not talk to each other.

I note the arithmetic honestly: Seiberth's 17.0% is a **share of faulty calculators** on five cases, Bierbrier's 1.4% is a **share of wrong outputs** on 1240 runs, and P1's figures are **defect counts** with no denominator over an input distribution. These are three different quantities and I do not combine them. That non-commensurability is itself a finding (see attack 5).
