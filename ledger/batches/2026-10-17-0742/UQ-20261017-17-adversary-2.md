## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

When an inpatient's 0–10 pain score falls over a day or two, part of the fall might not be the pain changing at all but the patient's internal yardstick moving — what they would have called a 7 on admission they would now call a 4, because the episode has taught them what severe pain in this body feels like. The candidate asks: (a) is there such a drift, detectable as a change in the number reported at a fixed *externally anchored* level of pain over the episode; (b) what share of the average observed NRS fall is that drift rather than anchored pain change; and (c) how often does the drift make us wrongly declare "pain controlled" at the NRS ≤ 4 titration target or "clinically meaningful improvement" at the 2-point / 30% MCID. The study it imagines is a within-episode then-test with randomised timing plus a pre-registered anchor set (PCA demands / opioid consumption, QST, a functional milestone), with the recalibration estimate required to survive an anchor-only refit.

Two things I note immediately as the shape of the candidate: limb (a) is a *sign* question, limbs (b) and (c) are *magnitude* questions, and the whole thing is explicitly anchor-relative. Those come apart under attack.

### What the opened sources actually say (scoped, not overstated)

- **Watkinson et al., *Pain* 2013;154(5):743–749, doi 10.1016/j.pain.2013.01.016** — PARTIAL READ (Warwick repository record; full text "not available from this repository"; publisher route not attempted — fetch cap reached). Design per the record: three blocks of 11 pressure pain stimuli each **rated on a 0–10 intensity scale**, with the *distribution* of stimuli varied between participants; Study 1 tested whether a given pressure is rated higher when it is the most intense in its block, Study 2 whether ratings rise when most stimuli in a block are intense with the mean held constant. Conclusion: "Pain ratings are relative to the surrounding context", well described by range frequency theory. **Scope limits I must respect:** the record does not state whether participants were patients or healthy volunteers (laboratory delivery of calibrated pressure makes volunteers likely, but that is my inference); it is within a single session; it contains no clinical episode, no share of observed change, and no decision threshold.
- **van Dijk et al., *Int J Nurs Stud* 2016;53:260–269** (abstract, opened) — grounded theory, 27 patients interviewed one day after surgery. Patients set a preliminary score internally from "score-related" and "intrapersonal" factors, then **weigh anticipated consequences — analgesic administration, wanted or unwanted, and possible judgements by professionals — before reporting it**. All interviews at one time point; no statement about change over the admission.
- **Bosdet et al., *Front Pain Res* 2021;2** (abstract, opened) — 45 inpatients, thematic analysis; patients anchor pain in physical properties, tolerability and function, and their relationship to analgesia, coping and staff experiences shape scale use; they ground pain in "semantically similar but idiosyncratic ways". The scale is "frequently used to request pain medication". Nothing on change across the admission.

**Provisional** — nothing is Unresolved, so no Rejection follows, but Disguise, Triviality, Presupposition failure, Vagueness, Significance and Reason-for-silence all come back Partial, novelty should drop to the floor of Medium (a second close neighbour, Watkinson 2013 in *Pain*, was missed by Gate 2), well-posedness is 2/3, and one on-point lead ("Anticipated adaptation or scale recalibration?", PMC4016565) is COULD NOT READ; promotion requires that paper read by publisher route, Watkinson 2013 read in full to fix its participant population, limb (b) re-specified to identify recalibration against strategic reporting, and limb (a) either dropped or narrowed.

## Single strongest killing point

Range frequency theory has already been fitted to the identical 0–10 pain rating response in the candidate's own flagship journal (Watkinson, Wood, Lloyd & Brown, *Pain* 2013;154(5):743–749 — "Pain ratings are relative to the surrounding context", three blocks of 11 pressure stimuli with the stimulus distribution varied between participants, per the Warwick record I opened; PARTIAL READ, full text not obtained). That single miss does triple damage: it largely pre-answers limb (a), it makes the record's "scale stability never questioned" claim false, and together with van Dijk 2016's finding that patients choose the number they report by weighing whether they want the opioid, it falsifies the taboo mechanism offered for the silence — leaving the candidate resting on limb (c) and on an anchor-relative residual in limb (b) that its own design cannot separate from strategic reporting.
