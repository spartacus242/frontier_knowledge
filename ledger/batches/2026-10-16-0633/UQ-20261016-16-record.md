Question (canonical, Gate 0 as intaked):
Conditioned on matched documented infection (same syndrome or pathogen, same age band, peak taken before any antipyretic), has the peak body temperature attained fallen across eras by more than the secular fall in afebrile baseline temperature — i.e. has the amplitude of the febrile response itself attenuated, or has only the baseline moved?

Question (canonical, Gate 1 REFORMULATION — the version to carry forward; one reformulation used, for well-posedness only):
In a multi-site EHR corpus spanning roughly 1995–2025, restricted to episodes of a documented infection (coded syndrome plus culture- or NAAT-confirmed pathogen) and taking as the episode's peak the maximum temperature recorded strictly before the first antipyretic administration timestamp, with the contemporaneous reference being the afebrile temperature distribution of non-infected patients measured in the same health system, same device class and same time-of-day window: within strata of age band, syndrome, pathogen, severity-at-presentation and device class, is the calendar-year slope of (episode peak − contemporaneous afebrile reference) equal to zero, and how does that slope compare with the calendar-year slope of the afebrile reference itself? The additive scale is pre-specified, with a mandatory ceiling-aware (bounded or multiplicative) sensitivity analysis; any archival (pre-1950) arm is reported as a bias-bounded estimate with cross-era thermometric bias carried as a term with uncertainty, never as a point estimate.
Why the reformulation: the intaked version (a) presupposes the secular baseline fall as an input (P6) when the quantity of interest is a contrast of two slopes estimable inside one dataset, (b) leaves "amplitude" scale-free (P5), and (c) leaves "era" undefined. The reformulation makes the primary arm instrument-homogeneous and the estimand scale-explicit, and it survives P6 being false — which matters, because Gate 2 found P6 is more contested than intake granted.

Alternative formulations:
- Is the fever response amplitude (peak minus that era's own afebrile baseline) in documented infection constant across birth cohorts, or has it declined?
- Of the difference between historical and modern fever observations, how much is explained by a shifted baseline and how much by a smaller rise per unit of infectious stimulus?
- (added at Gate 1) Has the acute-phase temperature response per unit of measured inflammatory stimulus (°C per log CRP, or per log pathogen load) changed by calendar year within matched syndrome and device class?

Motivating gap:
ANCHOR ANOMALY: mean US body temperature has fallen about 0.03 °C per birth decade since the 1890s (Protsiv et al., eLife 2020;9:e49555, as reported and cited by Wright & Auwaerter, Open Forum Infect Dis 2020;7(5):ofaa132, PMC7237822). THE ASKED LIMB: that review's Conclusions assert, with no data presented, that "Sensitivity for the detection of illness may be inappropriately lowered if 38.0°C and 38.3°C continue to be the standards." So "should the fever threshold be lowered because baseline fell" is in print as an unevidenced conjecture (asked-but-open; routes to routed-open-problems.md, not here), and the review explicitly does not claim the febrile mechanism changed. The un-asked quantity is the decomposition Δpeak = Δbaseline + Δamplitude. Nobody appears to have reported the peak attained in matched documented infection by era or calendar year, which is the only measurement separating "the ruler moved" (threshold now further from the mean in SD units: specificity up, sensitivity down — fix is re-referencing) from "the response weakened" (different physiology, a different fix, and a signal about secular change in innate immune activation). Note that under the second limb re-referencing the threshold does NOT restore sensitivity, which is what makes the decomposition decision-relevant rather than academic.

Presuppositions:
P1 (partly contested): historical series recording temperature in documented infection with an identifiable denominator exist in usable numbers (pre-antibiotic pneumonia, typhoid, malaria charts; military and hospital series). Gate 2 query 10 confirmed the raw material exists as digitised 19th/early-20th-century clinical lectures and case reports in PMC and NLM collections, but found no series with a stated denominator; availability and selection remain unknown without a scoping pass.
P2 (CONTESTED, central risk for the archival arm only): cross-era thermometry is comparable after calibration, site and dwell-time correction. Long-dwell mercury oral/axillary versus modern electronic oral, tympanic and temporal-artery devices carry site-specific offsets of the same order as the effect sought. Gate 2 query 7 found the device-comparison literature that supplies those offset magnitudes but no application of it across eras. The Gate 1 reformulation confines the primary arm to one instrument era, so P2 is not load-bearing for the feasible test.
P3 (CONTESTED): infection cohorts are matchable across eras on syndrome, severity at presentation and age; admission thresholds, survivorship and antibiotic truncation of the fever curve all differ.
P4 (contested in practice): the pre-antipyretic peak is identifiable; EHR drug-administration timestamps exist but are imperfect (ward-stock paracetamol, pre-hospital dosing), and historical charts may not record antipyretic use at all.
P5 (CONTESTED, physiological): "response amplitude" is well defined independently of baseline. This assumes an additive set-point shift; the response could be multiplicative or ceiling-limited (a hard upper bound near 41–42 °C compresses amplitude differences at the top of the range), in which case the decomposition means nothing until the scale is specified. The Gate 1 reformulation pre-specifies the scale and requires a bounded-scale sensitivity analysis; this is a repair of statement, not of physiology.
P6 (CONTESTED — downgraded at Gate 2, intake had accepted it): that the secular baseline fall is real and not an instrument artefact. Intake accepted it on Protsiv's within-cohort design. Jones (Science-Based Medicine, 2020), opened and read, disputes it directly on measurement-site ambiguity in the Union Army cohort, early-thermometer imprecision, unknown time-of-day and ambient temperature, and cohort selection, and states: "The consensus conclusion from these papers is not that we are cooler but that we have better thermometers." The reformulated question does not inherit P6 — it estimates both slopes inside one instrument era — and arguably answers part of the P6 dispute as a by-product.
P7 (NEW, added at Gate 1; the deduction against well-posedness): peak attained is a function of response amplitude, stimulus magnitude AND time allowed to develop before truncation. Earlier care-seeking, triage and earlier antimicrobial administration across calendar time lower the observed peak without any change in physiology. Unless the design conditions on time-from-symptom-onset and on a stimulus measure (CRP, pathogen load, bacteraemia yes/no), a negative Δamplitude is not identified as weakened response.

What would count as an answer:
An era- or calendar-year-stratified distribution of peak temperature in matched documented infection, with cross-era thermometric bias modelled explicitly as a bias term with uncertainty (not assumed away), yielding Δpeak decomposed into Δbaseline and Δamplitude with confidence intervals, on a pre-specified scale with a bounded-scale sensitivity analysis, and conditioned on time-from-onset and a stimulus measure (per P7).
- "Baseline only" if the Δamplitude interval contains zero while Δbaseline is clearly negative.
- "Response attenuation" if Δamplitude is clearly negative and survives the time-from-onset/stimulus conditioning.
- A null bounded away from a clinically meaningful amplitude change (|Δamplitude| < 0.2 °C) also answers it.
Feasibility does not depend on the archival arm: the identical decomposition is computable inside the modern EHR era alone (roughly 1990s–2020s; same instrument classes, recorded antipyretic timestamps, coded pathogens), where a 30-year trend in matched-infection peak minus matched contemporaneous afebrile baseline is a strictly feasible and falsifiable test. If modelled thermometry bias exceeds the estimated effect in the archival arm, the reportable answer is a bound, not silence.

Significance — consequence:
If Δamplitude is indistinguishable from zero while Δbaseline is negative ("the ruler moved"): the deficit is metrological, and the fix is to re-reference fever criteria to a contemporaneous or per-person baseline in SD units. This converts Wright & Auwaerter's conjecture into an evidenced recommendation and tells device and guideline bodies that a fixed absolute threshold has drifted in its operating characteristics (specificity up, sensitivity down) without any change in the patient.
If Δamplitude is clearly negative ("the response weakened"): re-referencing the threshold does NOT restore sensitivity, because the signal is smaller and not merely offset. The implication is a secular change in innate immune activation or set-point regulation — bearing on the reduced-inflammation and inflammaging explanations of the baseline fall, and raising the question of whether other acute-phase outputs have attenuated in parallel.
If a bounded null (|Δamplitude| < 0.2 °C): settles that the baseline is the whole story, licensing simple re-referencing and closing a live ambiguity cheaply. All three outcomes are decision-relevant and the design can produce a bound rather than silence, which is what makes the question worth asking rather than merely interesting.

Significance — audience (named, with the action that would change):
- Bodies that set fever criteria: AAP febrile-infant guidance (the 38.0 °C rule-in for invasive workup), Sepsis-3/SIRS temperature criteria, IDSA/NCCN fever-in-neutropenia (38.3 °C single / 38.0 °C sustained), FUO definitions, CDC/WHO entry and port-of-entry screening. Action: adopt a delta-from-reference criterion versus retain an absolute threshold — and the choice between those two is exactly what the decomposition decides.
- Clinical-thermometry device and standards community (ISO 80601-2-56; temporal-artery and tympanic vendors). Action: whether site-offset and bias budgets must be recalibrated against a drifting population mean.
- EHR clinical-decision-support builders firing sepsis and deterioration alerts on temperature thresholds (directly adjacent to ledger entry UQ-20261015-21). Action: threshold recalibration by cohort year, or per-patient referencing.
- Thermoregulation physiologists and immunologists of inflammaging. Action: whether the secular baseline fall is treated as a change in tonic inflammation alone or as a change in pyrogenic responsiveness.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: (a) °C of rise per unit of measured stimulus (per log CRP, per log pathogen load, per bacteraemic episode) by calendar year — a cleaner estimand than peak alone and the natural follow-on; (b) has the acute-phase response as a whole attenuated (heart-rate, WBC, ESR, CRP responses decomposed the same way)? (c) does the availability and norm of antipyretic use change first the measured and then the actual fever curve — a pharmaco-epidemiological question with its own design; (d) does the within-person fever delta measured by continuous wearable thermometry differ by birth cohort, now that such cohorts exist; (e) a veterinary replication in production animals, where records are long, standardised and instrument-stable, which would discriminate human-specific from general mammalian change.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the Protsiv/Gurven secular-decline anomaly and its unresolved instrument-artefact dispute (Jones 2020); the "blunted fever in the elderly" literature (PMC7740114) as a confounder whose era analogue has never been examined; the explicitly flagged open conjecture about fever thresholds in Wright & Auwaerter 2020; the long-running complaint that SIRS/Sepsis-3 temperature criteria are miscalibrated; and the observation that afebrile presentation in sepsis carries higher mortality, whose interpretation depends on whether afebrility is becoming more common for mechanical or physiological reasons.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism, strongest first):
1. New data, newly extractable: the decomposition's primary arm needs the pre-antipyretic peak, which requires medication-administration timestamps at episode resolution. That became routinely extractable only with mature EHR research corpora in the 2010s–2020s; before that the quantity was not computable at scale, so the question had no feasible form.
2. Disciplinary boundary: the baseline-trend result lives in epidemiology, demography and physiology, worked on cohorts that are afebrile by construction or illness-agnostic — fever observations are a nuisance to be excluded. Fever magnitude lives in bedside infectious disease, where temperature is a covariate of the patient and never an outcome tracked across calendar time. Cross-era instrument bias is owned by metrology, which has no access to clinical cohorts. No single community holds both terms of the subtraction.
3. An answer-shaped object absorbs the observation: "blunted fever in the elderly / the immunosuppressed" is a well-established result, so a clinician who notices a weak fever attributes it to the patient's age or immune status. The explanation is available and local, which closes the observation before the cohort/era version of the question can be posed.
4. Incentive gap: a secular trend in peak fever sponsors no trial, no drug and no product; the threshold question (which does have a guideline sponsor) can be argued from the baseline result alone, so the harder decomposition has no advocate.
