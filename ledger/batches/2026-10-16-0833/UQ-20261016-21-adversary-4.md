## Restated question (adversary's own words)

Take a defined set of completed multicentre RCTs in which a software-containing device either produces the value of the primary endpoint or sets the dose of the intervention (titration), and whose report names the device model. (i) For how many of these trials can an outside investigator, working from manufacturer release notes, regulatory/recall databases or direct enquiry, show that the manufacturer changed the device's firmware or calibration algorithm at some date inside the recruitment period, and by which route? (ii) Where such a date can be shown and the trial's data can be re-run, does the treatment effect estimated in participants enrolled before the change differ from the effect in those enrolled after it by more than the trial's own pre-specified MCID (or, failing that, by more than half the width of the trial's primary 95% CI), with the interaction's estimability and its collinearity with calendar time reported? The record's two "alternative formulations" add a stronger gloss: that trials are silently pooling incommensurable endpoint measurements, and that firmware era is an unmeasured effect modifier large enough to change what trials concluded.

## Attacks

### 1. Triviality — **Resolved**

**Objection.** The answer can be written down now: routine change-control records are proprietary; the only public databases (FDA recalls/MAUDE, EU FSNs) capture safety-relevant changes only; trial reports almost never carry a version (Khattak et al. 2024: 0 of 22 wearable trials). So limb (i) will return a tiny fraction dominated by recalled devices, and limb (ii) is already answered for the one device class where it mattered (BOOST-II 2013; NeOProM 2018). Nothing new will be learned.

**Best response.** The direction of the guess is plausible, but no number exists for any retrieval route, and the one published census-like figure (Khattak 0/22) measures REPORTING in trial documents, not whether a revision OCCURRED and can be dated from manufacturer/regulatory sources. FDA's 1998/2001 modification guidance (fda.gov/media/72429) tells IDE sponsors to assess software/firmware changes via 5-day notice and to 'consider if the poolability of the resulting clinical data would be affected', and the 2023 DHT guidance (§IV.H.4) tells sponsors to 'keep a record of the timing and nature of any updates' — so the records exist but sit with sponsors and manufacturers, and nobody has measured what fraction of them a third party can reach. A near-zero retrieval fraction would itself be the finding (regulators' databases cannot answer a question trialists need), which is a determinate empirical result, not a page of reasoning. Limb (ii) has been done only for one oximeter model (see Attack 2).

### 2. Disguise — **Partial**

**Objection.** Renamed versions of this question exist in at least four vocabularies. (a) Own field: the NeOProM IPD meta-analysis (Askie et al., JAMA 2018) ran a PRE-SPECIFIED treatment x oximeter-software interaction across five trials — 3,140 infants on original software, 1,751 on revised; death RR 1.06 (0.91-1.23) vs 1.38 (1.14-1.68), 'P = .03 for interaction' — which is the candidate's limb (ii) performed across three trials with both eras, not 'only in the trial that happened to notice' as the record states. (b) Regulatory science: FDA's DHT guidance §IV.H.4 already poses the normative question ('DHT updates and other changes during a clinical investigation may lead to inconsistencies in measurements... sponsors should compare data from DHTs before and after the update... Significant changes in the measurement after updates may invalidate the results'), and Morrell & Bierer 2022 pose the 'update problem' for apps. (c) Neuroimaging: scanner-upgrade harmonisation is a mature literature (2-4% biases; ComBat-type corrections). (d) Diabetes technology: the same Libre sensor read by two reader generations gives time-below-range 6.5% vs 3.5%. The mechanism and the normative concern are known; what is 'new' is only the label.

**Best response.** Each neighbour was opened and none asks the census. NeOProM is one device model, in a prospectively coordinated collaboration that held the switch dates itself, with no retrieval-route question, no MCID benchmark, and the authors themselves unresolved ('considerable debate among the study investigators whether the change in oximeter software was responsible'). The FDA guidance is prescriptive for ongoing DHT trials and creates sponsor-held records, not a measurement of how often revisions occur or can be established by outsiders; it also does not mention analysis by version. The neuroimaging and CGM work are single-device metrology without any between-arm contrast. The 16 new-vocabulary queries below turned up the mechanism in many fields and the normative question in regulation, but no occurrence/retrievability census across device classes and no cross-class MCID-benchmarked interaction study. However, the record's novelty statement must be corrected: the cross-trial, pre-specified interaction for the index device class is in print, so the candidate's novelty rests entirely on the census limb and on device classes other than oximetry.

### 3. Combinatorial novelty — **Resolved**

**Objection.** This is device change-control (regulatory affairs) x trial meta-research, bolted together. Each half is routine — manufacturers already log changes, meta-researchers already count reporting deficits — and nothing new happens at the join.

**Best response.** The join is where the problem lives: the quantity requires matching a date held by the manufacturer to an enrolment window held by the trialist, and the only documented instances where that match was made (BOOST-II/NeOProM) produced a formally significant interaction on mortality. FDA's own guidance recognises the join as consequential ('may invalidate the results') but only provides for it inside sponsor-controlled trials. The two reporting-census efforts that exist (Khattak; Cochrane device-reporting reviews) stop at the trial document and never cross to the manufacturer record. A problem that two fields each recognise and neither can solve alone is an interface problem, not a concatenation.

### 4. Presupposition failure — **Partial**

**Objection.** Three presuppositions in the design as written fail or are contested. (1) ROLE CONFLATION. Under concurrent randomisation era is balanced across arms, so work the cases. Let Y* be the true outcome and Y = g_e(Y*) the device value in era e. Case A, device generates a CONTINUOUS endpoint and the revision is an additive shift: the between-arm difference is identical in both eras — Nab et al. 2019 §2.1.2: 'the difference between the two treatment groups is unbiased'. Interaction is exactly zero; pooling eras is harmless. Case B, gain change g_e = a_e + b_e·y: measured contrast scales by b_e, so the interaction is (b1 − b0)·Δ*; Nab's simulation with a 1.05 multiplier moved 6.9 to 7.2. For the interaction to exceed an MCID equal to Δ* needs a gain change near 100% — not a calibration revision but a different instrument. Case C, device value THRESHOLDED into a binary endpoint (time-below-range, hypoxaemia episodes, hypertension control): a shift or local nonlinearity moves different fractions of each arm across the cut, so the RR/OR can change; the Libre reader-generation study (same sensor, TBR 6.5% vs 3.5%) shows the magnitude is real. Case D, device TITRATES the intervention (BOOST-II): recalibration changes the intervention actually delivered — the revised algorithm gave 'clearer separation between the two target groups' — and the outcome (death) is not device-measured at all; the interaction is genuine heterogeneity of the intervention, not measurement incommensurability. Case E, device used only for eligibility: shifts the population, not the within-era contrast. Consequence: the record's alternative formulation ('silently pool incommensurable measurements because the devices generating their endpoints were re-calibrated') describes Cases A-C, in which the commonest case (A/B) cannot move the contrast by an MCID, while the anchor is Case D with a different causal structure. The question treats 'generated or titrated' as one estimand. (2) IDENTIFICATION. Within a trial era is a step function of calendar time — BOOST II: 'the new algorithm was used for all infants who were subsequently enrolled' — so treatment x era is indistinguishable from treatment x period (practice drift, learning, case-mix). The reformulation's 'collinearity diagnostic' can describe this but cannot break it; only a centre-staggered rollout gives leverage, and the design does not ask for one. NeOProM's authors concede the point ('considerable debate... whether the change in oximeter software was responsible') and note that mortality was also higher in 'the first reported trial that used the original software exclusively' (SUPPORT), which cuts against a pure software explanation. (3) POWER. Brookes et al. 2004: 'For an interaction of the same magnitude as the overall effect, this inflation factor was four', rising to '100 or greater' for subtle interactions. The design benchmarks a POINT ESTIMATE of the interaction against the MCID with no CI rule. My own arithmetic: a trial powered at 80% for Δ = MCID has SE_main = MCID/2.80; with two equal eras SE_int = 2·SE_main = MCID/1.40, so under a true null interaction P(|est| > MCID) = P(|Z| > 1.40) ≈ 16%; under the CI-half-width fallback (1.96·SE_main) the benchmark is 0.98·SE_int and ≈ 33% of null trials 'exceed' it. At 90% power the figures are ≈ 10% and ≈ 33%. Limb (ii) as written would therefore manufacture 'era interactions exceeding the MCID' in one trial in six to one in three with no true effect at all.

**Best response.** Limb (i), the retrieval census, is untouched by all three points and is the candidate's genuine novelty. Limb (ii) is repairable rather than dead: partition the frame by device role (titration/intervention-defining; threshold-defined endpoint; continuous endpoint, where the pre-registered expectation is a null interaction and the analysis is a calibration check), replace the point-estimate benchmark with an inferential rule (e.g. interaction 95% CI lying wholly above the MCID, with the expected false-positive rate stated), and classify trials with synchronous rollout as period-confounded rather than estimable. Under that repair the question remains answerable, but the alternative formulations about 'incommensurable pooled measurements' must be dropped for continuous endpoints, and the headline becomes narrower: for titration devices and thresholded endpoints, how often is a datable revision present and how large is the era-period interaction. This is a plausible redesign, not a defence of the design as written, and the record contains none of it.

### 5. Vagueness — **Partial**

**Objection.** Limb (i) is declared a lower bound on a proprietary process; any fraction from 0% to 100% is consistent with the question, and the record itself admits 'two investigators could get different fractions from the same corpus with equal diligence'. Limb (ii) can return 'not estimable' for every trial and the record calls that a complete answer. A question that is satisfied by every outcome is not a question.

**Best response.** The lower bound is falsifiable in a specific direction: a later investigator with a new route can only raise it, and route-specific yields (recall databases vs release notes vs direct enquiry) are determinate numbers that answer the regulators' question regardless of the ceiling. 'Not estimable everywhere' is informative only if the design states in advance what counts as estimable (see Attack 4). The weakness is real but procedural: the record does not specify an enquiry protocol (number of attempts, time limit, who is asked, how refusals are coded), so inter-investigator variance in limb (i) is uncontrolled, and it does not pre-declare the inferential rule for limb (ii). Both are fixable by pre-registration detail, neither is fixed in the record.

### 6. Significance inflation — **Partial**

**Objection.** CONSORT 2025 was just published without any device, software, firmware, version or calibration item (confirmed from the statement text: 'software' appears only as 'statistical code'), after years in which the oximeter case was well known — so the guideline audience has already declined to act on this concern. Regulators have already acted where they can: FDA's 1998 modification guidance covers IDE devices and the 2023 DHT guidance already requires sponsors to log updates and compare before/after. Manufacturers have no incentive to disclose. The census would change nothing.

**Best response.** CONSORT 2025's silence is the gap the candidate measures, and the same steering groups did add a version item where device-class evidence existed (CONSORT-AI/DECIDE-AI), so the claim that they act on evidence rather than anecdote is supported. FDA §IV.H.4 shows regulators accept the mechanism and already mandate sponsor-held records; a census showing those records are unreachable to IPD meta-analysts and third-party reanalysts is directly actionable (deposit update logs with registration or IPD). But the FDA guidance also pre-empts part of the significance: for DHT-based trials going forward, the records will exist and the sponsor must address updates 'prior to unblinding', so the retrospective census matters mainly for conventional endpoint devices (oximeters, CGMs, BP monitors, analysers) and for the pre-2023 corpus. The audience is real; the inference that a census would move CONSORT is asserted, not evidenced.

### 7. Reason for silence — **Resolved**

**Objection.** If this were well-posed and important, the NeOProM collaboration — five trials, the exact device, a pre-specified interaction — would have generalised it, and the regulatory community that wrote §IV.H.4 would have counted how often updates happen. Absence of both suggests the question was considered and judged not worth asking.

**Best response.** The record's mechanisms are evidenced rather than asserted. Records asymmetry: FDA guidance places update logs with sponsors, with no publication requirement, and trial reports carry no versions (0/22). Disciplinary boundary: NeOProM could stratify only because the collaboration itself held the switch dates; the authors treated the finding as a property of their trials and listed it as 'future research', not as an instance of a class. Incentive gap: a manufacturer disclosing change-control dates for someone else's trial gains nothing and risks liability. I would add a fifth mechanism that the record misses and that Attack 4 exposes: trialists' correct intuition that randomisation protects between-arm contrasts from outcome-measurement shifts (true for additive error, Nab 2019) is over-generalised to titration devices and thresholded endpoints, where it does not hold, so the threat is discounted on principle. That is a conceptual-bridge gap, an acceptable mechanism.

### 8. Dissolution — **Partial**

**Objection.** Under analysis the question splits into pieces that already have homes. The retrieval limb is a records-access question about manufacturer change control (answer: proprietary except recalls, as FDA guidance structure implies). The interaction limb dissolves into either (a) 'the intervention changed mid-trial' — a fidelity issue CONSORT 2025 item 24a already covers ('whether they were delivered as intended (fidelity)') — or (b) 'the endpoint definition changed' — a measurement-validity issue; and in both the within-trial estimand is a period x treatment interaction that cannot be attributed to the device. Nothing called 'device-era effect modification' survives as a single object.

**Best response.** The retrieval fraction is an empirical quantity whatever its size, and no analysis of regulatory structure yields the number. The partitioned interaction limb survives for the two roles where the mechanism can operate (titration devices; thresholded device endpoints), and 'fidelity' as reported under CONSORT 24a is never operationalised as a vendor firmware date, so the existing category does not capture the trigger. The dissolution is partial and corrective: it removes the unified 'firmware era' construct and the continuous-endpoint case, leaving a narrower but real question. Whether what remains is worth the census cost is a judgement the record has not made because it did not see the partition.

## Strongest remaining objection

Attack 4, presupposition failure in limb (ii) as written. Under concurrent randomisation a firmware/calibration shift cannot move a between-arm contrast by an MCID when the device generates a continuous endpoint (additive shift: contrast unchanged, Nab 2019; gain change: interaction = (b1-b0)·Δ*, requiring a near-100% gain change), so the 'incommensurable pooled measurements' framing fails for the commonest outcome type; the index case (BOOST-II) is instead a titration device whose revision changed the intervention delivered ('clearer separation between the two target groups'), a different causal structure the question conflates with endpoint generation. Within a trial era is a step function of calendar time ('used for all infants who were subsequently enrolled'), so treatment x era is unidentified from treatment x period and the proposed collinearity diagnostic cannot repair it — NeOProM's own authors remain in 'considerable debate'. And benchmarking an interaction POINT ESTIMATE against the MCID, when the interaction SE is twice the main-effect SE (Brookes 2004 inflation factor 4), yields by my arithmetic roughly 16% false 'exceedances' under the null at 80% main-effect power and roughly 33% under the CI-half-width fallback. Compounding this, the cross-trial pre-specified era interaction for the index device class is already published (Askie et al. 2018, five trials, interaction P=.03), so the record's novelty claim for limb (ii) is wrong and the candidate stands or falls on the retrieval census and on device classes other than oximetry. The question survives only if limb (ii) is partitioned by device role, given an inferential rule with a stated false-positive rate, and restricted to trials with non-synchronous rollout.
