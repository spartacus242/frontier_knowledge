Question (canonical) [R1, adopted at Gate 1; V0 below]:
For open training runs with dense terminal checkpoints — at least three runs, including at least one open post-trained family — define the *shippable set* as those terminal checkpoints whose held-out validation loss lies inside a pre-registered equivalence band (band width delta fixed in advance from the within-checkpoint measurement error of the loss itself) around the released checkpoint's loss. Over that set: (a) what is the across-checkpoint spread (SD and range, checkpoint and eval item entered as separate variance components) of each decision-relevant measured quantity — a fixed standard capability-benchmark suite; refusal rate on a fixed harmful-request set; attack success rate under a fixed jailbreak suite; success rate on a fixed long-horizon agentic suite; (b) how does that across-checkpoint component compare with the item-level / paired-bootstrap interval that is currently reported for the same eval on a single checkpoint; and (c) under each of the two selection rules a developer could legitimately use — argmin validation loss, and argmax on a dev suite that is later reported — what is the optimism of the published number, defined as E[score on the reporting suite | selected] minus E[score of a uniformly drawn in-band checkpoint on a fresh eval draw]?

V0 (intake, superseded at Gate 1): Among the terminal checkpoints of a training run that are statistically indistinguishable on the training objective — validation loss inside a pre-specified equivalence band — how much do decision-relevant measured behaviours vary (standard benchmark scores, refusal and jailbreak rates, long-horizon agentic task success), and is the published number for the single released checkpoint therefore a selection-biased estimate by more than the eval error bars now reported alongside it?
Reason for the one permitted reformulation (well-posedness only): V0 left the band width unquantified, left "decision-relevant measured behaviours" an open list, and fused two distinct estimands — the spread of the sibling cloud and the bias induced by a selection rule — into one "therefore". R1 fixes the band's origin, fixes the measured suites, separates the two estimands, and models the two selection rules separately, as the contested presupposition P1 requires.

Alternative formulations:
- Is a released model's reported score a property of the training recipe or a property of which checkpoint happened to be shipped?
- How much of the reported gap between two models could be reproduced by shipping a different, equally-trained checkpoint of the same run?
- Does a published safety or capability number attach to a run or to a file — and by how many eval-error-bar widths?

Motivating gap:
Silence mapping — the area is saturated with how-answers and empty of why-questions. How to *select or combine* checkpoints is a literature of its own (EMA, best-on-dev selection, checkpoint averaging and ensembling, model soups — Wortsman et al. 2022; Nishida et al. 2025). How to *put uncertainty on an eval number* has been addressed (Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals", arXiv:2411.00640; Madaan et al., arXiv:2406.10229; seed-variance demonstrations). Open-training releases (Pythia, OLMo, LLM360) routinely display jagged intermediate-checkpoint eval curves and treat the jaggedness as noise to be smoothed in a figure or averaged away in a weight-space method. No source found asks what that jaggedness does to the published number for the one artefact that ships — i.e. whether reported capability and safety figures are artefact properties selected from a cloud of loss-equivalent siblings, even though safety cases, threshold-based capability claims, procurement decisions and scaling-law fits are all written about that single checkpoint. The enabling phenomenon is documented; the inference from it to the published number is not drawn anywhere I could find.

Presuppositions:
P1. A validation-loss equivalence band identifies checkpoints the developer could legitimately have shipped. CONTESTED — real selection also uses dev-suite evals and human judgement. Handled in R1 by modelling two selection rules separately (loss-only; dev-suite-best-then-reported) rather than assuming the loss band is the operative one.
P2. Enough dense terminal checkpoints are public to estimate the spread. ESTABLISHED for open pretraining runs (Pythia, OLMo, LLM360). CONTESTED for post-trained and aligned models, where refusal, jailbreak and agentic behaviour live. I did not verify that any open post-trained family publishes *dense terminal* post-training checkpoints; that is an unresolved feasibility item, not an assumption to be waved through. R1 keeps the declared-limit fallback: report the pretraining side fully and declare the post-training coverage limit.
P3. Eval resampling noise can be separated from checkpoint-to-checkpoint variation. ESTABLISHED — paired bootstrap and item-level resampling are available (Miller 2024 supplies the machinery; Xu et al. 2026 use an item-level multiplier bootstrap). This separation is the measurement's core.
P4. Reported scores are treated by third parties as properties of the model rather than of the artefact. ESTABLISHED by practice — leaderboards, system cards, procurement, threshold-based policy commitments. (Supporting policy documents surfaced in query 7 were listed, not opened; the practice claim rests on general observation, not on a source I read.)
P5. Checkpoints inside the band are not simply equivalent models. THIS IS THE QUESTION, not an assumption. An equivalence finding is a clean, publishable null (band (i)).
P6. Behavioural spread, if found, would be acted on. CONTESTED — developers might respond by reporting a checkpoint-ensemble interval; evaluators and regulators would have to decide whether a safety claim attaches to a run or to a file. This is the fecund part, and it is flagged, not assumed.

What would count as an answer:
For at least three open runs with dense terminal checkpoints, including at least one post-trained family: compute the across-checkpoint spread of each measured quantity inside the pre-specified loss-equivalence band; compare it against paired-resampling noise for the same eval on the same checkpoint; and estimate the optimism of "select best of K checkpoints on a dev suite, report on that suite" relative to a fresh eval draw. Pre-committed bands:
(i) spread <= eval noise — reported numbers are recipe properties and the worry is retired;
(ii) spread > noise but smaller than the margins used in published safety cases and leaderboard comparisons — a reporting-practice recommendation follows;
(iii) spread crosses at least one published decision threshold or leaderboard gap — reported numbers are artefact-specific and selection-biased, and both system cards and scaling-law fits over checkpoint scores need rebuilding.

Novelty — how this differs from each:
vs N1 (Nishida et al.): same raw phenomenon, opposite question. N1 asks "how do I pick or smooth a checkpoint despite the fluctuation", and treats the fluctuation as an obstacle to selection to be removed by averaging. The candidate asks what the fluctuation does to the number that was published for the checkpoint actually shipped. Concretely, N1 does not (a) define a validation-loss equivalence band identifying the legitimately shippable set, (b) compare the fluctuation's magnitude against the eval resampling interval that is reported alongside published scores — so it cannot say whether the jaggedness exceeds the error bars already in print, (c) measure refusal, jailbreak or long-horizon agentic behaviour, or (d) estimate best-of-K selection optimism or relate any of it to safety-case margins, thresholds or leaderboard gaps. N1 establishes the candidate's enabling fact; it does not ask the candidate's question.
vs N2 (Madaan et al.): N2's estimand is the reliability of a *comparison between training decisions*, and its variance metrics are seed variance plus a monotonicity statistic over training (a smoothness/ordering property of the eval curve), not the spread of a cloud of checkpoints pre-specified as loss-equivalent at the end of one run. N2 reports no selection-optimism quantity for a shipped artefact, does not define an equivalence band, and (per the abstract page) covers multiple-choice-style capability benchmarks with no refusal, jailbreak or agentic suite. The candidate also demands the comparison N2 does not make: across-checkpoint component versus item-level resampling interval on a single checkpoint.
vs N3 (Lu et al.): N3's checkpoints are deliberately different recipes — branches given different final-window data — so they are matched *by measured post-SFT behaviour*, not drawn from one unmodified run's terminal cloud and matched on validation loss. N3 therefore answers a causal data-attribution question ("what does the last pretraining window do to later refusal?") and recommends reporting final-window data. The candidate holds the recipe fixed and varies only which legitimate sibling ships, and asks whether the published number is a selection-biased estimate by more than its own reported error bars. N3 matters as direction-of-effect evidence that behaviourally-indistinguishable checkpoints can diverge on refusal; it does not measure a within-run sibling spread, state an eval-noise comparison, or frame anything as selection bias in a released artefact's number.
vs N4 (Xu et al.): supplies exactly the statistical machinery the candidate's part (c) would use (frozen shortlist, split selection from held-out eval, item-level multiplier bootstrap, procedure-level reporting), applied to a different selection object (prompts/programs under a tuning budget). The candidate's novelty is not the correction method but the empirical question of whether loss-equivalent weight siblings of one run differ decision-relevantly, and whether the resulting optimism is large against published safety and leaderboard margins.
vs N5 (Shen & Li): structurally the closest thing found to the candidate's *claim shape* — "published numbers are artefact properties, and the movement crosses published verdicts" — but in a different literature, model class and channel: unlearning efficacy for batch-normalized vision models, moved by refitting unreleased BN statistics at fixed weights. It is not about which of several loss-equivalent checkpoints was shipped, uses no loss-equivalence band, and covers no LLM capability benchmark, refusal/jailbreak rate or agentic suite. It does show the question-shape is thinkable and has been thought in a neighbouring field, which is why novelty here is Medium and not High.

Significance — consequence:
If band (iii) holds, a published capability or safety figure is a property of a file rather than of a recipe, and the margin by which it could have differed is currently unreported. Three specific things then break rather than merely wobble: threshold-based commitments, which trigger or withhold mitigations on a measured score for a model version, would need a run-level interval and an argument about which sibling was evaluated; scaling-law and emergence fits drawn through per-checkpoint benchmark scores inherit a selection component they do not model; and any inter-lab leaderboard gap narrower than the within-run spread becomes uninterpretable, because the same run could have produced either side of it. If band (i) holds, the current practice of reading one checkpoint's number as the recipe's number is licensed — which is itself worth having in print, because the licence is assumed everywhere and demonstrated nowhere I could find. Band (ii) yields a bounded reporting-practice recommendation: add a between-checkpoint variance component to published intervals. The argument rests on a named discrepancy, not on a hunch: the jaggedness is documented (N1, N2) and the published error bars demonstrably contain no checkpoint term (query 8: every error-bar proposal found is item- or seed-level), so the comparison that would decide whether the discrepancy matters is precisely what is missing.
Significance — audience:
(1) Frontier-lab evaluation teams and the authors of system cards and capability thresholds: they would have to decide whether a safety claim attaches to a run or to a file, and would plausibly report an artefact hash plus a sibling-spread bound. (2) Third-party evaluators (METR, the national AI safety/security institutes), who receive one snapshot and whose reports are read as being about "the model": a sibling-spread estimate tells them how far their finding generalises, and whether a replication on a different in-band checkpoint should be expected to reproduce it. (3) The eval-statistics community already adding error bars (N2; Miller 2024): a checkpoint variance component is a concrete, adoptable change to an interval they already compute. (4) Leaderboard maintainers and procurement evaluators: a minimum detectable difference above within-run spread. Caveat kept explicit (P6): the reaction could also be indifference, or a move to ensemble reporting that sidesteps the question.
Significance — fecundity:
New askable questions: does sibling spread shrink with model scale or with post-training length (an eval-reliability scaling law)? Is the spread behaviour-specific — do safety behaviours have systematically wider clouds than capability scores, which would be an alignment-stability result in its own right? Does averaging or souping inside the band land the safety behaviour at the cloud's mean, or somewhere outside it? Is "which sibling ships" an auditable covert-selection channel — a developer could, inside a legitimate loss band, ship the checkpoint that clears a threshold — which would make the band itself an object of regulatory interest? Does a third-party eval finding transfer across siblings, i.e. what is the replication rate of a safety result on a different in-band checkpoint of the same run?
Significance — anchoring:
Touches: the eval-reliability and error-bar programme (N2; Miller 2411.00640); the checkpoint-instability and weight-averaging literature (N1; model soups, Wortsman et al. 2022); classical selection-bias-in-performance-evaluation results and their LLM-era revival (Cawley & Talbot 2010; Dodge et al. 1909.03004; N4); the open question implicit in frontier safety frameworks of whether a measured property attaches to a version or to a process; the emergence/scaling debate, which fits curves through exactly the per-checkpoint scores at issue; and N3's finding that behaviourally matched checkpoints can diverge in refusal after preference optimisation.
Significance score: 3/3.

Reason for silence:
A role-and-data boundary reinforced by an incentive gap. The people who can see the cloud of terminal checkpoints are pretraining engineers, whose question about it is an optimisation question — "which one do I ship, and can I average them?" — answered by EMA, best-on-dev selection, averaging and soups. Once one artefact is handed on, the siblings are discarded: the people who write the published number, the system card and the safety case receive a single file and have no cloud to compare it against, so the question cannot form on their side of the handoff. The incentive runs the same way: a developer has no reason to publish the spread of the checkpoints it did not ship, since doing so widens its own reported intervals and could show a threshold being cleared by artefact luck; and an external researcher cannot see the cloud at all for post-trained models. So the group with the data lacks the motive and the group with the motive lacks the data. The releases that do publish dense checkpoints (Pythia, OLMo, LLM360) publish them for interpretability and training-dynamics research, where the jaggedness is a nuisance in a figure rather than the object of study — which is exactly why the measurement exists (N1, N2) while the selection-bias inference from it does not. N5 shows the inference is thinkable: it was drawn in a neighbouring literature, for a different quantity and model class, which supports a boundary mechanism rather than an "it is obvious and wrong" explanation.
