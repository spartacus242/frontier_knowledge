Domain(s): Medicine — hospital care of the deteriorating patient; clinical monitoring and early-warning scores. Method-owning neighbours: clinical prediction-model methodology (calibration/transportability, TRIPOD school) and biostatistics of informative observation processes.

Question (canonical — V1, reformulated once at Gate 1 for well-posedness; V0 retained below):
In a surgical or medical ward cohort carrying, on the same patients and the same hours, (a) continuous device-derived vital signs and (b) concurrent routine clinician-scheduled observation sets, with deterioration outcomes ascertained independently of both streams: under a pre-declared aggregation rule (primary: worst value per 1-hour window; sensitivity: median per window, time-above-threshold, and windows of 5/15/60/240 min), and restricted to the parameter set common to both streams, what are the realised calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, discrimination, alert rate per patient-day, positive predictive value and lead time of the unchanged published NEWS2 thresholds on each stream; what intercept/slope/threshold recalibration restores the intermittent stream's operating point on the continuous stream; and — tested within the intermittent stream alone, so it is identified without the device — does observation intensity (time since last observation set, observations per 24 h, and the clinician-initiated escalation of frequency) carry prognostic information for the same outcome beyond the recorded values, i.e. how much of the score's apparent prognostic value is carried by the timing of the observations rather than by the physiology they recorded?

Question (V0, as at intake): When an early-warning score derived and calibrated on intermittent, clinician-scheduled vital-sign observations (e.g. NEWS2) is instead computed from continuous wearable ward monitoring, how much of its published calibration and threshold performance survives — that is, how much of its apparent prognostic value came from the informative *timing* of the observations rather than from the physiology they recorded?

Reason for the reformulation: V0 asks "how much survives" of a "published calibration" without naming an aggregation rule, without naming the estimands, and without separating the timing effect from the device-versus-nurse measurand effect. V0 as written is also partly answerable "whatever the evidence shows" because any number can be read as a fraction surviving. V1 pre-declares the aggregation rule and its sensitivity analyses, names six estimands, restricts to the shared parameter set so the cross-stream comparison is not a disguised Bland–Altman study, and moves the timing limb inside the intermittent stream where it is identified.

Alternative formulations:
- Is an early-warning score a measurement of the patient, or partly a measurement of how often somebody decided to measure the patient?
- Under continuous ward monitoring, what are the realised alert rate, positive predictive value and lead time of unchanged early-warning thresholds, and what recalibration restores the published operating point?

Motivating gap: Ward observation frequency is clinician-chosen and rises with concern, so the derivation data behind every ward early-warning score carry informative observation times: the score was fitted to values recorded at moments selected partly by prognosis. Continuous wearable ward monitoring removes that selection, and hospitals are feeding the new stream into the same scores. Anchoring anomaly: continuous-monitoring studies report large amounts of out-of-range physiology that intermittent rounds never captured (recalled from the postoperative-hypoxaemia continuous-monitoring literature, Sun et al., Anesth Analg 2015 — recalled, NOT opened or verified in this run; treat as unverified). The newly measurable quantity is the full within-patient distribution of the score over time, against which the published operating point was never defined.

Presuppositions:
P1. Observation times in derivation-era data are informative given the recorded values. CONTESTED at intake; now ESTABLISHED in the candidate's own field. Jarvis et al. 2015 states the mechanism outright ("sicker patients get more vital sign checks, especially just before an adverse outcome, and monitoring frequency may be driven by EWS values") and shows it moves measured discrimination: AUROCs were higher when one observation set per care episode was used than when all were used, significantly so for many scores including NEWS. Agniel et al. 2018 establishes the general form in the neighbouring vocabulary: for laboratory tests, the presence of an order was associated with 3-year survival in 233/272 tests (86%), and timing data out-predicted the result values in 118/174 tests (68%).
P2. Device-derived and nurse-recorded vital signs measure the same measurand closely enough to substitute. CONTESTED, and the one opened paired-stream paper quantifies the failure: Bland–Altman device-minus-nurse differences of +3 bpm heart rate, **+9.4 breaths/min respiratory rate**, −2.1% SpO2, −0.9 °C temperature, with systolic BP and consciousness not measurable by the sensors at all (sensor EWS max 11 vs nurse EWS max 14). A respiratory-rate offset of that size alone moves NEWS2 by 2–3 points. This is not a side issue: it means any naive cross-stream comparison measures measurand mismatch at least as much as timing, which is why V1 restricts to the shared parameter set and still cannot fully identify the split.
P3. The score can be defined on a continuous stream at all, which requires a declared aggregation rule. CONTESTED and materially consequential — confirmed quantitatively: in the opened prior, alarm rate rose from 1.2–2 per patient-day at 1-hour windows to 70 per patient-day at 1-minute windows. Pre-declaration plus sensitivity analysis is mandatory, and V1 does this.
P4. Deployments are in fact applying published thresholds unchanged to continuous data. NOT DOCUMENTED in this run. Searches surfaced implementation and protocol literature (a digital-NEWS2 implementation qualitative study; a registered surgical-ward continuous-monitoring trial; a NICE medtech briefing) but I did not open a deployment report stating that unchanged NEWS2 thresholds are applied to a continuous stream in service. Record as assumed, not shown.
P5. A cohort exists with both continuous monitoring and concurrent routine intermittent observations on the same patients, outcomes ascertained independently. ESTABLISHED — such cohorts exist and one has been analysed (n=46 analysed of 60 enrolled, upper-GI resection and hip fracture, nurse observations >=3/day plus per-minute sensor data). Available cohorts are small, which bears on power for calibration slope, not on existence.
P6. NEW, and it damages V0: that a *published calibration* exists to survive. Largely FALSE for ward early-warning scores. Gerry et al. 2020 (systematic review, 95 articles) found calibration assessed for 8 of 22 development models reporting apparent performance (36%), mostly by Hosmer–Lemeshow, and in a minority of external validations (abstract 13/84, 15%; results text says 15 studies — the paper is internally inconsistent). So "how much of its published calibration survives" presupposes a baseline that mostly was never published; the honest version of the limb is *realised* calibration on each stream, measured de novo, which is what V1 asks.

What would count as an answer: In a cohort carrying both streams on the same patients, the score computed both ways under the pre-declared aggregation rule, reporting calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope, discrimination, alert rate per patient-day, PPV and lead time for each stream; plus a within-intermittent-stream test of observation-time informativeness (does measurement intensity add prognostic information beyond the recorded values, e.g. as added covariates or as an explicit joint model of the observation and outcome processes); plus the intercept/slope/threshold recalibration that restores the intermittent operating point on the continuous stream; plus a declared bound on how much of the cross-stream gap is attributable to measurand mismatch rather than timing (shared-parameter restriction, and re-running with nurse values substituted at nurse observation times). Equal calibration and operating point across streams answers in the negative and licenses reusing thresholds.

Novelty confidence: **Low**. A near-duplicate exists and was opened (N1), covering the alert-rate/PPV/threshold/aggregation-window limb on a paired-stream cohort; and the candidate's conceptual core — that clinician-chosen observation timing is informative and inflates apparent EWS performance — is stated and empirically probed in the candidate's own field (N2) and established in general form next door (N3). The surviving unasked conjunction (formal calibration-in-the-large and slope transported between streams, a lead-time distribution, an explicit recalibration map restoring the operating point, and a quantified timing-versus-physiology decomposition) is a genuine increment but a narrow one, and one unread paper (10.1186/s44247-025-00151-x) could shrink it further.

Significance — consequence: If unchanged thresholds transport (equal calibration and operating point), hospitals can pipe continuous streams into existing NEWS2 escalation rules and the published evidence base carries over; the current practice is licensed. If they do not, the realised consequence is already visible in N1's numbers and is severe in a specific direction: 2 alarms per patient per day at an FDR of 85%, rising to 70 alarms per patient-day if the aggregation window is shortened to a minute, with sensor EWS "rarely dropping below 2" — i.e. the whole population sits nearer the trigger because the continuous stream catches excursions the rounds missed. The practical consequence is therefore not "the score fails" but "the escalation threshold is a free parameter that each deployment is silently choosing by picking an aggregation window", which is an unowned decision in current practice. The timing limb has a sharper consequence: if observation intensity carries prognostic information beyond the values, then part of every published EWS AUROC is credit for the nurse's judgement in deciding to measure, and that part does not transfer to any automated stream — which would also mean continuous monitoring must *beat* the intermittent score by more than the nominal gap to be worth deploying.
Significance — audience: (i) hospital deterioration/deteriorating-patient committees and digital-EWS configuration owners choosing an aggregation window and a trigger on a continuous feed — they would act by pre-declaring the window and recalibrating the trigger instead of inheriting 5; (ii) the score custodians (RCP for NEWS2, and national equivalents) who would have to state whether NEWS2 is defined on a continuous stream at all; (iii) continuous-monitoring device vendors and the trials behind them, whose alarm-burden claims depend on the window; (iv) prediction-model methodologists, for whom this is a transportability-across-sampling-regimes case with an unusually clean paired design; (v) regulators and HTA bodies (the NICE medtech-briefing route surfaced in query 9). The audience is real and identifiable — but points (i)–(iii) can already act on N1 without this question being answered, which is what caps the score.
Significance — fecundity: opens (a) whether *every* ward score has an aggregation-rule-dependent operating point and whether the dependence is monotone; (b) whether nurse observation timing should be an explicit covariate in deterioration models, and what happens to a deployed model when continuous monitoring removes the covariate it was silently using; (c) joint modelling of the observation process and the outcome in ward data, which is the biostatistics literature of query 8 transplanted into EWS development; (d) whether continuous monitoring should be scored at all, versus modelled as a trajectory with no score; (e) whether the measurand gap (N1's +9.4 breaths/min) means device NEWS2 needs its own respiratory-rate points rather than the manual ones.
Significance — anchoring: alarm fatigue and false-alarm burden in ward monitoring; the Gerry/Collins critique that EWS development is methodologically weak and calibration mostly unreported (36% of development models); EHR process-bias (Agniel 2018); informative-visit/observation-process joint modelling (arXiv 2410.13113, arXiv 2602.15374); the postoperative-hypoxaemia continuous-monitoring anomaly (Sun et al. 2015 — recalled, not verified here); and the ledger's own performativity cluster (UQ-20261015-21).

Reason for silence: Partly new instrument, partly disciplinary boundary, and partly a false premise — and the honest version is that the silence is much smaller than the intake claimed. New instrument: continuous ward wearables only recently reached cohorts large enough to compute a score on, and N1 (2023) is early-generation evidence on 46 patients. Disciplinary boundary: the people who own continuous ward monitoring are perioperative and device researchers whose reporting conventions are sensitivity, alarm rate and false discovery rate (exactly N1's quantities), while calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope and recalibration maps are the vocabulary of prediction-model methodologists who work on intermittent registry data and, per Gerry 2020, do not discuss continuous monitoring at all; neither group writes the other's outcome table. False premise: the question asks what survives of a *published* calibration that, for ward EWSs, mostly was never published (8/22 development models) — so there was no baseline to transport, and the question could not have been posed in the form the intake chose. That last mechanism is a reason the V0 phrasing is silent, not a reason the underlying comparison is unasked; and the underlying comparison is not unasked (N1), nor is the mechanism unnoticed (N2). A candidate whose silence is best explained by its own false premise should not be read as a frontier.
