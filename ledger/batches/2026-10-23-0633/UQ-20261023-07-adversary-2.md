## Restatement in my own words

Hospitals increasingly transfuse red cells one bag at a time and then re-check the patient's haemoglobin ("single-unit policy"). But a "unit" is not a standardised amount of haemoglobin: regulators set only a floor on grams per bag, so the grams actually delivered differ from bag to bag and nobody at the bedside knows the number. The question asks three separate things. **L1:** of all the episode-to-episode scatter in how much a patient's haemoglobin rises, how much is attributable to how many grams were in the particular bag (as against the patient's blood volume, diagnosis, etc.), and what is the rise per gram delivered? **L2:** because patients get whichever compatible bag the fridge hands over, does the chance of being sent back for a second bag track the grams in the first one? **L3:** if the service labelled bags by grams and issued by grams to hit the same haemoglobin target, how much would the second-unit rate fall? V2 (Gate 1's one allowed repair) adds a population restriction (non-bleeding adult inpatients, documented one-unit-then-reassess policy), a 12-48 h sampling window, inventory issue order within the compatible pool as the assignment mechanism with a covariate-balance test, and a 48-h second-unit hazard as L2's endpoint.

Plain reading of what it stakes: *that the bag is an unmeasured, consequential dose, and that treating it as a countable item rather than a weighable dose costs second units.*
