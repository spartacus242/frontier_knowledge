## Claim under test: `UQ-22-catch-order`

## Verdict: **PARTLY_CONFIRMED**

## What the sources actually say

(1) ARRIVE 2.0 E&E, Item 4a (Randomisation) — quoted sentence CONFIRMED verbatim, from both the canonical site (https://arriveguidelines.org/arrive-guidelines/randomisation/4a/explanation) and the EQUATOR mirror: "Selecting an animal 'at random' (i.e., haphazardly or arbitrarily) from a cage is not statistically random, as the process involves human judgement." The next sentence: "It can introduce bias that influences the results, as a researcher may (consciously or subconsciously) make judgements in allocating an animal to a particular group" — or bias arises "because of unknown and uncontrolled differences in the experimental conditions or animals in different groups." MECHANISM NAMED: selection bias via conscious/subconscious experimenter judgement. It does NOT name catch order, ease of catching, or which animal is grabbed first. REMEDY: "Using a validated method of randomisation helps minimise selection bias"; examples given are "online random number generators" or "a function like Rand() in spreadsheet software"; plus allocation concealment (Item 5, Blinding) and the NC3Rs Experimental Design Assistant. MEASUREMENT OF THE BIAS: none. The sentences carry no citation; the "validated method" sentence cites refs 62-64 (Hirst 2014; Bebarta 2003; Vesterinen 2010 etc.). The 30-45% effect-size inflation figures in the E&E sit under Item 5 and concern non-blinded outcome assessment, not haphazard selection. — The catch-order mechanism IS explicitly in print elsewhere, hypothetically: NC3Rs enrichment guidance (https://nc3rs.org.uk/3rs-resources/evaluating-environmental-enrichment/improving-scientific-quality-your-enrichment) warns against "choosing those animals that are slower and easier to catch or handle for the first group" because "the slower animals could be bolder and more confident than their harder-to-catch cage mates." Phrased with "could"/"may"; no data, no magnitude.

(2) Schipper et al. 2025 — citation VERIFIED: Schipper L, van Heijningen S, Karapetsas G, Olivier JDA, van Dijk G (2025). "Social housing conditions, hierarchical status and testing order affect behavioral test outcomes of male C57BL6/J mice." Physiology & Behavior 293:114859, doi:10.1016/j.physbeh.2025.114859 (PMID 39999904), open access CC-BY. Abstract/highlights (via OpenAlex, the RUG research portal and refinementdatabase.org): "Handling order and hierarchical status affect test outcomes of socially housed mice"; "Social housing conditions, social hierarchical status and within-cage testing order are factors that likely influence behavioral outcomes, but it is unknown to what extent these factors interact"; tests were open field, elevated plus-maze and three-chamber social test; "differences in behavior outcomes between individually and socially housed mice depend on the social hierarchical status" and on test order. So (ii) effect of within-cage order on endpoints: YES, measured. (i) repeatability of catch order as an individual trait across days: NO sign of it anywhere in the abstract, highlights or indexing; (iii) comparison of order-induced shift in group means to published treatment effect sizes: NO sign of it. CAVEAT I must flag: I could NOT read the full text, so the Methods definition of "within-cage testing order" is UNVERIFIED — whether it is the order in which mice were physically caught out of the cage or a pre-assigned/counterbalanced order. The CC-BY PDF at https://research.rug.nl/files/1241351906/1-s2.0-S0031938425000605-main.pdf failed with client_error on six attempts; sciencedirect.com and europepmc.org are robots-disallowed to my fetcher; direct curl to ebi.ac.uk/semanticscholar was refused by the egress proxy (403 CONNECT). Three independent abstract-level sources agree on the above, but the methods wording is not confirmed.

(3) The magnitude HAS been measured once, and earlier than Schipper — this is the finding that bears on the claim. Takao K, Shoji H, Hattori S, Miyakawa T (2016). "Cohort Removal Induces Changes in Body Temperature, Pain Sensitivity, and Anxiety-Like Behavior." Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience 10:99, doi:10.3389/fnbeh.2016.00099. It analyses serial position of removal from the home cage (positions 1-5) in archival control C57BL/6J data, order not randomised. Verbatim results: "Rectal temperature increased significantly in a stepwise manner according to the position of removal" (F(4,1695)=52.29, p<0.0001, n≈1,700); "mice removed first (position 1) had significantly longer response latencies than mice removed later" on the hot plate (p=0.0048 to p<0.0001); "Mice removed first from their cage (position 1) spent less time on the open arms compared to mice removed later" (p<0.0001 vs each later position); "Plasma corticosterone concentrations in the mice removed fourth were significantly higher than in mice removed earlier" (F(3,36)=9.261, p=0.0001). It reports no Cohen's d or eta-squared, does NOT benchmark against drug effect sizes, and does NOT test whether removal order is a stable individual trait (no ICC — position is a categorical ANOVA factor). Remedy it prescribes is counterbalancing, not randomisation: order "should be counterbalanced across genotypes." An earlier precedent exists: "'Cohort removal' induces hyperthermia but fails to influence plus-maze behaviour in male mice" (PMID 8140167, 1994). — REPEATABILITY (ICC) OF CATCH ORDER: 21 queries found NO study measuring whether the same individual rodent is consistently caught first out of a cage. The nearest literature is wild-rodent trappability, a different construct (capture probability in field traps, not within-cage catch order): "Mind the trap: large-scale field experiment shows that trappability is not a proxy for personality" (Animal Behaviour 2018, S000334721830191X) and "Bias averted: personality may not influence trappability" (Behav Ecol Sociobiol 2019, doi:10.1007/s00265-019-2742-4), both concluding trappability is NOT a personality proxy. — ALLOCATION BIAS FROM HAPHAZARD SELECTION, QUANTIFIED: none found. The randomisation literature (Hirst et al. 2014 PLoS ONE; "The Need for Randomization in Animal Trials: An Overview of Systematic Reviews," doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0098856) quantifies the consequences of not reporting/not performing randomisation in general, never haphazard catch selection as its own mechanism. All ARRIVE Item 4 citations are to clinical-trial methodology or general non-randomisation reviews, none to a catch-order measurement.

DIRECT ANSWER TO THE ROUTING QUESTION: Has anyone ASKED in print whether catch order biases groups? Yes — ARRIVE 2.0 Item 4a rules out haphazard selection from a cage (though it names human judgement, not catch order, as the mechanism), and NC3Rs guidance names the easier-to-catch/bolder mechanism explicitly but hypothetically and without data. Has anyone MEASURED it? Partly, and more than the claim allows: Takao et al. 2016 measured the effect of removal position on four endpoint classes in ~1,700 mice (p<0.0001), and Schipper et al. 2025 measured within-cage order effects on three behavioural tests. What remains unmeasured is narrower than "the magnitude": (a) no study anywhere measures the repeatability/ICC of catch order as an individual trait across days, and (b) no study converts an order-induced shift in group means into a comparison with published treatment effect sizes.

## Effect on the ledger verdict

The ledger verdict should be narrowed, not upheld as written. The first half of the claim stands: the question is on the record in its own field (ARRIVE 2.0 Item 4a; NC3Rs guidance, which names the easier-to-catch mechanism in so many words). The second half — "the magnitude has never been measured" — is too strong and should be corrected. Takao et al. 2016 (Front Behav Neurosci 10:99) measured the magnitude of removal-order effects on rectal temperature, hot-plate latency, elevated-plus-maze open-arm time and plasma corticosterone in ~1,700 control C57BL/6J mice, nine years before Schipper, and Schipper et al. 2025 measured within-cage order effects on three behavioural tests. Schipper is also not the first or the strongest measurement of this and should not be cited as if it were; note too that I could not read its Methods, so whether its "testing order" is physical catch order or a pre-assigned order is unverified. The defensible residual gap, and the one a routing decision should turn on, is twofold and narrower: (1) no study measures the repeatability (ICC or equivalent) of catch order as a stable individual trait across days — the only repeatability work is on wild-rodent trappability, a different construct, and it concluded trappability is not a personality proxy; and (2) no study benchmarks an order-induced shift in group means against published treatment effect sizes. If the claim is restated as those two gaps, it is CONFIRMED; as written, it is PARTLY_CONFIRMED with the "never measured" clause refuted.
