Domain(s): Medicine — prehospital/resuscitation care and systems-of-care quality measurement (target field). Adjacent: clinical informatics / healthcare technology management; measurement-error statistics and time metrology.
Question (canonical): In registries and quality programmes that assemble each case's timeline from timestamps generated by independent clocks (CAD dispatch, monitor/defibrillator, EMS ePCR, ED triage, inpatient EHR, lab analyser), what is the distribution of inter-source clock offsets, and how much does that offset distribution attenuate or distort the estimated per-minute dependence of outcome on elapsed time from which time-critical-care targets are set?
Alternative formulations: (1) How wrong are the clocks that make up a cardiac-arrest or stroke timeline, and what does that error do to the per-minute survival decrement used to justify response-time targets? (2) Treating a time-critical-care timeline as a composite measurement by several uncalibrated instruments, what is the measurement-error model for its timestamps and what bias does it impose on estimated time-dependence? (Gate 1 reformulation, retained in full in the record, names the source pairs, the traceable-UTC reference and the three estimands (a) per-pair signed offset, (b) share traceably synchronised, (c) slope/CI change under regression calibration or SIMEX.)
Motivating gap: As submitted — "synchronisation is assumed as a precondition and the offset itself is never measured" (Campbell, Ann Emerg Med 1995;26:697-701, links CAD and defibrillator stamps and claims only that synchronised clocks "may provide a method of accurately measuring the time elapsed"). FALSIFIED AT GATE 2: the offset has been measured against a traceable reference in this setting (Brabrand 2012), quantified inside one CAD (Srour & Badr 2017), reviewed as an error class (Goodwin 2022), and engineered around in industry since 1996 (US5749902).
Presuppositions: (1) one physical timeline per case, so per-source offset is well defined — ESTABLISHED. (2) Contributing clocks not already traceably synchronised — PARTLY TRUE for human-read and defibrillator clocks (Brabrand: 2/11 ED, 4/11 ambulance within 1 min; max spread 19:05), FALSE for at least some CAD (Srour & Badr: NTP from GPS), and further weakened by sync-at-download in defibrillator data workflows (US5749902, 1996). (3) Offsets recoverable retrospectively — CONTESTED; only the prospective route is shown feasible. (4) Published per-minute estimates are fitted on multi-clock timestamps — FAILS for the headline slope (Utstein 2024 core "Response time" runs PSAP call-connect → arrival at scene, both dispatch-domain); holds only for supplemental multi-clock intervals (call-to-first-shock, EMS-to-ED hand-off). (5) Offsets non-negligible at minutes scale — CONTESTED; doubtful for server-written stamps. (6) Offset not caused by outcome — CONTESTED; correctly separated from charting latency.
What would count as an answer: An empirically estimated per-source-pair offset distribution (each source's clock against a traceable GPS/NTP reference at service or upload events, or from events co-witnessed and independently logged by two sources), the share of contributing sources already traceably synchronised, and a regression-calibration or SIMEX re-fit of a published time-dependence model under that error model reporting the change in per-minute slope, its CI, and the implied benefit of a one-minute improvement. A null settles it equally well.

Verification (post-batch, two fresh-context checkers, logs UQ-20261017-16-verification-utstein.md and -brabrand.md): **the Gate 2 rejection stands, and the sharpening the adversary added to it is REFUTED.** The entry's closing clause held that the slope the question targets — the Utstein core "Response time" — "is written by a single dispatch-domain clock, so inter-source offset cannot bias it at all". The defining documents say otherwise, and they say the opposite of the clause's premise.
**The Utstein clause falls, on the template's own words.** In the 2015 update (Perkins et al.) response time is "The time interval from incoming call to the time the first emergency response vehicle stops"; in the 2024 template (Bray et al.) it is the interval between time point 1, "Time call connects to primary PSAP (recommended)", and time point 2, "Time dispatched first responders or EMS arrived at scene (wheels stop turning; recommended)". So the start is a call-handling event and the end is a **vehicle** event, and neither document says a CAD system records both — a crew or mobile-data-terminal status press, or an AVL/GPS stamp, would be a second clock domain (that mechanism is the checker's inference, labelled as such, not the template's text). The shock-based intervals cross systems unambiguously: call to first defibrillation runs from the same PSAP call to time point 2, "Time of first defibrillation (recommended)". Decisively, the 2015 template names the candidate's own problem and prescribes against it: "The problem of lack of synchronization of clocks and other time-recording devices persists", and "The recommendation remains that 1 clock (or synchronization to a single clock) be used to determine all times". The 2024 template is silent on clocks and instead requires that "A description of all time points used must be included in reporting" and that "This information should be collected from the same source for all cases and documented in reporting". The clause is struck. Note what this does and does not change: it widens the set of intervals the routed slope limb may legitimately address, and it adds the field's own consensus document to the list of places the problem is posed — which if anything *hardens* the asked-but-open finding on the slope limb rather than reviving the candidate. Caveat: the 2015 text was reached only through a secondary copy (a DC Fire and EMS server) because Circulation and Resuscitation could not be opened, and the quotes come from the page summariser, so the wording should be checked against the published version before citation.
**Brabrand's figures are PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, and three of them are misattributed in this entry.** The design is confirmed: each device measured against time.gov on 26 August 2010 at a stated accuracy of ±0.2 s, over 11 devices in the resuscitation rooms of two EDs and 11 ambulance defibrillator watches. But the paper reports ED medians **per hospital**, not pooled: Esbjerg −03:00 (IQR −03:00 to −02:00) and Kolding −00:30 (IQR −08:00 to 00:00). **The −00:30 this entry gives as the ED median is the Kolding figure**; the pooled 11-device median from Table 1 is −02:00. The ambulance median −00:45 and IQR −02:40 to −00:01 do appear in the text, but Table 2 yields a median of −01:26, so **the paper's own text and table disagree** and this entry inherited the text value without the discrepancy. The two "ranges" (−17:05 to +01:00 and −05:22 to +02:00) match the extreme table values but are not printed as ranges. Confirmed as printed: "only two of 11 (18.2%) time telling devices at the emergency departments were running correctly", "the watches deviated less than one minute in only four of 11 defibrillators (36.4%)", and "The maximum deviation between two time telling devices was 19 minutes and five seconds". Also confirmed: the paper reports **no** distribution of pairwise differences, only those two maxima, and links no device to any patient case.
**The "asserts but never estimates" finding is confirmed, and is stronger than the entry claimed.** Verbatim: "the use of unsynchronized time telling devices in research introduces a systematic error", "could lead to skewed results and introduce bias", and "Survival decreases with 7-10% per minute [8] and an error of several minutes in this context could potentially be disastrous." No bias, no survival shift and no effect on any registry estimate is computed anywhere. The checker also surfaced a sentence this entry did not have, which cuts against the prior's own force: "Such a small deviation will probably have little impact on the treatment of our patients or on research." So the nearest prior not only declines to estimate the consequence, it dismisses it in passing — which makes the slope limb more clearly asked-and-unanswered, not less, and is the strongest available support for the routed row.
**Where it lands. Rejected, unchanged, on the ground that survives.** The Gate 2 kill never rested on the Utstein clause: it rests on the offset limb having been asked, patented and corrected for in 1996 (US5749902) and measured against a traceable reference in this very setting in 2012 (Brabrand), with the slope limb named as an explicit open recommendation by Goodwin et al. 2022. Verification touches none of that. What it changes is the entry's accuracy: one supporting clause is struck, three figures are reattributed, and the routed row gains a better source. Caveat: Brabrand was read through the summariser and a direct PDF fetch returned HTTP 403, so the Table 2 median mismatch should be re-checked against the PDF before anyone cites either median.
