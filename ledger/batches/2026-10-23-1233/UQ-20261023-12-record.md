Alternative formulations:
(1) Are instruments brought in for calibration early because somebody already suspected them, and if so by how much is the drift / out-of-tolerance-rate-versus-interval curve that the interval policy rests on wrong?

Motivating gap:
THE SETTLED CLAIM BEING INVERTED: reliability-based calibration-interval analysis estimates the probability that an instrument is still in tolerance as a function of elapsed time from the as-found results of returned instruments, and adjusts the interval to hold that probability at a declared target. Its presupposition is that an as-found observation at elapsed time t is a draw from the drift distribution at t — arrival timing non-informative. In real fleets a user who distrusts a reading can send an instrument in early, and an instrument in productive use that looks fine can run overdue; either makes arrival timing a function of the outcome being measured.

What would count as an answer:
(i) DEPENDENCE TEST WITH AN EFFECT SIZE. The distribution of as-found |error|/tolerance compared between on-time, early-unscheduled and overdue arrivals, after conditioning on elapsed time since last calibration, instrument model and a usage proxy — reported as a standardised difference or a quantile shift with a confidence interval.
(ii) BIAS ESTIMATE IN DECISION-RELEVANT UNITS. The in-tolerance-probability-versus-elapsed-time curve estimated under the naive ignorable-timing analysis minus the same curve under an informative-timing model (inverse-probability-of-arrival / inverse-intensity weighting, or a joint model of arrival hazard and drift), converted into the change in the interval that meets a declared end-of-period in-tolerance target (e.g. 85% or 95%), in days and as a percentage of the current interval.
(iii) A SIGN: whether the naive analysis over- or under-states drift.
A NULL IS AN ANSWER: no detectable dependence and an interval change inside a declared equivalence band, reported with the design's power against a declared alternative.
NOT AN ANSWER: a demonstration that drift exists; a simulation with no fleet data behind the arrival mechanism; a reliability curve refit with no arrival model.

Significance — consequence:
YES (dependence is real, expected sign): suspect instruments arriving early put excess as-found error at SHORT elapsed times, so the fitted reliability curve decays faster than the fleet's true drift and the interval that meets a declared target is set too short — every interval set from uncorrected return data is then biased in a known direction, and the correction is a reweighting of records labs already hold rather than new instrumentation. Overdue arrivals of healthy instruments push the same way at long elapsed times, so the two arms may partially cancel, which is why the question asks for a SIGN and not only a magnitude. A finding of too-short intervals is a direct cost finding: fewer calibrations for the same declared in-tolerance confidence.
NO (null inside a declared equivalence band): the central presupposition of reliability-based interval analysis gets its first empirical defence. That is worth having precisely because, on the four documents opened here, it is assumed and never tested — Prior 1 notes arrival times vary and stops, Prior 2 assumes an unweighted fit, Bare 2006 screens nothing.
SMALL: bounds the effect below the fitting uncertainty already carried in the method — Prior 2's own end-of-interval bias intervals (±0.914 mm, ±1.110 mm at the stated confidence) are wide, so a small selection effect would be formally dominated, and that too is a usable answer.
