## Question (canonical)

Gate 0 intake version (as received):
> In clinical datasets where values originally recorded as missing were later recovered from an independent source, what is the empirical coverage of multiple-imputation predictive intervals and the residual bias in the target estimand — does the missing-at-random assumption that licenses routine imputation hold where it can finally be checked?

Gate 1 reformulation (the single permitted reformulation, for well-posedness only; both versions recorded):
> For a pre-specified set of (dataset, variable, estimand) triples in which (a) the missingness was real and present in a **frozen** analysis file, (b) the true values were subsequently recovered for some records from a source **independent of the analysis file and of the imputation model's predictors** (national record linkage, retrospective chart retrieval, registry back-fill), and (c) the recovery mechanism is itself characterised: what fraction of recovered values fall inside their nominal 95% multiple-imputation predictive intervals, and what is the bias and interval coverage of the target estimand under multiple imputation relative to the complete-after-recovery analysis — with complete-case analysis and a pre-specified MNAR pattern-mixture model as comparators?

Why the reformulation was needed: the intake version left three things undetermined that decide whether any evidence could settle it — (i) whether the missingness is real or analyst-induced (without "frozen analysis file" the question collapses into the existing artificial-amputation / masking-benchmark literature, where the analyst sets the mechanism); (ii) whether the recovery source is independent of the imputation model's own predictors (if not, the comparison is partly circular); (iii) whether the readout is per-value calibration or estimand bias (they can diverge, and the intake version conflated them). The reformulation also demotes "does MAR hold?" from a binary to its measurable surrogate (distance of coverage from nominal, per triple), because the binary is not decidable even with recovered values — only the degree and location of miscalibration is.

Alternative formulations:
1. How well calibrated is multiple imputation against the very values it was imputing, measured on real clinical data rather than in simulation?
2. Where record linkage retroactively fills in missing clinical values, how large is the bias that standard multiple imputation leaves in the analysis it was used for?
3. (Lay) When a medical study fills in blanks statistically and the real numbers turn up later, how often was the fill-in right, and how wrong was the study's conclusion?

## Motivating gap

Dense how-literature, empty why-literature. Hundreds of imputation methods and packages exist, and their performance is established almost entirely in simulation — a design in which the analyst *specifies* the missingness mechanism the method is then shown to recover. The matching empirical question (does the licensing assumption hold in the datasets where these methods are actually applied?) is near-absent, because MAR is non-identifiable from observed data alone: every MNAR model has an MAR counterpart with identical fit to the observed data. That non-identifiability result is correct and has been read as a prohibition on measurement. It is not: it forbids inference from the observed data alone, not inference from recovered data. National record linkage, retrospective chart retrieval, LLM-assisted chart abstraction and registry back-fill now recover, at usable scale, values that an analysis file had recorded as missing — so the licensing assumption has become checkable in the exact place it is being relied on, and the check has not been done in clinical biostatistics.

A finding from Gate 2 sharpens the gap rather than closing it: official statistics ran precisely this design on real nonresponse in 1988–89 (US Census Bureau, below) and found systematic underimputation. The design has existed for ~35 years in an adjacent field and was never imported into clinical biostatistics with modern MI readouts.

## Presuppositions

| # | Presupposition | Status |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Recovery sources exist at usable scale for clinically relevant variables | **Partly established; LIMITING and flagged.** Must be demonstrated per dataset. Weak support from the EHR-recovery literature (Lotspeich et al. 2025 recovered a missing-predictor status by chart review and a coded roadmap); unproven at the scale of "enough recovered values per variable to estimate coverage with useful precision". |
| P2 | The recovered value *is* the value that was missing, measured comparably | **CONTESTED.** A registry value may be a different measurement of the same construct (different assay, different time, different observer). Requires a concordance substudy on records where both the analysis-file value and the recovery-source value exist, run before any calibration claim. Lotspeich et al. explicitly call chart review an "alloyed" gold standard, "presumed to be more accurate... but still imperfect" — so the truth set carries its own error and must be modelled, not assumed. |
| P3 | Recovery is not itself so selective that the recovered subset misrepresents the missing set | **CONTESTED.** If recovery is non-random, the study estimates calibration on a subpopulation. Must be bounded explicitly (recovery-propensity model + worst-case bounds over the unrecovered stratum). This presupposition also conditions the Gate-1 decomposition: refitting the imputation model with recovered data in hand separates misspecification from mechanism failure *only if* the recovered subset is representative of the missing set. |
| P4 | The MI predictive distribution is the right object to calibrate, with interval coverage and estimand bias as the readouts | **Defensible; stated as a choice.** Rubin's MI is not designed to be individually predictive, so per-value coverage is not the quantity MI optimises; this is why the estimand-level readout is carried alongside it and is the primary one for the regulatory audience. Recorded as a deliberate choice, not a claim. |
| P5 | Analysts and regulators would change practice given a calibration curve | **Plausible and partially evidenced.** ICH E9(R1) already mandates sensitivity analyses for missing data whose *magnitude* (delta-adjustments, tipping-point offsets) is currently set by convention and expert opinion rather than by measurement. A calibration curve supplies the missing input to a decision already being made. |
| P6 (implicit, surfaced at Gate 1) | Analysis files are frozen early enough, and version history retained, that "what was missing at analysis time" is recoverable as a fact | **Flagged.** Requires datasets with auditable extraction timestamps; silently back-filled registries would destroy the design by making the missingness unobservable retrospectively. |

## What would count as an answer

Across a pre-specified set of (dataset, variable, estimand) triples, with the triple list and all comparators registered before recovery data are examined:

(i) **Per-value calibration** — coverage of nominal 95% MI predictive intervals against recovered values, reported per triple alongside a characterisation of the recovery mechanism (recovery propensity model, and the concordance substudy from P2).

(ii) **Estimand-level consequence** — bias and interval coverage of the target estimand under MI versus the complete-after-recovery analysis, with complete-case analysis and a pre-specified MNAR pattern-mixture model as comparators. This answers whether miscalibration at the value level propagates to the quantity anyone acts on.

(iii) **Decomposition** — attribution of any miscalibration to MAR failure versus imputation-model misspecification, separable by refitting the imputation model with the recovered data in hand (conditional on P3, and reported with the bounds from P3).

(iv) **Bounds** — explicit bounding of (i)–(iii) over the unrecovered stratum.

Coverage close to nominal across the triples is a substantive empirical defence of current practice and an equally useful answer. The question is therefore not answerable-whatever-the-evidence: nominal and non-nominal coverage are distinguishable, and both change what the audience does (confirm convention, or re-anchor sensitivity-analysis magnitudes).

## Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, with sources, all opened)

1. **Bailey L, Jansto AE, Smith C. "An Evaluation of Imputed Data for Nonrespondents to the 1987 Economic Censuses: Single-Unit Establishments." US Bureau of the Census, Statistical Research Division research report RR-92/03 (follow-up interviews Nov 1988 – Jul 1989; report c. 1992).** https://census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/1992/adrm/rr92-3.pdf — Opened and verified. Telephoned a sample of real census nonrespondents and used their reported data as truth: "The resultant data were used as a standard by which the imputed data for nonrespondents to the census were compared." Reports imputation correction ratios (reported/imputed) and finds "a tendency toward underimputation in the censuses," with most trade-area ratios not significantly different from 1.00.

2. **Joseph R, Sim J, Ogollah R, Lewis M. "An empirical evaluation of the impact of missing data on treatment effect." Trials 2015;16(Suppl 2):P136 (3rd International Clinical Trials Methodology Conference).** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4660178/ — Opened and verified. Two pragmatic RCTs (TATE, STarT Back). Outcome responses obtained after a given number of reminders were relabelled as missing, and MAR-based analyses on the reduced data were compared with the full data: "The impact of the reminder responses was assessed by comparing the estimates from MAR-based analyses between the two data scenarios."

3. **Glidden D. "Double-Sampling Designs for Dropouts." A subsample of dropouts gets intensive vital-status ascertainment; the recovered outcomes drive a weighted estimator. States the assumption as "Data is MAR conditional on Robs" and notes "Rate of death is about 16 times higher in dropouts compared to non-dropouts." Representative of the double-sampling literature (Frangakis; PEPFAR evaluations) that query 10 surfaced.

Also checked and found **not** to be a prior: **Lotspeich SC, Wells BJ, Rigdon J, Collins A, Khanna AK, D'Agostino McGowan L. ## Novelty — how this differs from each

**vs. prior 1 (Census 1992).** This is the closest prior in design and the most important finding of Gate 2: the imputed-vs-recovered-truth comparison on *real* nonresponse was executed 35 years ago, so the candidate is not conceptually unprecedented. Differences: (a) field and estimand — economic-census establishment totals, not clinical variables or a clinical target estimand; (b) imputation object — census hot-deck/single imputation, not a multiple-imputation *predictive distribution*, so no interval exists to calibrate; (c) readout — aggregate correction ratios at trade-area/SIC level, i.e. average accuracy, with no per-record interval coverage and no bias/coverage of a downstream inferential estimand; (d) no decomposition of error into mechanism failure versus model misspecification; (e) no comparator MNAR model, and no bounding over the unrecovered stratum. Net: the candidate asks for the *uncertainty* calibration and the downstream inferential consequence, in medicine, where prior 1 asked only about the *point* accuracy of imputes in official statistics. The existence of prior 1 lowers novelty of the idea while raising confidence in the reason-for-silence (disciplinary boundary, below).

**vs. prior 2 (Joseph et al. 2015).** Closest clinical prior, and the reason novelty is not High. Differences: (a) the "missingness" is manufactured — late (reminder-obtained) responses that *were* observed are relabelled missing, so the mechanism is "slow responder", a known and relatively benign proxy, not the real missingness that stood in the frozen analysis file; this is the artificial-amputation design the Gate-1 reformulation explicitly excludes; (b) the recovery source is internal to the trial, not independent of the analysis file or of the imputation model's predictors; (c) readout is agreement between two MAR-based treatment-effect estimates — no per-value predictive-interval coverage, no complete-after-recovery reference treated as truth, no MNAR comparator, no decomposition; (d) it is a one-page conference abstract on two trials, not a pre-specified multi-triple calibration study; (e) MI is not named. Net: prior 2 asks "do late responders move the estimate?"; the candidate asks "is the MI predictive distribution calibrated against the values it was imputing, and does the target estimand survive?".

**vs. prior 3 (double sampling for dropouts).** Shares the key resource — recovered values for records that were missing — but puts it to the opposite use. Double sampling *consumes* the recovered subsample inside an estimator to obtain a valid estimate under a weaker assumption; the candidate *spends* the recovered values on auditing the assumption and the calibration of the method that would otherwise have been used, keeping MI as the object of study rather than as a tool. Differences: (a) purpose — estimation versus measurement of method error; (b) MAR is assumed conditional on the design (MAR | R_obs), never evaluated; (c) no comparison of any imputation-based estimate against the recovered truth ("it does not compare imputation-based estimates against the recovered outcomes of the sampled dropouts"); (d) the double-sampling literature is prospective-design, whereas the candidate exploits retrospective recovery on datasets already analysed — which is what makes the question answerable at scale and across many triples rather than one trial at a time.

## Significance — consequence

If coverage is close to nominal across the triples: current practice gets its first real-data defence, and a very large methodological edifice — hundreds of packages validated only where the analyst set the mechanism — acquires external validity it does not currently have. That is not a null result: the magnitudes of mandated sensitivity analyses could then be anchored to measured miscalibration rather than to convention, which generally means narrower, defensible sensitivity ranges and fewer analyses whose conclusions hinge on a conventional delta.

If coverage is materially below nominal: every nominal 95% interval produced by routine MI in the affected variable classes is understating uncertainty by a measured amount, and the estimand-level readout says whether that propagates to the quantity decisions are made on. The practical outputs are concrete: a per-variable-class map of where MI is trustworthy, an empirical anchor for delta-adjustment and tipping-point magnitudes, and a test of whether the conventional MNAR pattern-mixture comparator actually brackets the truth — the current unexamined hope on which regulatory sensitivity analysis rests. Either direction converts an assumption-management problem into a measurement with error bars.

The decomposition carries its own consequence independent of the headline: if miscalibration is mostly imputation-model misspecification, the fix is better models and is already within the field's reach; if it is mostly mechanism failure, better models cannot fix it and the field's effort is misallocated. Those two diagnoses imply opposite research programmes, and nothing currently distinguishes them.

## Significance — audience

- **Trial and registry statisticians, and regulators operating ICH E9(R1).** They must specify sensitivity analyses for missing data and currently choose their magnitude by convention and expert judgement. They would act by replacing a conventional delta with a measured one, or by restricting MI for variable classes shown to be miscalibrated. This is an audience already forced to make the exact choice the answer informs — the strongest form of audience evidence.
- **Pharmacoepidemiology and real-world-evidence groups** working on EHR/claims/registry data, where missingness is endemic, MI is routine, and regulatory acceptance of RWE turns on credibility of missing-data handling.
- **Imputation method developers** (mice, Amelia, mi, ML imputers) and the methodological-review community, including the STRATOS initiative's missing-data topic group, for whom a real-data benchmark would be the first external yardstick against which simulation-tuned methods could be ranked.
- **Journal methods reviewers and guideline writers** (TRIPOD, STROBE/RECORD-adjacent reporting guidance), who currently have no evidence base for how strongly to require or distrust MI.
- **Data custodians of linkage infrastructure** (national linkage authorities, registries, biobanks), who would learn what recovery is worth analytically and could price recovery effort against inferential gain.

Counter-check against significance inflation: the audience is not merely "people who use MI". It is the much smaller set who must *choose a number* for a sensitivity analysis and currently have none to choose from. That set is real, named in a regulatory guideline, and makes the choice repeatedly.

## Significance — fecundity

New questions that become askable once a calibration curve exists:
1. Is MAR failure *predictable* from observable features of the measurement process — test-ordering intensity, visit density, documentation style — i.e. can the informative-presence literature be turned into a prospective warning system for which variables will break MI?
2. Do the conventional MNAR comparators (delta-adjustment, pattern mixture, control-based imputation) bracket the measured truth, and what delta would have been correct? Answering this turns regulatory sensitivity analysis from convention into calibration.
3. Is miscalibration *transportable*? If a variable class behaves the same way across health systems, measured deltas become reusable priors; if not, each dataset needs its own recovery substudy, which is a design question with a cost curve.
4. What is the optimal recovery design — how many recovered records, chosen how, buy how much reduction in inferential uncertainty? This is a two-phase-design optimisation question that cannot even be posed without the calibration measurement.
5. Does the ordering of imputation methods established in simulation survive on real recovered data? If the ranking inverts, the simulation literature's internal validity becomes a meta-scientific question in its own right.
6. Can imputation models be fit *to recovered data* and redeployed to unrecovered records — i.e. does recovery infrastructure make a new class of estimator available, not just a new audit?

## Significance — anchoring

- **The non-identifiability of MAR** (every MNAR model has an MAR counterpart with identical observed-data fit) — a standing result that is treated as closing the question and which this candidate does not contradict but routes around, by adding data the result does not cover.
- **ICH E9(R1) estimand and sensitivity-analysis framework**: an active regulatory requirement whose quantitative inputs are currently unmeasured.
- **Informative presence / informative missingness in EHR** (Agniel et al. BMJ 2018 — carried from intake, not re-verified in this run; Tan et al. 2023, surfaced in query 11): strong evidence that EHR measurement processes carry outcome information, hence that MAR is suspect, but stopping short of measuring what that does to imputation.
- **The simulation-validation problem in methodological research** more broadly: methods whose only evidence base is data generated under the assumptions they require. This candidate is a concrete, resolvable instance of that meta-scientific anomaly.
- **Two-phase / validation-sampling and measurement-error methodology**, which supplies the estimators for using a recovered subsample and is the field most likely to already contain a close formulation (see novelty flags).

## Reason for silence

Three mechanisms, compounding; none is "nobody thought of it".

1. **A correct theorem over-generalised into a prohibition (conceptual blockade).** The non-identifiability of MAR from observed data is true and well known. It licenses the conclusion "you cannot test MAR *from the observed data alone*", and the field adopted the shorter, false version: "you cannot test MAR". The entire methodological response was therefore routed into assume-and-bound sensitivity analysis rather than measurement — visible in Gate 2 query 6, where every hit is a sensitivity-analysis method and none is a validation study. A question you believe is provably unanswerable does not get asked, and this belief has a precise, citable origin rather than being mere neglect.

2. **A disciplinary boundary, evidenced rather than assumed.** Official statistics ran the imputed-versus-recovered-truth design on real nonresponse in 1988–89 (prior 1) and reported systematic underimputation. Survey methodology has nonresponse follow-up, reinterview and administrative-record validation as standard instruments, and clinical biostatistics imported MI from the same intellectual lineage (Rubin) while leaving the validation design behind. The transfer failed in one direction only: medicine took the method and not the audit. That the design demonstrably existed for 35 years in an adjacent field is positive evidence for a boundary mechanism and against "new instrument alone".

3. **Incentive asymmetry in method validation.** A simulation study is cheap, fast, publishable, and lets the author choose the data-generating mechanism — so the method under study can be shown to work. A real-data calibration study is expensive, needs linkage governance and a frozen-analysis-file audit trail, sells no new method, and risks indicting the toolkit its likely authors maintain. Nobody's career is advanced by measuring that the field's default is miscalibrated, and the people best equipped to run it are the package maintainers.

The genuinely new element — and the reason the silence has just expired — is **instrumental**: recovery at usable scale (national linkage, registry back-fill, LLM-assisted chart retrieval, cf. Lotspeich et al. 2025) plus auditable extraction provenance (P6) is recent. Mechanisms 1–3 explain why the question was not asked for decades; the instrument explains why asking it now is answerable rather than rhetorical.

## Scores

**Well-posedness: 3/3.** The Gate-1 reformulation states exactly what evidence settles it (per-value coverage, estimand bias and coverage versus complete-after-recovery, named comparators, decomposition by refit, bounds), both answer directions are informative and distinguishable, and it is a question rather than a thesis — nominal coverage counts as a genuine answer and is explicitly welcomed. All six presuppositions are enumerated, with P1 flagged as limiting and P2/P3 flagged as contested and assigned specific empirical checks (concordance substudy; recovery-propensity bounding). Residual weakness recorded rather than hidden: the sub-clause "does MAR hold" is not decidable as a binary even with recovered values, only as located degrees of miscalibration; and the mechanism-versus-misspecification decomposition is valid only conditional on P3, so it must be reported with bounds, not as a clean split.

**Significance: 3/3.** Consequence argued in both directions, with a non-null interpretation of the nominal-coverage outcome. The audience is not a diffuse user base but the specific set of statisticians and regulators who must already fix a sensitivity-analysis magnitude under ICH E9(R1) with no measurement to fix it from. Fecundity is concrete: six follow-on questions, at least two of which (transportability of measured deltas; optimal recovery design) are unaskable without this measurement. Anchoring touches a standing non-identifiability result, an active regulatory framework, the EHR informative-presence literature, and the broader meta-scientific anomaly of methods validated only under their own assumptions.
