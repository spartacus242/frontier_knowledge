## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Hospital oxygen comes from one piped network, and a nurse sets a dial. The question asks two things. (1) Hydraulic/metrological limb: at a given wall outlet, is the oxygen that actually comes out lower than the number on the dial by an amount that grows with how much oxygen *everybody else* on the same branch of pipework is drawing at that moment — measured against that branch's engineered design flow, with the breakpoint fixed in advance? (2) Clinical limb: for patients wired to continuous oximetry, how much of the up-and-down in their *own* oxygenation (time in SpO2 target, FiO2 shortfall, escalation to a bigger device) is explained by their neighbours' concurrent draw, judged by comparing the same patient's busy hours with their quiet hours and against a branch that should not be affected?

In one sentence: is a patient's oxygen dose partly rationed by their neighbours' breathing, and does it show up in their saturations? The candidate claims the engineering literature stops at the pipe and the clinical literature starts at the dial, so nobody has computed the join.

## What the two papers I opened actually say

**Davidson et al. 2012 (P3), full text — this discharges the record's promotion condition (2) and it cuts both ways.**
Methods: meters were tested in series from a hospital wall outlet at a Medicinal Gas Unit, with pressure *fixed*, not varied — verbatim fragments as served: "wall outlet passed through a 50 psi reducing valve, then entered the flow meter to be tested"; "In Brazilian hospitals, oxygen delivered at a patient's bedside usually comes from a central supply"; "at a pressure of 92 psi."; "Since the flow meters are calibrated at 50 psi, a reduction valve is connected at the wall outlet"; "All flow meters were manufactured to the same specifications (70°F, 760 mm Hg, and 50 psi)".
So: **inlet pressure was controlled at the calibration point and never varied, and concurrent use of other outlets is not addressed.** P3 is therefore *not* a hidden prior for the load-response question — good for the candidate's novelty.
But the same paper states the mechanism the candidate needs, **as an assertion rather than a measurement**: its Introduction says "One of the factors that may influence the value of the flow release by the flow meter is the gas pressure" from the wall outlet, and its Discussion that "if the gas source and the gas outlet pressure are unknown, a reducing valve is mandatory". Read level: full text. Direction and magnitude of the pressure dependence are stated nowhere in it.
Numbers (as printed): 5 L/min setting → "22% higher"; 10 L/min → "40% higher"; conclusion "good precision and poor accuracy". Table 1, flow-analyser means ± SD, used meters (n=66) vs new (n=25): 1 L/min 0.76 ± 0.97 / 1.27 ± 1.90; 3 L/min 3.10 ± 1.34 / 3.77 ± 2.52; 5 L/min 6.24 ± 1.71 / 7.46 ± 2.99; 10 L/min 13.76 ± 2.14 / 16.03 ± 3.82.
*My own arithmetic on those means* (not the authors' figures, and the authors' own stated percentages are 22% and 40%): used-meter ratios are 6.24/5 = +25% and 13.76/10 = +38%; pooling used and new by n gives ≈ +32% and +44%. The discrepancy with the paper's 22%/40% means the paper computed its percentages some other way, so I use the authors' figures where a figure is load-bearing and carry the SDs (1.71 and 2.14 L/min for in-service meters) as the stated spread. These are **between-meter** SDs across 91 devices; the paper separately reports precision (test–retest, 11 meters) as good, and it is that within-meter component, not the between-meter SD, that bounds a within-outlet design.

**The 2024 medRxiv preprint (title/byline not served) — a near neighbour on the demand side that is not a prior, and it incidentally strengthens presupposition P2.**
Its exposure is patient-level oxygen delivery computed from EMR device settings over 2020-03-01 to 2022-03-01, summed per hospitalisation and compared against facility liquid-oxygen procurement and an oxygencalculator.com model. It records SpO2, severe hypoxaemia, UVA score, IMV and mortality but **does not link demand or delivery to them statistically**, never measures pipeline pressure, and never measures delivered flow at a terminal unit — flows are "calculated with device-specific formulas from recorded settings". Two short verbatim fragments as served: the authors "can only adjudicate the amount of oxygen patients received, not the amount they required", and "there was no oxygen resource scarcity and the mean SpO_2_ was within the recommended range". So: per-patient-hour device-and-setting retrieval at two-year scale is demonstrated (P2 moves from contested toward established for at least one health system), and the aggregate-demand-to-outcome join is conspicuously *not* made even by a group holding both halves.
