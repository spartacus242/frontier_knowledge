Question (canonical):
V1 (as submitted): In a managed heap, is the death hazard of an object a function of its age alone — as every generational collector's mechanism assumes — or do the identifiable curvature components of program-phase (period) and allocation-epoch (cohort) effects differ from zero, and how is the deviance in object mortality split among age, period and cohort?

V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, the one permitted; this is the version scored): For a managed-runtime program traced to exact object death times on a single declared clock (bytes allocated), fit a Poisson/GLM hazard model of object death over cells of (age a, death time p), with the cell's exposure being the objects at risk in that cell, and with allocation epoch c = p − a entering as the third axis. Reporting only quantities that are identifiable under that exact linear dependence: (i) do the second-difference (curvature) sequences of the period axis and of the cohort axis differ from zero, with confidence intervals; (ii) does a pre-declared likelihood-ratio test reject the age-only submodel; and (iii) what are the nested-model deviance reductions, reported for ALL orderings of the three axes rather than one, since no unique attribution of deviance to collinear axes exists?

Why V1 needed repair (recorded, per Gate 1): V1's third clause asks for "the split of the deviance among age, period and cohort". Under the exact dependence p = c + a that V1 itself invokes, no unique split exists — any split is a function of the arbitrary constraint, which is the very obstruction the question is about. V1 therefore asks in its second clause for an identifiable quantity and in its third for a non-identifiable one. V2 keeps the curvatures and the age-only test and replaces the single split with all-orderings nested deviance reductions, which are well defined once the ordering is reported.

What V2 itself presupposes (tested as hard as V1, per the operating rule on repairs):
- That the at-risk set of each (age, death-time) cell is recoverable from the trace. It is, arithmetically, from birth and death times on one clock — my own reasoning, not a source finding; an object born at b and dying at d is at risk in every cell (a, b+a) for a < d−b.
- That all-orderings deviance reductions are interpretable. They are well defined, but they are a reporting convention and not one number; a reader wanting a single "share carried by period" will not get one, and the entry must say so rather than imply otherwise. This is my own reasoning about the model's algebra, not a cited result.
- That a phase rule independent of object age exists and is declared before tracing. UNCHANGED and still contested — the repair does not remove this, it is load-bearing in both versions.
- V2 drops V1's phrase "every generational collector's mechanism assumes". That phrase is an unenumerated universal over a kind of artifact (collector implementations); I opened none, so it is Unverified and should not appear in a canonical wording. The measured content of the question does not need it.

Motivating gap:
The generational hypothesis ("most objects die young") is an age-only statement about mortality. The runtime literature has measured the age MARGINAL of that mortality and has printed the raw two-way death surface, but has not modelled the surface, has not named the cohort axis, and has nowhere stated the identification obstruction that makes only curvatures estimable. Specifically, from sources I opened in full:
- Stefanović & Moss (LFP 1994) PRINT measured age-specific mortality curves (Fig 2(d), 4(g) and the Yacc equivalent: x = age in bytes, y = mortality in ppm/byte) and define object mortality as "the probability of objects of that age dying within the next infinitesimal time increment". They also state that variation along the phase axis is outside their scope. So the age axis is a measured known, and the phase axis is explicitly set aside rather than measured.
- Jones & Ryder (ISMM 2008) print, in Figures 2, 3, 4 and 8, surfaces whose axes are x = time of death as % of total allocation, y = object age as % of total allocation, z = bytes that died at that time having lived that age. That is a Lexis-style age-by-period death tabulation in all but name — but of death VOLUME with no at-risk denominator, per allocation site or site cluster rather than per object, with no cohort axis, no rate, no model and no identification discussion.
- Dolan (ISMM 2025) states as his opening motivation that of the generational hypothesis "it is not obvious how it can be measured as a property of a program (independent of a particular GC strategy)" and proposes lifetime dispersion quantified by a Gini coefficient instead — an inequality summary of the lifetime distribution, moving the field's measure further from a hazard rather than toward a decomposition of one.
So the gap is not "nobody has a hazard" — two of my opened sources have one on the age axis — it is that the period and cohort curvatures of that hazard, and the deviance those axes carry beyond age, have never been estimated, and the identification obstruction has never been stated in this field.

What would count as an answer:
For at least five programs traced under an exact-liveness oracle, a fitted age–period–cohort hazard model of object death reporting (i) the second-difference curvature sequences for each of the three axes with confidence intervals, (ii) a pre-declared likelihood-ratio test of the age-only submodel against the full model, and (iii) nested deviance reductions under all orderings of the axes. Non-zero period or cohort curvature with a rejected age-only submodel answers in the multi-axis direction; curvature confined to age, with the age-only submodel not rejected, vindicates the age-only mechanism and supplies the first measured bound on how much the other two axes can carry.

Significance — consequence:
If the period or cohort curvatures are materially non-zero and the age-only submodel is rejected, then the single variable every generational mechanism can cheaply observe — survival count, a proxy for age — is not the variable that carries the mortality signal, and promotion policy is conditioning on the wrong axis by a measured amount. That is a design change (phase-keyed nursery sizing, epoch-keyed cohort spaces, promotion conditioned on allocation epoch) and not a tuning change. If curvature is confined to age, the age-only mechanism is vindicated for the first time with a measured bound on what the other two axes could contribute, and Dolan's stated difficulty — that it is "not obvious how it can be measured as a property of a program" — gets an answer in hazard terms with an exposure denominator, independent of any collector's strategy.

Significance — audience:
Runtime and collector implementers (OpenJDK's G1/ZGC, .NET, V8, the OCaml 5 runtime — Dolan's ISMM 2025 paper comes from that quarter), the ISMM community, and the pretenuring and lifetime-prediction line that descends from Jones & Ryder's site clustering. They act by choosing what promotion and pretenuring condition on; a non-zero cohort curvature says allocation epoch is a predictor they are discarding, and a non-zero period curvature says the same of phase.

Significance — fecundity:
A positive cohort curvature turns "which allocation epochs produce long-lived objects" into a prediction problem with a correct denominator, which the pretenuring literature has only ever attacked through allocation site. A phase-conditioned hazard gives a principled, collector-independent definition of a GC-relevant phase change, which is what Jones & Ryder's hand-identified nested phases currently lack. And the rest of the demographic and survival toolkit becomes applicable at once: Lexis surfaces on the two clocks, competing risks with Veroy & Guyer's causes of death as the risk types, frailty models for the site and type heterogeneity Jones & Ryder measured, and lifetime-dispersion indices (Dolan's Gini) recovered as functionals of the fitted hazard rather than as a separate measure.

Significance — anchoring:
Directly on Dolan's ISMM 2025 statement of the measurement gap; on the weak-versus-strong generational hypothesis distinction, whose strong form is stated as a monotonicity condition on m(t) that nobody has tested as such; on Jones & Ryder's unmodelled death surfaces; and on older-first collection (returned, not opened), whose premise is that objects which are not young are also profitable to collect — an age-axis anomaly that a period or cohort effect would help explain.

Reason for silence:
Three mechanisms, none of them "no one thought of it". (i) A disciplinary-vocabulary boundary with a half-crossing: the runtime literature imported demography's NOUNS — demographics, mortality, generations, infant mortality — but took the lifetime DISTRIBUTION as its object rather than the rate-with-exposure, and the identification obstruction is a statistical result with no currency in programming languages; query 7 found the APC machinery has never been pointed at program execution at all. (ii) An instrument gap with the wrong timing: cell-level risk sets need exact death times, and the Merlin line that supplies them arrived in 2002–2006, after the 1993–2000 characterisation wave (priors 2 and 3) had already fixed what the field asked; the 2008 and 2017 studies that had the traces asked about sites and causes instead. (iii) An incentive gap: a collector can observe age almost free and phase or epoch only at a cost, so a phase- or epoch-conditioned hazard had no actionable use at the time the measurements were being taken — which removes the pull to measure it without making the fact about the heap any less true.
