## Restatement in my own words

Modern frontier models are built by burning a lot of compute that never touches a weight update: generating rollouts and scoring them with verifiers/reward models, generating synthetic training data, running a teacher model to distil from, and runs that were thrown away. The question is: if you write down an explicit rule for what counts as "the compute that made this model," and then do the sums for a list of models fixed in advance, (i) how big is that non-weight-update compute relative to the weight-update compute, (ii) does the ordering of models by "total" compute differ from the published ordering by "training compute," and (iii) how many models move across the EU's 10^25 FLOP systemic-risk line when you swap one defensible counting rule for another. Put bluntly: is the headline FLOP number a property of the model, or of the bookkeeping convention — and by enough to matter legally?

## My own arithmetic (labelled as mine, from P1's quoted figures)

Taking P1's point figures at face value — and P1 labels them *best guesses* with no intervals, so these carry no stated uncertainty and must not be read as P1's conclusions: B2/B1 for an R1-like model is ≈1.2 from RL alone (0.2 of pretraining), and ≈1.45 if a phi-4-like synthetic-data share (0.25) is added on top. **My arithmetic:** a model at 7e24 FLOP under B1 needs R ≥ 0.43 to reach 1e25, so the record's own illustrative "crosses without a further gradient step" works only at the top of that range and fails at R = 0.2. Conversely P1's projection that reasoning training *"could soon constitute the majority of total training compute"* implies R > 1, far above the Commission's 30% tolerance. So the existence claim is one paragraph of arithmetic over already-published numbers; the *sign for any given present-day model* is not.
