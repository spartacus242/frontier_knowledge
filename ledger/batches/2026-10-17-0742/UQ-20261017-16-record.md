Domain(s): Medicine — prehospital/resuscitation care and systems-of-care quality measurement (target field). Adjacent: clinical informatics / healthcare technology management (owns the offset), measurement-error statistics and time metrology (own the method).

Question (canonical — Gate 0 as submitted):
In registries and quality programmes that assemble each case's timeline from timestamps generated by independent clocks (CAD dispatch, monitor/defibrillator, EMS ePCR, ED triage, inpatient EHR, lab analyser), what is the distribution of inter-source clock offsets, and how much does that offset distribution attenuate or distort the estimated per-minute dependence of outcome on elapsed time from which time-critical-care targets are set?

Question (canonical — Gate 1 reformulation, the one permitted reformulation, for well-posedness only; both versions retained):
For a defined set of source pairs that jointly populate one case timeline in an out-of-hospital-cardiac-arrest or acute-stroke/STEMI registry (CAD server x monitor/defibrillator; monitor/defibrillator x ePCR; ePCR x ED triage; ED triage x inpatient EHR; EHR x lab analyser), (a) what is the signed offset of each source's clock from a traceable UTC reference, and hence the per-pair offset distribution, measured either against that reference at service/upload events or from events co-witnessed and independently logged by both sources; (b) what share of contributing sources is already traceably synchronised in routine operation; and (c) when a published per-minute outcome-vs-elapsed-time model is re-fitted by regression calibration or SIMEX under that measured error model, by how much do the per-minute slope, its confidence interval, and the implied benefit of a one-minute improvement change?
[The reformulation changes nothing substantive; it names the source pairs, the reference, and the estimand, so that limb (a) is separable from the documentation-latency error process the candidate itself distinguishes.]

Alternative formulations:
1. How wrong are the clocks that make up a cardiac-arrest or stroke timeline, and what does that error do to the per-minute survival decrement used to justify response-time targets?
2. Treating a time-critical-care timeline as a composite measurement made by several uncalibrated instruments, what is the measurement-error model for its timestamps, and what bias does it impose on estimated time-dependence and on the targets derived from it?

Motivating gap (as submitted, and as corrected by Gate 2):
As submitted: "Synchronisation is assumed as a precondition and the offset itself is never measured." VERIFIED at intake: Campbell JP et al., Ann Emerg Med 1995;26(6):697-701, doi 10.1016/S0196-0644(95)70040-4, links CAD and event-recording-defibrillator timestamps in 92 primary-VF patients and concludes only that synchronised clocks "may provide a method of accurately measuring the time elapsed"; no measured offset, no timing-error sensitivity analysis (abstract only — COULD NOT READ full text, paywalled).
**CORRECTED BY GATE 2: the "never measured" claim is false.** Brabrand et al. (Dan Med J 2012) measured ED and ambulance-defibrillator clock offsets against a traceable NIST reference and reported the distribution, the share within one minute, and the maximum inter-device spread; Srour & Badr (HEALTHINF 2017) quantified synchronisation-error prevalence inside one CAD over 24,391 call records; Goodwin et al. (Front Digit Health 2022) review the whole error class for clinical databases and cite a four-hospital audit of >1,700 clocks and >1,300 medical devices. What remains unmeasured is the per-source-pair offset distribution for the specific data systems that populate registry timelines, and the slope re-fit under it.

Presuppositions (status after Gate 2):
1. There is a single physical timeline per case, so a per-source offset is well defined — ESTABLISHED.
2. Contributing clocks are not already synchronised to a common traceable reference in routine operation — CONTESTED at intake, now PARTLY SETTLED IN THE CANDIDATE'S FAVOUR for human-read clocks and defibrillator clocks (Brabrand: 2/11 ED devices and 4/11 ambulance defibrillators within 1 minute; max inter-device spread 19 min 05 s), and SETTLED AGAINST the candidate for at least some CAD infrastructure (Srour & Badr's CAD ran NTP from a GPS source, albeit with a 24-hour sync period the authors call a worst case). The share synchronised is itself part of the answer, as the candidate says.
3. Offsets are recoverable retrospectively or prospectively — CONTESTED, unchanged. Brabrand recovered them prospectively by direct comparison to a reference, which shows the prospective design is feasible; nothing found shows the retrospective (logged clock-set / co-witnessed-event) route works.
4. Published per-minute time-dependence estimates are fitted on timestamps from more than one clock — PLAUSIBLE and now partly supported: Utstein-style concern about clock synchronisation is VERIFIED as quoted in two opened sources (Brabrand's introduction quotes the Utstein report, "Clock inaccuracy and lack of synchronization continue to be a problem"; Goodwin et al. discuss the same class). Not verified against a named registry data dictionary in this step.
5. Offsets are non-negligible relative to the minutes-scale effect — CONTESTED; Brabrand's figures (minutes, up to 19 min spread) support it for the device population he sampled; a null remains informative.
6. Offset is not caused by outcome, so the error is non-differential or its differentiality is modellable — CONTESTED, unchanged. Correctly separated from charting latency, which is a distinct and plausibly outcome-dependent error process.

What would count as an answer:
An empirically estimated offset distribution per source pair — from comparison of each source's clock to a traceable GPS/NTP reference at service or data-upload events, or from co-witnessed events logged independently by two sources (first shock on the defibrillator and on the ePCR; EMS hand-off and ED triage) — followed by a regression-calibration or SIMEX re-fit of a published time-dependence model under that error model, reporting the change in the per-minute slope, in its confidence interval, and in the implied benefit of a one-minute improvement; plus the share of contributing sources already traceably synchronised. A null (offsets small, slope unchanged) settles it equally well; a materially attenuated slope would mean response-time targets were calibrated on a biased estimate.

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, with sources I opened):

PRIOR 1 — Brabrand M, Hosbond S, Petersen DB, Skovhede A, Folkestad L. "Time telling devices used in Danish health care are not synchronized." Dan Med J 2012;59(10):A4512. https://content.ugeskriftet.dk/sites/default/files/scientific_article_files/2018-12/a4512.pdf (opened, full text).
- Unit of analysis: the individual time-telling device — 11 devices in two emergency departments (wall clocks, telephone clocks, defibrillator clocks) and 11 ambulance defibrillator clocks.
- Endpoint: signed clock offset, in mm:ss, from a traceable reference.
- Reference/comparator: the NIST/time.gov online time service (stated +/-0.2 s), converted to Danish time.
- Estimand: descriptive offset distribution (ED median -00:30, range -17:05 to +01:00; ambulance median reported -00:45, IQR -02:40 to -00:01, range -05:22 to +02:00), the share correct to within one minute (2/11 ED = 18.2%; 4/11 ambulance = 36.4%), and the maximum inter-device difference (19 min 05 s; 5 min 22 s between an ambulance defibrillator and an ED wall clock). No outcome estimand.
- Quoted: "the use of unsynchronized time telling devices in research introduces a systematic error and could lead to skewed results", alongside "Survival decreases with 7-10% per minute".

PRIOR 2 — Srour FJ, Badr NG. "Time Synchronization in Emergency Response Time Measurement." HEALTHINF 2017 (BIOSTEC), 199-204, doi 10.5220/0006096001990204. https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2017/60960/60960.pdf (opened, full text).
- Unit of analysis: the individual emergency call record (24,391 valid records, six months, one CAD system).
- Endpoint: presence and magnitude of logically impossible (negative) intervals, treated as the signature of synchronisation error, across three response intervals.
- Reference/comparator: none external; internal logical ordering of timestamps only — the authors state "the errors reported here potentially reflect only half of the errors actually present in the data".
- Estimand: prevalence of negative intervals (89.04%, 1.81%, 0.77% by interval) and the magnitude distribution of those errors (medians 70 s, 37 s, 30 s; maxima to 86,400 s, attributed to a daylight-saving transition).

PRIOR 3 — Goodwin AJ, Eytan D, Dixon W, Goodfellow SD, Doherty Z, Greer RW, McEwan A, Tracy M, Laussen PC, Assadi A, Mazwi M. "Timing errors and temporal uncertainty in clinical databases: a narrative review." Front Digit Health 2022;4:932599, doi 10.3389/fdgth.2022.932599. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2022.932599/full (opened, publisher route).
- Unit of analysis: the error mechanism (a taxonomy across clinical databases: inaccurate clocks, clock drift, sample-rate error, inferred time, algorithmic and network delay, human rounding and transcription, anonymisation time-shifts, time zones, nonstandard definitions, software bugs).
- Endpoint: enumeration and characterisation of mechanisms, plus recommendations for practice.
- Reference/comparator: none — it is a review; it relays device-level empirical figures from others (video-vs-EMR relative drift ~140 ppm; wearable sample-rate error up to 290 ppm; 900 ms ABP and 1,400 ms PPG display delays; an 8.9% start-after-end conformance failure rate in one audit; Goldman's >1,700 clocks and >1,300 devices at four hospitals, "inaccurate timekeeping was pervasive", no figures).
- Estimand: none; it estimates no effect. It says only that "Measurement errors in one or more variables can introduce a bias into both linear and non-linear regression techniques" and recommends "Employ techniques that incorporate temporal uncertainty into analysis."

SUPPORTING PRIOR (closest match to the candidate's slope limb) — Guo Y. "Effect of Time Measurement Error on Assessing Treatments with Time Dependent Effect." MS thesis, University of Washington Biostatistics, 2017 (advisor S. May). https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/f9d21b18-baf6-4f87-b94b-32d8552456ef/full (record opened).
- Unit of analysis: the simulated patient (ROC TXA Phase II ~1,000; CRASH-3 ~13,000), prehospital TBI.
- Endpoint: binary clinical outcome via logistic regression; evaluation metrics bias, power, type I error.
- Reference/comparator: placebo arms, with the error-free simulation as the comparison condition.
- Estimand: the treatment effect at time zero and the treatment x time-to-treatment interaction coefficient, under added error in time from injury to treatment (concludes a 0.5 h average error has little impact in the ROC trial; 1 h average error still leaves >80% power in the meta-analysis).

Significance — consequence: If offsets are large relative to minutes and non-differential, the fitted per-minute decrement is attenuated, so the true benefit of a one-minute improvement is *larger* than published, and response-time targets set on the published slope are miscalibrated in a knowable direction — with the uncomfortable corollary that systems were asked to buy ambulances against an understated marginal return. If offsets are small, or if modern NTP-synced infrastructure already covers the sources that write registry timestamps, the published slopes stand and the field can stop treating clock synchronisation as a standing caveat; that null is worth having, because the caveat has been recycled since the Utstein report without a number attached to it. A third outcome is live and would weaken the candidate: documentation latency, which the candidate correctly separates out, may dominate clock offset, in which case the answer redirects effort rather than correcting a slope.
Significance — audience: registry stewards and definition-writing bodies (ILCOR/Utstein writing groups, CARES, AHA Get With The Guidelines, national ambulance-service regulators) would act by either publishing a traceability requirement with an audit figure or dropping the caveat; healthcare technology management and clinical engineering, who already own the MD PnP synchronisation agenda, would gain an outcome-denominated argument for a sync budget expressed in survival rather than in compliance; measurement-error methodologists gain a named applied target. The audience is real and is already half-mobilised — which cuts both ways, since the half-mobilisation is itself the prior art.
Significance — fecundity: makes askable (i) whether offsets are differential by case severity through the device-use pathway rather than the charting pathway; (ii) whether a traceability requirement changes measured system performance or only measured-measured performance; (iii) whether the same correction changes the ranking of systems in public benchmarking, which is where the money is; (iv) whether registry-derived time-dependence slopes in other acute settings (sepsis bundles, trauma golden hour) inherit the same bias.
Significance — anchoring: the long-contested stability of the 7-10%-per-minute decrement and of the 8-minute response-time target; the Utstein report's own standing complaint that "clock inaccuracy and lack of synchronization continue to be a problem"; and Goodwin et al.'s explicit open recommendation to incorporate temporal uncertainty into analysis.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism): a disciplinary boundary with an incentive seam on top of it. The offset belongs to healthcare technology management, biomedical/clinical engineering and the device-interoperability community (MD PnP), who measure clocks and publish in trade and informatics venues; the slope belongs to resuscitation epidemiology, who fit models and publish in Annals/Circulation/Resuscitation; the correction belongs to measurement-error statistics. Nobody owns the composite, and the evidence shows exactly that dispersal: the one measured offset distribution in this setting sits in a national-language general medical journal (Danish Medical Journal), the one CAD-error quantification sits in a health-informatics conference proceedings, and the one simulation of the statistical consequence sits in an unpublished master's thesis — three literatures that do not cite each other. The incentive seam: performing the correction requires a registry to label its own flagship exposure variable as mismeasured, and would revise a number already embedded in national targets, so the party with the data has the least reason to ask.
