Question (canonical — Gate 1 reformulation, the version to carry forward):
Over the frame of species holding BOTH (a) at least one assembly flagged chromosome-level in a public archive, with a pseudo-chromosome count extracted under a pre-declared rule (chromosome-assigned units only; unlocalized scaffolds, organelles, alternate haplotypes excluded; sex-chromosome and B-chromosome treatment declared in advance) and (b) at least one published haploid chromosome number in a compiled karyotype resource (CCDB for plants, the coleoguy/karyotypes compilations for animal clades), matched by accepted name against one named taxonomic backbone after synonym resolution: what is the rate, signed direction and taxonomic structure of disagreement between the two counts; and when one chromosome-number evolution model is refitted on one fixed phylogeny per clade using assembly-derived counts in place of cytogenetic counts, by how much do the estimated polyploidization, demi-polyploidization, fission and fusion rates move, relative to the movement produced by perturbing cytogenetic counts at the measured error rate and direction alone?

Alternative formulations:
1. How often does a chromosome-level genome assembly contradict the karyotype of record for the same species, and in which direction does it err?
2. Is the chromosome-number data underlying chromosome-evolution models measurement-error-free, and what does realistic count error do to inferred rates of polyploidy and rearrangement?

Motivating gap:
EBP / DToL / VGP pipelines now emit chromosome-level assemblies at thousands-of-species scale, so a pseudo-chromosome count is for the first time an independent, high-throughput realization of haploid chromosome number for species that already carry a 20th-century karyotype of record. Chromosome-number evolution models (the ChromEvol lineage) are fitted to compiled cytogenetic counts and treat each count as observed without error. The only literature that interrogates those models tests MODEL adequacy, not DATA error (Rice & Mayrose 2021). Two further facts sharpen the gap, both established by reads in this Gate 2:
- Hjelmen 2024 (GENETICS) states in its Introduction the hope "that the resulting assembly contig/scaffold number aligns with the haploid chromosome count", and then uses the haploid count only as the denominator of an average-chromosome-size scaling term (PN50). The presupposition is named in print and not tested.
- Rice & Mayrose 2021 found the best-fitting model inadequate in a large share of 233 angiosperm datasets, and names in its Discussion that "possible sources of errors include those in the assumed phylogenetic hypothesis, in the collection of chromosome counts or in taxa sampling" — i.e. count-collection error is explicitly floated as a candidate explanation for those failures and left unexamined.

Presuppositions:
1. A chromosome-level assembly's pseudo-chromosome count is a measurement of haploid chromosome number. CONTESTED: assemblers collapse and split repeat arrays, sex chromosomes and B chromosomes are handled inconsistently, polyploids may or may not be phased, and curation targets may themselves be set from the literature karyotype (a circularity that the design must detect, not assume away — see Strongest remaining objection).
2. The cytogenetic count of record is retrievable and attributable to a species identification matching the assembly's voucher. CONTESTED for synonymy, cryptic species and intraspecific karyotype races.
3. Enough species carry both data types, with enough taxonomic spread, to estimate a discordance rate by clade. PARTLY SUPPORTED: Hjelmen 2024 analysed ~15,000 assemblies with genome-size estimates out of >40,000 eukaryotic assemblies, and combined 12,773 karyotype records for mammals, dipterans, coleopterans, amphibians and polyneopterans; Wright et al. worked with a few hundred chromosome-level Lepidoptera assemblies. The size of the INTERSECTION is not published and is itself part of limb (i)'s deliverable.
4. ChromEvol-class inference is sensitive to count error at realistic magnitudes. CONTESTED — must be demonstrated by perturbation, not assumed. Rice & Mayrose demonstrated sensitivity to MODEL inadequacy only.
5. Neither measurement is a gold standard: the estimand is discordance plus its propagation, not which source is right. (Stated to stop the question collapsing into "are old karyotypes wrong?")

What would count as an answer:
(i) A discordance table with explicit denominators, by clade, giving the share of species where the two counts differ, the signed direction (assembly below vs above karyotype), and a pre-specified adjudication subset in which a third method (FISH, optical mapping, Hi-C contact-map inspection, or fresh meiotic/pollen counts) resolves individual cases.
(ii) Refits of one chromosome-number model on one fixed phylogeny under cytogenetic versus assembly-derived counts, reporting the shift in polyploidization / demi-polyploidization / fission / fusion rate estimates with uncertainty, plus a simulation in which counts are perturbed at the measured error rate and direction to isolate how much rate bias that error alone produces.
A null — discordance rare and rates unmoved — settles it the other way and licenses pooling the two sources.

Significance — consequence:
If discordance is common and directional (the plausible prior is assembly-below-karyotype, since under-assembly dominates assembly error — Hjelmen found nearly half of assemblies short of their genome-size estimate, mostly from missing repetitive sequence, and collapsed arrays and unplaced sex chromosomes both subtract units), then the two sources are not interchangeable and the direction is a bias, not noise. Downward-biased counts on recent tips, against an unbiased older record, mimic exactly the signal that fission/fusion and dysploidy models read as directional chromosome-number decrease; pooled or source-switched datasets would then carry a spurious trend. If instead discordance is rare and symmetric, the answer licenses pooling cytogenetic and assembly counts into one dataset — which materially enlarges the taxa that chromosome-number models can be fitted to, especially in animal clades where cytogenetic coverage is thin — and it retires count error as an explanation for the Rice & Mayrose adequacy failures, strengthening the case that those failures are genuinely about model structure. Either way the answer is actionable, which is what distinguishes it from novel trivia: a null result here is a licence, not a shrug.

Significance — audience:
(a) Chromosome-number model developers (the ChromEvol / ChromoSSE / chromePlus line: Mayrose and Rice; Freyman & Höhna; Blackmon) would gain or lose a reason to add an observation layer for count error, the same way sequence models carry one for sequencing error; (b) curators of compiled count resources (CCDB, the coleoguy/karyotypes compilations) would gain a per-record concordance flag and a provenance field distinguishing cytogenetic from assembly-derived counts, which they currently do not need to distinguish; (c) assembly-curation teams at EBP / DToL / VGP would gain a quantified expectation for how often a curated pseudo-chromosome count should be expected to differ from the literature karyotype, which is directly operational — curation currently treats a mismatch as a flag to investigate, with no published base rate to calibrate against; (d) the polyploidy / WGD macroevolution community, whose rate estimates are the downstream quantity.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: what is the right observation model for a count measured by two instruments with different, asymmetric failure modes; does assembly-derived count error correlate with repeat content, genome size, heterochromatin fraction or sequencing platform (all available from Hjelmen's covariate set), making it predictable and hence correctable; can the Rice & Mayrose adequacy failures be partitioned into model-structure and data-error components; does the same two-instrument audit change conclusions in the adjacent compiled-trait literatures (genome size, ploidy level, sex-determination system) that share the same architecture of a 20th-century compiled record now being re-measured at scale; and the reflexive one — how often is a curated assembly's chromosome count itself set FROM the literature karyotype, which if common makes the two measurements non-independent and is worth knowing in its own right.

Significance — anchoring:
Anchors on (1) the Rice & Mayrose 2021 finding that the best-fitting ChromEvol model is often inadequate across 233 angiosperm datasets, an unexplained anomaly for which they themselves list count-collection error among the candidate causes; (2) Hjelmen 2024's demonstration that assembly metrics routinely fail against an independent measurement of a different genome quantity, together with its untested expectation about scaffold-vs-karyotype alignment; (3) the Lepidoptera result that fusions are concentrated in particular lineages beyond a uniform null — a lineage-heterogeneity claim whose robustness to count error is unexamined.

Reason for silence:
Three mechanisms, compounding. (i) NEW INSTRUMENT: the intersection the question is defined on barely existed before about 2020 and became large only with EBP/DToL/VGP output in 2022–2025, so for most of the history of chromosome-number modelling there was only one measurement system and the question was not formulable. (ii) DISCIPLINARY BOUNDARY with no shared key: the cytogenetic record lives in taxon-specific compilations keyed to accepted names, the assembly record in sequence archives keyed to accessions and vouchers; joining them requires a synonym-resolution step that belongs to neither community, and the one person who built both halves of the join (Hjelmen) needed only the quotient, not the difference. (iii) INCENTIVE ASYMMETRY, the sharpest of the three: in a genome paper the karyotype is cited as CORROBORATION of the assembly, so a match is reported and a mismatch is read as an assembly defect and resolved during curation rather than published as a data point — which systematically converts the candidate's observations into private curation decisions. Symmetrically, comparative-methods papers inherit counts from a database and have no instrument-level stake in them. The result is a quantity that every participant encounters individually and no participant is positioned to tabulate. Note the aggravating possibility that this same incentive makes some assembly counts non-independent of the karyotype record, which is an objection to the design and simultaneously evidence for the silence mechanism.

Strongest remaining objection (to hand to Gate 4):
Measurement non-independence by construction. If assembly curators consult the literature karyotype when deciding how many pseudo-chromosomes to emit — and the incentive structure above says some do — then the two "independent" measurements are partly the same measurement, concordance is inflated toward agreement, and the measured discordance rate estimates curation practice rather than measurement error. The design must establish independence per record (from curation logs, assembly notes, or the subset of assemblies whose species had no karyotype at assembly time) before any discordance rate means what limb (i) claims. A secondary objection: limb (ii) may be unfalsifiable in the direction of interest if the intersection, once restricted to clades with a usable phylogeny, is too small or too phylogenetically clumped to refit any model — making the perturbation simulation the only real content of limb (ii), which weakens it from a refit to a sensitivity analysis.
