## What the sources actually say

(1) AIN 1996 — CITATION CONFIRMED EXACTLY, title is near-verbatim as reported. Crossref (https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/j.1875-9114.1996.tb03039.x) and OpenAlex (https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title.search:restricting%20levothyroxine%20dosage%20strength) both return: Ain KB, Pucino F, Csako G, Wesley RA, Drass JA, Clark C, Ketteridge P, Crawford K, Banks SM, Dorworth TE. "Effects of Restricting Levothyroxine Dosage Strength Availability." Pharmacotherapy: The Journal of Human Pharmacology and Drug Therapy, 1996;16(6):1103-1110, DOI 10.1002/j.1875-9114.1996.tb03039.x. PMID 8947984 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8947984/). Independently corroborated by the ACNP journal index (https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/273195), which gives the identical volume/issue/pages. CONTENT: a randomized trial in which 33 endocrinologists were assigned to a restrictive vs. non-restrictive levothyroxine dosage-strength formulary, covering 241 outpatients at a federal research hospital. Measured: thyroid function tests, clinic visits, compliance/regimen complexity, cost. Reported conclusion, quoted: "Restriction of levothyroxine's dosage strength did not significantly alter therapeutic outcomes." Restriction was associated with more complex dosing regimens and no significant cost savings; the authors note effects on non-specialist care are unknown and call for prospective studies before wide adoption. CAVEAT ON MY SOURCING: I could not open Wiley, PubMed or PMC directly (reCAPTCHA / client errors), so the abstract wording above comes from Crossref and OpenAlex metadata read through WebFetch; the quoted sentence appears in both, but I did not see the publisher's own abstract page. The bibliographic record itself is verified from three independent indexes.

(2) ONTARIO DELISTINGS — CONFIRMED, WITH A DATE CORRECTION. The delisting was announced 20 July 2016 and IMPLEMENTED 31 January 2017; 2018 and 2019 are the publication years of the two evaluations, not the intervention dates. Delisted from the public formulary for non-palliative prescribers: 75 and 100 mcg/h fentanyl patches, 24 and 30 mg hydromorphone controlled-release capsules, 200 mg morphine sustained-release tablets. Study A: Guan Q, Khuu W, Martins D, Tadrous M, Chiu M, Do MT, Gomes T. "Evaluating the early impacts of delisting high-strength opioids on patterns of prescribing in Ontario." Health Promot Chronic Dis Prev Can. 2018;38(6):256-262, doi:10.24095/hpcdp.38.6.07 (https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/phac-aspc/documents/services/publications/health-promotion-chronic-disease-prevention-canada-research-policy-practice/vol-38-no-6-2018/ar-08-eng.pdf). Quoted: "We observed a 98% decrease in the total number of publicly funded recipients of high-strength opioids" (5,930 to 133, Dec 2016-Jul 2017); "The policy led to a significant decline in the total volume of all three opioids dispensed" (hydromorphone -17%, p=.008; morphine -17%, p=.028; fentanyl -39%, p=.007); "this reduction generally corresponded to an increase in the number of low-strength opioids dispensed" (50 mcg/h fentanyl patches +~76%). Study B: Martins D, Khuu W, Tadrous M, Juurlink DN, Mamdani MM, Paterson JM, Gomes T, on behalf of the Ontario Drug Policy Research Network. "Impact of delisting high-strength opioid formulations from a public drug benefit formulary on opioid utilization in Ontario, Canada." Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf. 2019;28(5):726-733, doi:10.1002/pds.4764 (https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1633953; https://odprn.ca/research/publications/impact-of-delisting-high-strength-opioid-formulations-from-a-public-drug-benefit-formulary-on-opioid-utilization/). Primary outcome was weekly median daily dose in MME, Aug 2016-Jul 2017, interrupted time series stratified by palliative status: immediate 10 MME/week drop among publicly funded non-palliative patients, 3.9 MME/week gradual decline among palliative, and only 0.7 MME/week gradual decline when all prescriptions regardless of payer were included. Quoted: "Although observed dose reductions of less than 1 MME weekly are likely not clinically relevant," safety implications "require further monitoring." A third, related paper measures tapering: Martins D et al., "Impact of changes in opioid funding and clinical policies on rapid tapering of opioids in Ontario, Canada," Pain (online Sept 2021 / Jan 2022 issue) — transient rise in rapid tapering (1.4% to 2.3% peak) around the delisting. IMPORTANT LIMIT: all three measure PRESCRIBING AND DISPENSING, not patient clinical outcomes. Guan et al. explicitly call for future patient-level outcome studies, and I found no published study of pain control, overdose, ED visits or mortality attributable to the Ontario strength delisting.

(3) VARIANCE SHARE — NOT COMPUTED ANYWHERE I COULD FIND, IN ANY DRUG CLASS. Across 23 queries I found no variance decomposition, R-squared, ICC or any other partition of prescribed or dispensed daily dose into (a) rounding onto the marketed-strength lattice versus (b) variance in the underlying intended dose. Closest adjacent work, none of which does this: (i) Zhang L, Hu Y, Pan P, Hong C, Fang L. "Estimated Manipulation of Tablets and Capsules to Meet Dose Requirements for Chinese Children: A Cross-Sectional Study." Front Pediatr. 2021;9:747499, doi:10.3389/fped.2021.747499 — quoted: "17,123 (22%) of the total 78,366 tablet or capsule prescriptions were assessed as requiring manipulation," and ~20% of those manipulations would have been avoidable had the lowest nationally approved strengths been available. This is the nearest existing estimate of the fraction of orders unreachable on marketed strengths, but it is a count of prescriptions, is pediatric and single-jurisdiction, and the paper reports only counts, percentages, medians and IQRs — no variance partitioning or regression. (ii) "Practicality of 3D Printed Personalized Medicines in Therapeutics" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8072378/) states only qualitatively that "required strengths/doses of solid dosage forms for specific patients may not be available," with no count or percentage attached. (iii) The chemotherapy dose-rounding-to-nearest-vial-size literature (e.g. Support Care Cancer 2005, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00520-004-0606-5) quantifies cost and waste consequences of rounding, not the share of dose variance it creates.

## Effect on the ledger verdict

The "disguised known" verdict should NOT stand as applied to the whole question. It holds for one limb and fails for the other, and the two must be routed separately.

LIMB 2 (does discontinuing/restricting a strength move patient outcomes) — the verdict stands, with a qualification. Ain 1996 is real, the title is essentially as reported, and it is a randomized trial that measured patient outcomes (thyroid function tests) under a restricted strength formulary and found no significant change. The strength-removal natural experiment has also genuinely been run in Ontario and published twice. QUALIFICATION: the Ontario studies measured dispensing and dose trajectories, not patient clinical outcomes, and Guan et al. themselves call for patient-level outcome work. So "discontinuing a single strength moves patient outcomes" is answered for levothyroxine in a specialist RCT and unanswered for opioids at the clinical-outcome level. Also note the claim's framing is slightly off: Ain restricted a SET of strengths rather than discontinuing one, and the Ontario intervention removed five products at once, so neither is cleanly a single-strength removal.

LIMB 1 (variance share attributable to the strength lattice) — the verdict does NOT stand. This quantity has not been computed in any drug class I can find. Calling the full question a disguised known collapses an answered limb and an unanswered one.

ON THE ROUTING DISTINCTION YOU ASKED FOR — the variance-share limb is closer to (b), a question nobody has posed, but it is a weak (b) and I want the weakness on the record. What IS in print: the underlying phenomenon is explicitly named as a problem (the 3D-printing and scored-tablet literature states that required strengths "may not be available"), and the adjacent, coarser version of the question — what fraction of prescriptions cannot be filled on the marketed lattice — has been both posed AND descriptively answered (22% in Zhang 2021). What I could NOT find anywhere: the variance-share formulation posed as a question, named as a parameter to estimate, or listed as a research gap or future-work item. So this is not a question asked in print and left hanging; but neither is it a new object of inquiry. It is a methodological restatement, as a variance decomposition, of a documented and partly quantified phenomenon. Route it as "novel framing of a known phenomenon," not as "open question in the literature" and not as "disguised known."

CONFIDENCE CAVEAT: the limb-1 finding is an absence-of-evidence result from 23 queries using public search, Crossref and OpenAlex. I was blocked from PubMed, PMC, Wiley, Semantic Scholar and Europe PMC's API (reCAPTCHA, HTTP 429 and client errors), so I could not run a controlled MeSH or field-restricted bibliographic search. A null on a variance-decomposition phrasing is exactly the kind of result that a proper indexed search could overturn; treat "nobody has posed it" as not-found rather than established.
