Domain(s): Biology — preclinical research methods / laboratory animal science (neighbours: behavioural ecology/capture-recapture, animal breeding temperament scoring, clinical-trial allocation methodology)

Question (canonical):
In laboratory rodent experiments, is the order in which animals are removed from a cage a repeatable individual trait that predicts behavioural and physiological phenotype, such that the widespread practice of assigning animals to groups in the order they are caught induces between-group differences comparable in size to the treatment effects such experiments report?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — the one permitted reformulation, used only to pin the decisive thresholds; both versions retained):
For cage-mate groups of individually marked laboratory mice or rats caught by a standard protocol on >=5 separate days: (i) what is the within-individual repeatability (ICC, with 95% CI) of catch rank within cage; (ii) conditional on cage as a random effect, what is the per-rank coefficient for each pre-specified endpoint (open-field distance, plasma or faecal corticosterone, body-weight trajectory slope, glucose AUC); and (iii) does allocation in catch order, versus catch order randomised, produce group-mean shifts whose standardised magnitude is at least 0.5x the median standardised effect reported in a pre-specified sample of published studies using those same endpoints? "Yes" = ICC lower CI bound > 0 AND criterion (iii) met; "no" = a null at >=90% power on either (i) or (iii).

Alternative formulations:
- Is "easy to catch" a phenotype, and does allocating mice to arms in catch order quietly un-randomise preclinical experiments?
- How much of the between-group difference in a rodent study is explained by the order in which animals were lifted out of the cage rather than by the treatment?

Motivating gap (as submitted, and as corrected by Gate 2):
Submitted: preclinical randomisation is treated as satisfied once a random sequence is applied to animal IDs or cages, which presupposes that the physical step between cage and sequence — reaching in and catching the next animal — is itself random; that step is uncontrolled, essentially never reported, and ARRIVE 2.0 asks how animals were allocated, not how they were selected out of the cage. Two literatures were said to bracket the gap: ecological measurement of individual heterogeneity in capture probability (trappability, "trap-happy/trap-shy"), and laboratory evidence that handling METHOD alters anxiety-like measures (tunnel vs tail handling; Hurst & West, Nature Methods 2010 — FROM MEMORY, NOT VERIFIED IN THIS GATE 2; no query was spent on it).
CORRECTION FROM GATE 2 (load-bearing): the premise that the field has not posed this is false in its qualitative form. The ARRIVE 2.0 Explanation and Elaboration text for Item 4 (Randomisation) states, in the field's own voice: "Selecting an animal 'at random' (i.e., haphazardly or arbitrarily) from a cage is not statistically random", and attributes the resulting bias to the researcher "(consciously or subconsciously) mak[ing] judgements in allocating an animal to a particular group", with allocation concealment named as the remedy. So the concern is explicitly on the record in the candidate's own flagship reporting guideline. What remains unmeasured is the quantitative content: no repeatability estimate for catch rank, no endpoint regression on catch rank, and no comparison of the induced group-mean shift against the published effect-size distribution. The guideline also frames the bias as experimenter judgement rather than as a repeatable ANIMAL trait imposing a deterministic order — that trait framing is the candidate's only genuinely distinct element.

Presuppositions:
1. Catch order can be recorded without itself perturbing the phenotype — low risk; needs individual marking plus a log.
2. CONTESTED AND LOAD-BEARING: catch rank has non-zero within-individual repeatability across days. Gate 2 found a direct negative from the neighbouring field: Jolly et al. 2019 (Behav Ecol Sociobiol 73:129) found repeatable boldness and emergence latency in a wild rodent yet "neither boldness nor emergence latency affected detection probability" and "detection probability did not vary detectably between individuals". Capture heterogeneity is therefore not a safe import; it is the thing most likely to be zero. If ICC is indistinguishable from zero the question collapses.
3. CONTESTED: allocation in catch order is actually common practice. Undocumented. Not establishable from the literature, because the step is not reported; ARRIVE's E&E implies it is common enough to warn against, which is weaker than evidence. Careful labs already do the opposite: Varholick et al. 2019 report randomising and counterbalancing within-cage individual order.
4. The endpoints have enough between-individual variance for trait-ordered allocation to move group means detectably. Partly supported: Varholick et al. 2019 give dominance-related R^2 of 0.02-0.11 (males) and 0.01-0.17 (females) for within-cage variation, with no dominance association for faecal glucocorticoid metabolites or elevated-plus-maze measures — so the physiological endpoints may carry less exploitable between-individual signal than the behavioural ones.
5. Catch-order-associated differences reflect a stable individual trait (boldness/docility/body condition) rather than only the transient stress of being caught later. A transient mechanism is independently documented: the "cohort removal" literature (Front Behav Neurosci 2016, 10:99, "Cohort removal induces changes in body temperature, pain sensitivity, and anxiety-like behavior" — surfaced in search, not opened) shows that removing cage-mates changes the remaining animals. Design must separate trait from this.
6. Not presupposed: that the effect is large. The question is explicitly comparative against published effect sizes on the same endpoints.

What would count as an answer:
Three pre-registered components. (i) Repeatability: ICC with CI for catch rank across >=5 days in individually marked cage-mates, mixed model with cage and day random effects, cage size reported (rank has few levels at n=3-5/cage, so analyse as ordinal and as first-caught vs last-caught). (ii) Prediction: regression of each pre-specified endpoint on catch rank with cage as random effect, power stated in advance, with a design arm that distinguishes stable trait from transient removal-order stress (e.g. all animals caught and held before any procedure, versus procedures run in catch order). (iii) Magnitude: by simulation over the fitted coefficients, or by direct experiment contrasting catch-order-ordered with catch-order-randomised allocation, compare induced group-mean shifts against the standardised effect distribution in a pre-specified sample of published studies using those endpoints. "Yes" requires ICC clearly above zero AND shifts of the magnitude set in the reformulation; "no" is a well-powered null on either leg. A practice survey alone is not an answer. Note that criterion (iii) has an existing empirical benchmark at the aggregate level: Hirst et al. 2014 (PLoS ONE 9:e98856, "The need for randomization in animal trials: an overview of systematic reviews" — surfaced in search, not opened) relates non-randomisation to effect-size inflation, which bounds the plausible answer from above without resolving the catch-order channel.

Novelty — how this differs from each:
vs 1 (ARRIVE E&E): the guideline poses the hazard and stops. It gives no repeatability estimate for catch rank, no endpoint regression, and no magnitude comparison against published effects; and it locates the bias in the experimenter's judgement rather than in a repeatable animal trait that imposes an order even on an experimenter with no preferences. The candidate's distinct content is the trait claim plus the three quantities. But the question "could the way animals are taken out of the cage un-randomise the experiment?" is NOT un-asked: it is asked and answered-by-assertion in the field's own guidance, which is why novelty is graded Low below.
vs 2 (Schipper 2025): overlaps limb (ii) for behavioural endpoints, but its order variable is testing order within cage (an experimenter-assigned sequence) rather than catch rank as an animal trait; it reports no repeatability of order across days and no comparison against the published effect-size distribution. It is nevertheless strong evidence that order-within-cage moves behavioural outcomes, which removes much of the surprise from limb (ii).
vs 3 (Varholick 2019): same target quantity (within-cage between-individual variance that an allocation rule could exploit) but a different, test-measured trait (dominance rank), and it demonstrates that the candidate's "free fix" (randomising within-cage order) is already standard in careful labs. It supplies the variance magnitudes limb (iii) would need, and its null for glucocorticoid metabolites weakens the physiological half of the candidate's endpoint set.
vs the Jolly 2019 neighbour: that paper tests the candidate's load-bearing presupposition in the wild and finds against it — repeatable personality without detectable individual heterogeneity in capture probability. The candidate's setting differs (seconds-long hand capture in a 500 cm2 cage versus hours-long baited trapping), which is exactly why the lab measurement is not entailed by the ecological one; but the direction of the nearest evidence is unfavourable.

Significance — consequence:
If yes (ICC clearly > 0 and shifts >= 0.5x median published effect): the physical selection step becomes a reportable item with a mandated fix — catch all animals in a cage before assigning any, or randomise catch order — and a slice of existing between-group differences in the preclinical literature becomes uninterpretable in a specific, checkable way rather than as generic "non-randomisation". It also converts an aggregate finding (non-randomised animal studies report larger effects) into an identified mechanism, which is what makes a bias fixable rather than merely deplored.
If no (well-powered null on either leg): it retires a plausible-sounding worry that currently sits in guidance without evidence, and tells guideline bodies not to add a reporting item. That is a real decision too: reporting checklists have a length budget, and items with no measured effect crowd out ones with measured effects. A null on limb (i) with a positive limb (ii) would have a third consequence: order effects would be transient-stress effects (the cohort-removal channel), fixed by holding time rather than by allocation.

Significance — audience:
(a) Guideline and funder bodies — NC3Rs/ARRIVE, EQUATOR, journal editors running checklists: they would either add "how animals were selected from the cage" to Item 4 or explicitly rule it out; the ARRIVE E&E already asserts the hazard, so they are on record as caring. (b) Animal facility managers and technicians writing SOPs: the fix changes a daily procedure (catch-then-allocate rather than catch-as-you-allocate) at near-zero cost. (c) Behavioural neuroscience and metabolic phenotyping labs, where the named endpoints live and where within-cage order is already known to matter (Schipper 2025). (d) Systematic reviewers and risk-of-bias assessors (SYRCLE tool users) who need to know whether "randomised" in a methods section is worth anything without the selection step. Weakness to concede: the audience's action if yes is one they have already been told to take, so the marginal behaviour change is compliance pressure, not new knowledge of what to do.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: is catch rank heritable or strain-dependent (does it differ between C57BL/6J and BALB/c, which would make it a confound in strain comparisons)? Does catch rank predict dominance rank, and is it a cheaper proxy than the tube test? Does catch rank predict survival/attrition in long studies, which would make it an informative-censoring problem rather than an allocation problem? Does the same logic apply to larger animals (which pig walks to the gate first; which macaque presents first) where the order is even more clearly behavioural? And the general form: which other uncontrolled physical steps sit between a random sequence and the animal (which tube the technician grabs, which cage is at eye level), i.e. an audit of the gap between a randomisation list and the bench.
Anchoring: the preclinical reproducibility crisis and the specific finding that non-randomised animal studies report larger effects (Hirst et al. 2014, unopened); the capture-heterogeneity problem in capture-recapture ecology, where individual heterogeneity in detection biases abundance estimates and is actively contested (Jolly et al. 2019 against, the woylie and technique-bias papers for); allocation-concealment subversion in clinical trials, where the analogous "the clinician learned the next allocation" failure mode is documented and quantified; and the within-cage phenotypic-variation literature (Varholick 2019), which is the variance budget any answer must draw from.

Reason for silence:
Not "no one thought of it" — they did, in print, in ARRIVE's own explanation. The credible mechanism is a guideline-closure incentive gap: once a reporting guideline asserts a bias and prescribes a free remedy, the empirical magnitude becomes unrewarding and mildly unwelcome to measure. A positive result only confirms what the checklist already says; a negative result reads as arguing against good practice; neither is publishable in a high-visibility venue, and no funder has a programme for it. Two further mechanisms compound it: a disciplinary boundary — the people who measure capture heterogeneity rigorously (behavioural ecologists, using capture-recapture models) have no stake in laboratory allocation, and the people allocating animals do not read Wildlife Research; and a documentation boundary — catch order lives in technicians' tacit practice and is absent from methods sections, so it cannot be studied retrospectively from the literature at all, only prospectively with individual marking and a log, which is a cost with no paper at the end.
