## Restatement in my own words

Take a large group of mice that are all the same genotype and all raised the same way, and count every cell in one organ (the brain), broken down region by region. Now look across animals. When one region happens to come out low, does another come out high — so that the whole-organ total is steadier than the regions are? Concretely: is the across-animal variance of the organ total smaller than the sum of the across-animal variances of its regions, after you strip out the part of the wobble that is the measuring instrument rather than the mouse; and does whichever way that comes out repeat in a second line and under a second way of cutting the organ into parts?

Algebraic note (my own algebra, stated so the attacks below can be checked): Var(T) − Σ_i Var(X_i) = 2·Σ_{i<j} Cov(X_i,X_j). So the candidate's primary criterion is exactly "is the MEAN PAIRWISE COVARIANCE between parts negative." Merging parts i and j into one part changes the statistic by exactly −2·Cov(X_i,X_j); equivalently, SPLITTING a part moves the statistic by +2·Cov of the two pieces. Direction matters for attack 4 and the record states it backwards (see below).

### Spot-check of the record's cited priors

PRIOR 1 (Herculano-Houzel et al. 2015, Front Neuroanat 9:64) — REAL, and the record's quotations are accurate. Verified verbatim from F1: "We analyzed the brain and spinal cord of 19 male mice of the Swiss variety, a non-isogenic strain"; "significant positive correlations between numbers of neurons in the cerebellum and in the olfactory bulb" (rho = 0.4734, p = 0.0071); "between the cerebral cortex and spinal cord" (rho = 0.3796, p = 0.0321); "a negative correlation between numbers of neurons in the rest of brain and in the spinal cord (rho = -0.4820, p = 0.0060"; "No other significant correlations were found between numbers of neurons in different structures across individuals"; "numbers of neurons are regulated and allocated independently to different brain structures"; "we found no evidence of compensatory changes in the distribution of neurons across brain structures." Max/min ratios confirmed: cortex 1.77x, cerebellum 2.90x, olfactory bulb 4.33x, remaining areas 3.48x, spinal cord 2.24x, whole brain (excl. OB) 1.63x. Also found, and NOT in the record: "variation in numbers of neurons across individuals actually exceeds variation in brain mass in each CNS structure" and the structures have "larger coefficients of variation (CV) than structure mass" — so the paper does engage CVs of parts explicitly.

Unit of analysis: individual mouse. Endpoint: absolute neuron/non-neuron number per CNS structure, all structures measured in the SAME brain. Comparator: across-individual, between-structure, within one commonly reared stock. Estimand: sign structure of between-structure across-individual count covariance, plus the total's range against the parts'. All four MATCH the candidate except that the cohort is, in the paper's own words, "a non-isogenic strain."

PRIOR 2 (Elkind et al. 2023, eLife 12:e82376) — REAL, record's quotations accurate. Verified verbatim from F2: "Cell count estimates in the right and left hemispheres served as a proxy for technical noise"; "If the average difference in cell count between hemispheres was higher than 15.5% of the total cell count for that region, we excluded the case from downstream analysis"; "33 regions were omitted due to right-left differences"; "The median male C57BL/6J mouse brain contained a total of 76 ± 11 × 10^6 cells"; "Yet, inter-individual variability was always greater than the effect size of a single qualifier"; and on the embedding, "each region is characterized by a vector of 204 components, each representing its density across one brain", "The density-based tSNE embedding map in Figure 6F reveals clear 2D 'clusters'". Two corrections to the record: (a) the region-to-region structure is computed on DENSITY vectors over 204 brains, not on counts, so the record's "they computed the across-mouse region-region correlation matrix [of counts] and spent it on a tSNE" overstates what is there; (b) the paper reports NO regional CV or regional SD of cell count across mice, so the candidate's statistic cannot be assembled from the published summary statistics alone — only from the underlying table. Also found: "the standard deviation across individuals was ~10 million cells (for C57BL/6J male alone)", giving CV(total) ≈ 13-14.5% (MY arithmetic from their stated mean/median 76 × 10^6 and SD 10-11 × 10^6; the Results "±" is not defined as SD in the text, so this carries their ambiguity).

PRIOR 3 (Johansen et al. 2023) — not re-checked; the record grades it a cleared near-miss and nothing in my sweep contradicts that. Its proportion endpoint is on the simplex, so it cannot bear on an absolute-total question either way.
