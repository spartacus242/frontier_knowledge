## 1. Restatement in my own words (not the generator's framing)

Pick a population of built assets *before* looking at the data — one whose register you can enumerate unit by unit, with a date each unit went into service, a date (or a reconstructible date) it came out, and some record of why it came out. Then answer three things.

1. **How long do they actually last?** Not the average age of the ones that happen to be gone, but a proper survival estimate — median, quartiles, and the probability a unit survives to the service life its design code declared — with units that entered the register mid-observation (left truncation) and units still standing (right censoring) handled, and the denominator written down.
2. **Why do they leave?** Split removals into "the thing the design life is about" (would more structural resistance have stopped this removal?) versus everything else, under a stated rule rather than a hand-written list; report the share in each non-structural class, how often two coders agree on a sub-sample, and the share nobody can classify.
3. **So what, for the code?** Take the code's target reliability, which is stated over a nominal reference period (50 or 100 years), and integrate it over the *realized* life distribution instead. Report the change in implied reliability index. Then do it again under the opposite reading of what a reference period means — that it is only a normalising convention for the load statistics, not a claim about how long the thing stays up — where the answer should be "no change."

In one line: *do assets leave service for the reason the code's design life is about, do they get as far as that life, and does the code's safety arithmetic change if you feed it the real exit times?*
