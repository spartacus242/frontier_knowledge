### Abstract, reconstructed
Reconstruction from **10 ordered consecutive segments**; junctions verified by grammatical continuity only (segments were returned without overlap), so treat word-level fidelity at the seams as high-confidence, not certain.

> "High administrative costs in U.S. health care have provoked concern among policymakers over potential waste, but many of these costs are generated by managed care policies that trade off bureaucratic costs against reductions in moral hazard. We study this trade-off for prior authorization restriction policies in Medicare Part D, where low-income beneficiaries are randomly assigned to default plans. Beneficiaries who face restrictions on a drug reduce their use of it by 26.8%. Approximately half of marginal beneficiaries are diverted to another related drug, while the other half are diverted to no drug. These policies generated net financial savings, reducing drug spending by $96 per beneficiary-year (3.6% of drug spending), while only generating approximately $10 in paperwork costs. Revealed preference approaches suggest that the cost savings likely exceed beneficiaries' willingness to pay for foregone drugs."

### What the paper measures (its own unit, endpoint, comparator, estimand)
- **Unit of analysis:** the beneficiary–drug(–region–year) cell; beneficiaries are Medicare Part D **low-income subsidy (LIS)** enrollees in Parts A, B and D, excluding Medicare Advantage.
- **Endpoint:** use of the focal ("restricted") drug, then substitution across drugs, then drug spending, then a calibrated paperwork-cost figure, then a revealed-preference welfare comparison. §6 adds health outcomes, which the introduction reports as statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- **Comparator:** a beneficiary whose randomly assigned default plan **restricts** the drug versus one whose randomly assigned default plan **does not**, within drug × region × year.
- **Estimand:** the effect of facing a prior-authorization restriction on one's own use of that drug — an effect on **realised utilisation**, not on the fate of any request.

### 26.8%
Stated more than once, and the formulations differ in hedging:
- Abstract (flat, no citation, no hedge): "Beneficiaries who face restrictions on a drug reduce their use of it by 26.8%."
- Introduction, p. 2: "We estimate that prior authorization restrictions reduce the use of focal drugs by 26.8%." — note "focal drugs," the paper's own term.
- §3.3: "prior authorization reduces the use of the restricted drug by 26.8%," identified as the preferred specification, **Table 5 column (6)**. The summariser reports that this percentage is constructed by dividing the coefficient by a **reweighted control mean** — i.e. it is a derived percent change, not a directly estimated one.

I could not read Table 5, so I cannot carry the stated uncertainty (standard error / confidence interval) on 26.8%. **The ledger reports 26.8% as a bare point estimate with no uncertainty, and I was unable to supply the interval.** That is a gap in the ledger entry, not a contradiction.

### 28.9% — the one real framing problem
The paper's sentence, reconstructed from **consecutive segments**:

> "First, we find that prior authorization deters 28.9% (0.12 users per capita) of users from taking the restricted drug."

This is **not** a decomposition of the 26.8%. The two numbers are on different units: 26.8% is a regression-derived percent change in use (Table 5 col. 6); 28.9% is the simulated share of **users** deterred, expressed in **users per capita** (0.12), out of the §4.1 substitution/demand system (Table 6). A ledger line reading "28.9% attributed to deterrence" invites the reader to take 28.9 as a share *of* the 26.8 effect, or as the deterrence component of a decomposed total. The paper does not say that. **Reword to: "the simulation deters 28.9% of users (0.12 users per capita) from the restricted drug (§4.2, Table 6), a separate estimate on a different unit from the 26.8% utilisation effect."**

### 46.2% / 53.8%
The paper's own sentences, reconstructed from **consecutive segments**:

> "Second, of the 0.12 users deterred by prior authorization from taking a restricted drug, 46.2% of them substitute to an unrestricted drug and 53.8% substituted to no drug."

The ledger's framing — "of those deterred, 46.2% substituting and 53.8% taking no drug" — matches the paper's denominator exactly. Good. Two notes: the denominator is the **0.12 deterred users**, not all users and not all beneficiaries; and the abstract rounds the same split to "Approximately half ... while the other half," so the paper itself offers a hedged version and a precise version. The 46.2/53.8 version is the unhedged one and sits in §4.2 against Table 6. Neither carries a citation; both are the authors' own estimates. The tense slip ("substitute" / "substituted") is in the source — reproduced as served.

### Identification
Confirmed in substance. Abstract: "Medicare Part D, where low-income beneficiaries are randomly assigned to default plans." §3.1 fragments: "random assignment to default plans"; "default assignment is conditionally random within-market." The design instruments the enrollee's plan with the **restriction status of the randomly assigned default plan**, within drug, region and year; §3.2 is a first stage.

**Defect in the ledger's wording.** "Identification from randomly assigned **unrestricted** default plans" reads as though identification came from one arm. It comes from random assignment to default plans that **differ** in whether they restrict the focal drug — restricting plans and non-restricting plans are both arms of the comparison. Drop "unrestricted," or write "randomly assigned default plans that differ in whether they restrict the drug."

## (c) The two quoted strings

Both strings are **present and verbatim** in **§5, "Administrative Cost Burdens."** Both are **cut**, and one of the cuts matters.

### String 1: "no data on the steps in the bureaucratic process"
Whole sentence (returned complete in one segment, so this is not a reconstruction):

> "Unfortunately, we have no data on the steps in the bureaucratic process of prior authorization."

- **Clause before the cut:** "Unfortunately, we have" — the quoted fragment begins mid-predicate and is not a standalone sentence.
- **Clause after the cut:** "of prior authorization" — the dropped tail is the **object** of "process." The quoted fragment leaves "the bureaucratic process" unqualified; the paper says it lacks data on the steps of *the prior-authorization process specifically*.
- Both cuts are **narrowing, not reversing**. The fragment does not misstate the paper's meaning. But as given it is an ungrammatical fragment presented as the paper's words, so it should be quoted whole or with ellipses.
- Sentence immediately before the passage, reconstructed from 2 consecutive segments: "In this section, we attempt to quantify the size of that burden and compare it to the spending reductions caused by prior authorization restrictions."

### String 2: "nor can we compute rejection rates"
Whole sentence, reconstruction from **3 ordered consecutive segments**:

> "Therefore, unlike prior studies, we cannot directly estimate the cost of compliance from accounting data (Shi 2022) or revealed preference (Dunn et al. 2021); nor can we compute rejection rates."

- **Clause before the semicolon:** the paper's statement that, *unlike prior studies*, it cannot estimate compliance cost from accounting data (citing **Shi 2022**) or from revealed preference (citing **Dunn et al. 2021**). The quoted fragment severs the whole comparative frame, including both citations. The sentence is the paper positioning itself against named prior work; the fragment reads as a flat confession.
- **Sentence immediately after,** reconstruction from 4 ordered consecutive segments: "We therefore take an alternative approach, wherein we calibrate relevant parameters (per-application costs and rejection rates) and combine them with our demand system estimates to estimate the total paperwork burden generated by compliance with prior authorization restrictions."
- **This following sentence is the one the ledger most needs.** "Nor can we compute rejection rates" means *cannot compute from our own data*. The paper nonetheless **has rejection-rate numbers**: §5.1 is titled "Calibrating Application Costs and Rejection Rates" and calibrates them from the outside literature at **1.5% to 15%, plus a 0% case**. Quoting the fragment alone implies the paper is silent on rejection rates. It is not silent; it is non-observational.
- §5 also carries a third, independent statement of the same limitation: **"The number of requests is also unobserved."** The ledger records two strings; there are three.

**Reconstruction provenance, stated plainly:** sentence 1 returned whole (1 segment). Sentence 2 = 3 consecutive segments. Preceding sentence = 2 segments. Following sentence = 4 segments. ## (d) The point of the check

### (i) Provider-side abandonment before a request is submitted — **ABSENT. Not measured anywhere I could read.**

Say it plainly: the paper nowhere measures what fraction of prior-authorization-requiring clinical decisions are dropped on the provider side before any request reaches the payer. It cannot: it has no data on the steps of the process and the number of requests is unobserved. There is no request-level data of any kind.

The two nearest things, neither of which is that measurement:
1. **§4.2 mechanism discussion** — the paper raises, as an interpretation of its reduced-form effect, that some patients may give up on hitting a requirement at the pharmacy, "not returning to their provider to request that they complete the required paperwork." This is **patient-side** abandonment, offered as a candidate explanation for an estimated utilisation drop, not a measured quantity and not provider-side.
2. **Footnote 27** cites an outside **CoverMyMeds survey** for a 17%/83% prospective-vs-retrospective split. Cited from elsewhere, and about timing of review, not about abandonment.

**Per instruction (9):** the paper's §5 statements that it lacks data on the bureaucratic steps and cannot compute rejection rates are **not** a flag of the gap the ledger is about. They are the authors declaring the limits of their own claims data for a paperwork-cost calculation, framed from the payer/claims side. The paper does not pose provider-side pre-submission abandonment as an open question, and it should not be cited as having noticed the gap. It is an absence in the paper, and the paper is silent about it rather than pointing at it.

### (ii) Comparing insurers or plans by approval or denial rates — **ABSENT. Not done.**

Plans are compared, but on a different axis: by **how often they restrict a drug** (the summariser locates this at Figure 4), i.e. restriction *prevalence*, which is the paper's treatment variable. No approval rate, denial rate, or turnaround metric is computed per plan, per insurer, or at all from the authors' data. Rejection rates enter only as a **calibrated parameter range taken from outside studies** (§5.1), applied uniformly in the cost calculation — not estimated, not observed, not compared across plans. A §3.3 reference to "high prior authorization approval rates" is a claim from elsewhere that the authors push back against, not a measurement they produce.

### What it does instead
Estimates, from random assignment of LIS beneficiaries to Part D default plans, the effect of a drug being restricted in one's plan on (1) one's use of that drug (−26.8%, §3.3/Table 5 col. 6), (2) substitution to other drugs and to no drug (§4.1–4.2/Table 6), (3) drug spending ($96 per beneficiary-year, 3.6%), (4) a **calibrated** paperwork burden (~$10, §5 with rejection rates assumed at 0%/1.5%–15%), and (5) welfare by revealed preference (§6.1), plus health outcomes reported as indistinguishable from zero. The whole design runs on **realised fills in claims** — the outcome is whether a drug was dispensed, never what happened to a request.

## (e) Setting: Part D drugs; medical services only as background

**Confirmed, with one nuance worth carrying.**

- The treatment and every empirical estimate concern **prior authorization on prescription drugs in Medicare Part D**. Abstract: "prior authorization restriction policies in Medicare Part D." §2 "Setting & Data" is built on Part D Event files (claim-level outpatient prescription fills), public drug-level formulary data, the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File, plan election and plan characteristic files, Micromedex Red Book, SSR Health rebate estimates, and RxNorm.
- **Medical services and procedures are never a treatment the paper studies.** Surgeries, durable medical equipment and imaging appear as background in **§1.1 "Prior Authorization Restrictions in Practice"**, and footnote 5 mentions retrospective utilisation review for hospital and physician bundles. Background only.
- **Nuance:** the paper is not wholly drug-claims-only on the *data* side. The sample requires enrollment in "Medicare Parts A, B, and D, and not enrolled in Medicare Advantage," and the data are described as including "medical utilization for all beneficiaries outside of Medicare Advantage" — medical utilisation serves as **outcome** data for the §6 health analysis. So: medical claims are used as outcomes; prior authorization on medical services is not analysed. A ledger line saying the paper "does not cover medical services at all" would be slightly too strong; "does not study prior authorization on medical services or procedures" is exact.
- **Read limit here:** §6 and the appendix were truncated for me, so I confirmed the health outcomes only from the introduction's description (a case study of oral anticoagulants plus an aggregate link from prior-year drug exposure to patient-level outcomes, estimates indistinguishable from zero), not from §6 itself.

## Defects

1. **(b) "28.9% attributed to deterrence" misframes the number.** 28.9% is the simulated share of users deterred (0.12 users per capita, §4.2/Table 6) on a different unit from the 26.8% utilisation effect (§3.3/Table 5 col. 6). It is not a component of the 26.8%, and the ledger's wording invites exactly that reading. Reword.
2. **(b) "randomly assigned *unrestricted* default plans" is wrong about the design.** Identification comes from random assignment to default plans that differ in restriction status — restricting and non-restricting plans are both arms. Drop "unrestricted."
3. **(c) String 2's severed clause changes what the sentence does.** The full sentence is a comparative — "unlike prior studies, we cannot directly estimate the cost of compliance from accounting data (Shi 2022) or revealed preference (Dunn et al. 2021)" — and the *next* sentence says the authors calibrate rejection rates anyway (§5.1: 1.5%–15%, plus 0%). Quoted bare, "nor can we compute rejection rates" implies the paper has no rejection-rate numbers. It has calibrated ones. Carry the following sentence with the quote.
4. **(c) String 1 is an ungrammatical fragment cut on both sides**, dropping "Unfortunately, we have" and the object "of prior authorization." Meaning survives; the quotation should still be whole or elided.
5. **(c) A third statement of the same limitation is unrecorded:** "The number of requests is also unobserved." (§5). It is the most direct support for (d)(i) and the ledger omits it.
6. **(b) No uncertainty is carried on 26.8%** in the ledger, and I could not supply it — Table 5 was outside my read. The figure is also a derived percent change (coefficient over a reweighted control mean), not a directly estimated percentage.
7. **Table attributions in this note are second-hand.** Every "Table 5" / "Table 6" / "Figure 4" locator comes from the body text's own cross-reference; the tables themselves were not in my read. Do not treat them as table-verified.
8. **(a) Published-version status is open, not settled.** None listed on NBER and none found by search, but the authors circulate a full PDF at `zarekcb.github.io/PriorAuth_Web.pdf` that I did not fetch (cap), and the lead author now appears as "Zarek Brot," so a later version may differ in author name or content. Record as "no published version found," not "unpublished."
9. **(e) "Does not cover medical services at all" would overstate it.** Medical utilisation is in the data as outcomes for the §6 health analysis; what is absent is prior authorization *on* medical services as an object of study.
10. **(d) Do not credit the paper with noticing the gap.** Its §5 data-limitation sentences are about its own paperwork-cost calculation, not a flag that provider-side pre-submission abandonment is unmeasured. Instruction (9) applies: a source noting the absence of a different thing is not a flag of this gap.
