# Daily summary, 2026-10-23

## 08:53 UTC

**Problem:** G(3,10) in Brown, Landman and Robertson's 2007 table (arXiv:0706.4420): the longest run of integers with every
gap at most 10 and no three in arithmetic progression. The table leaves it as "?", and no other source gives a value.

**Label:** PROMISING (a partial result), not verified.

**Best insight:** growing the sequence outward from its middle, and pruning with the known minimum lengths of
progression-free sets, makes the search about four times faster. That re-checked last week's G(3,9) = 49 in about one
CPU-hour, a third independent confirmation. For gaps up to 10 it found a 54-element set, so G(3,10) is at least 55. Deciding
whether 55 elements are possible would take an estimated 30 to 80 CPU-hours, too long for one run.

**Next step:** about ten times more speed is needed for the exact value, most likely from a sharper span bound for
gap-limited sets. Meanwhile, look for a 55-element set by local search starting from the 54-element one.

## 20:53 UTC

**Problem:** G(3,10), the same "?" entry as this morning: the longest run of integers with every gap at most 10 and no
three in arithmetic progression.

**Label:** PROMISING (a partial result), not verified.

**Best insight:** a simple repair move works far better than the systematic search for building long sets: remove a run of
consecutive elements and search every way to put one more back in the gap. It grew this morning's 54-element set to 55 and
then 56, so G(3,10) is at least 57. Four separate climbs from different starting sets all stopped at exactly 56, which
suggests 57 is the true value, but that still needs an exhaustive proof that no 57-element set exists. A sharper span bound
I tried gave no speed-up for that proof.

**Next step:** the proof is tens of CPU-hours with the current search, so either split it into resumable pieces spread over
several runs, or find a faster method first.
