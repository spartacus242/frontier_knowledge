Question (canonical) — V0 as intaken: If multi-site clinical laboratory series are homogenized by the pairwise-reference breakpoint methods used for climate station records — contemporaneous other sites as reference series, laboratory method-change logs as station metadata — how many published secular trends in laboratory-measured quantities survive, and what fraction of each trend's magnitude is attributable to method-change breakpoints rather than to the population?

Question (canonical) — R1, the one Gate 1 reformulation, adopted for well-posedness: For a pre-specified frame of multi-site laboratory records (>=K contributing sites, >=15 years of record, and an analyte set restricted in advance to analytes with documented commutability across the method generations present — e.g. enzymatic creatinine, haemoglobin, albumin, total cholesterol), and a pre-registered list of published secular-trend claims resting on those analytes: (a) applying a pairwise-reference breakpoint procedure transplanted from climate-station homogenization (contemporaneous co-catchment sites as reference series; differenced series tested by SNHT / pairwise comparison / ACMANT-style detection), what breakpoints and offsets are detected per site-analyte; (b) against analyzer/method/calibrator change logs retrieved independently for the same site-analyte pairs, what are the sensitivity and specificity of detection and the agreement between estimated and documented offsets; and (c) on the homogenized record, what fraction of each published trend's magnitude is attributable to breakpoint offsets (with CIs), and how many of the pre-registered trends retain their sign and statistical significance?
[Both versions recorded per Gate 1. Alternative formulations:
1. (lay) How much of the long-term change we think we see in patients' blood test results is actually the laboratory's equipment changing?
2. (technical) Can a breakpoint-corrected, homogenized version of a multi-site decades-long laboratory record be constructed, and does it change the sign or magnitude of reported secular trends in analytes such as haemoglobin, creatinine/eGFR, albumin or total cholesterol?
3. (methodological, the narrow residue that survives Gate 2) Do contemporaneous co-catchment laboratories function as valid reference series for each other in the climatological sense — i.e. does the differenced series between two sites' same-analyte distributions isolate instrument change from population change well enough that blind detection matches independently retrieved method-change logs above chance?

Motivating gap: Climatology treats an un-homogenized station trend as uninterpretable and has a mature, effectively mandatory practice for repairing it: detect and correct breakpoints caused by instrument, site and observer changes using neighbouring stations as references (SNHT, pairwise comparison, ACMANT), with adjustment uncertainty propagated into the published trend. Multi-decade clinical laboratory series in EHRs, biobanks and registries have the same event structure — analyzer replacement, reagent and calibrator lot changes, method generation changes are station-history events — and are routinely used raw to support secular-trend claims. The interface is not an A-x-B pastiche: a laboratory is not a weather station. Patient mix moves with the instrument (a new analyzer often arrives with a new service line), referral and testing indication are endogenous, sampling is patient-indicated rather than scheduled, results are censored at reporting limits and at capped thresholds, and a non-commutable method change is a change of measurand rather than a bias — so the climatological "neighbours share the signal" assumption needs a stated clinical analogue before any correction is admissible. Clinical chemistry's IFCC/JCTLM standardization and harmonization programmes act forward on new methods and do not reconstruct a homogeneous historical series; nothing in the EHR-research toolchain plays the role of a reference station.
GATE 2 RESULT ON THE GAP: the gap as stated above is partly false, and the searches below say so. Presuppositions (each marked):
P1. Analyzer/method/reagent/calibrator change dates are recoverable for at least some sites and analytes. CONTESTED. Poh et al. 2015 state there is no Read code for the creatinine assay, so no ground truth existed in their 127-practice extract; non-retention of instrument history is the normal case and is itself a reportable finding.
P2. Enough contemporaneous sites share the true population signal to serve as reference series. CONTESTED — this is the core transplanted assumption and the most likely point of failure. No source found tests it.
P3. For the chosen analytes, method changes produce offsets or scale changes rather than a change of measurand. CONTESTED; R1 restricts to analytes with documented commutability, which narrows the question rather than establishing the premise. ARIC's HDL case (magnesium-dextran precipitation -> direct enzymatic) is a worked instance where the premise is doubtful.
P4. Published secular trends for these analytes exist and are retrievable in enough detail to be re-estimated. PLAUSIBLE but untested at frame level; no enumeration of such claims was found.
P5. Breakpoint offsets are large enough relative to within-site noise to be detectable at the available site and record density. PARTLY ESTABLISHED for creatinine: ARIC found >10% bias for creatinine and uric acid (2 of 8 analytes), with effects large enough to move CKD prevalence from 21.7% to 1.3% at one visit; Poh found step changes "almost all improvements" coinciding with method change. So for at least one analyte the offsets are not marginal. Unknown for the rest.
P6. Climatological pairwise methods transport to irregular, censored, indication-driven sampling. CONTESTED; sampling is not scheduled observation, indication drifts, and reported values are truncated (Poh had to discard eGFR values of exactly 60 and 90 as capped).
P7. The patient population at a site is not itself discontinuous at the method-change date. CONTESTED; co-timing of analyzer purchase with a service-line change would confound detection and correction alike, and would do so identically in the differenced series against a neighbour that did not change service line.
P8 (added at Gate 1). "Specificity of detection" is well-defined — i.e. a site-analyte-period with no documented change is a true negative. CONTESTED AND NOT REPAIRED BY R1: logs are incomplete (see P1), so absence of a documented change is not absence of a change, and specificity is therefore estimable only against a log subset independently certified as complete. This is the main reason well-posedness is scored 2/3 rather than 3/3.

What would count as an answer: An implemented homogenization on a defined multi-site record for a pre-specified analyte set, reporting (i) detected breakpoints with estimated offsets, validated against independently retrieved method-change logs as sensitivity/specificity of detection against known changes (with the true-negative frame defined per P8), and (ii) re-estimated secular trends with and without correction, with the fraction of trend attributable to breakpoints and a CI. Three distinct outcomes are each decision-relevant: trends unchanged within CI licenses raw-series trend claims for those analytes; trends materially changed obliges correction before any secular claim; and breakpoint detection no better than chance against known logs is a negative answer about the transplant itself (and, per P2/P7, an informative one — it would mean laboratory neighbours do not share a signal the way stations do).

Novelty — how this differs from each:
vs 1 (Poh/de Lusignan): same target (blind detection of laboratory method change in multi-site routine records, with trend interpretation as the motive) and same scale of multi-site data, so the detection limb is not new. Three differences remain. (i) Reference: Poh's reference is internal to the patient — the SCr-to-eGFR mapping, which works only because the laboratory reports both a raw measurand and a derived quantity; the candidate's reference is contemporaneous other sites, which requires no derived quantity and so generalises to analytes with no equation attached (haemoglobin, albumin, cholesterol). This is the actual transplant and Poh does not contain it. (ii) Correction: Poh explicitly does not correct — "identification is the first step towards backward calibration", and the paper reports that recalculating MDRD from reported SCr reduces but does not remove the step. Back-calibration is named as future work. (iii) Validation: Poh had no ground truth (no Read code for the assay) and validates only by the argument that a step coinciding exactly with the inferred change point is "highly unlikely" to be physiological. Limb (b) of the candidate — operating characteristics against retrieved logs — is absent. No trend is re-estimated.
vs 2 (ARIC/Parrinello): this is the closest neighbour on the question's quantitative limb and it has already produced the number the candidate asks for, for one corpus — so the candidate cannot claim the magnitude is unmeasured. Differences: (i) ARIC has no detection step at all; the five scheduled visits supply the candidate change times and the central-laboratory history is documented, so sensitivity/specificity of detection is undefined there. (ii) ARIC's reference is a physical gold standard — 200 participants' stored aliquots reassayed on one contemporaneous platform — not a contemporaneous neighbouring site; this is the strongest available design and precisely the one unavailable in routine multi-site records, where no aliquots were banked. (iii) ARIC is a research cohort with scheduled sampling, fixed participants and one central laboratory per visit; the candidate's frame is indication-driven, open-cohort, many-laboratory routine data, where patient mix is not held fixed across the breakpoint (P7) and sampling is not scheduled (P6). (iv) ARIC re-estimates prevalence/incidence within its own cohort; the candidate re-estimates a pre-registered frame of externally published trend claims and asks how many survive. Net: ARIC answers a narrower, better-instrumented version of limb (c) and leaves limbs (a) and (b) untouched.
vs 3 (Sternberg/NHANES): the assay change is known, dated and singular; the paper's subject is the estimator for a bridging adjustment, not the discovery of breakpoints. Its design requires stored specimens run on both assays — the same physical bridge ARIC used, and the same thing routine multi-site records lack. No multi-site structure, no reference series, no detection, and it is the methodological statistics of a correction whose location is given.

Significance — consequence: If a material fraction of published trend magnitude is breakpoint-driven, then secular-trend claims built on routine multi-decade laboratory data require homogenization before publication, and a body of prevalence-trend literature (CKD, anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, lipid trends) needs re-examination; ARIC already shows this is not hypothetical, since correcting creatinine moved one visit's CKD prevalence by more than an order of magnitude, which is larger than any secular effect anyone would claim. If trends are unchanged within CI for commutable analytes, that licenses raw-series trend claims and retires a standing worry cheaply — a useful negative. If blind detection is no better than chance against retrieved logs, the transplant fails, and the mechanism of failure is itself the finding: it would mean the differenced series between two laboratories does not isolate instrument change, most plausibly because patient mix moves with the instrument (P7) or indication drifts (P6), which is a substantive statement about why laboratory series are not station series and would redirect effort to within-patient references (Poh's design) and to banked-aliquot bridges (ARIC's design).
Significance — audience: (i) EHR/claims-research methodologists, specifically the OHDSI data-quality line — their current instrument is a plausibility flag; an offset estimator against reference sites would be a different kind of check, one that yields a correction rather than an exclusion. (ii) Biobank and registry curators (UK Biobank, All of Us, national laboratory registries) who currently ship assay-era indicators but no corrected values, and would have to decide whether to publish a homogenized series alongside the raw one, as national meteorological services do. (iii) Clinical-chemistry metrology (IFCC/JCTLM harmonization) which has no retrospective arm and would gain, or be shown it cannot gain, a way to speak about historical records. (iv) Epidemiologists publishing analyte trend papers, who would face a new methods requirement. (v) Journal reviewers and the authors of the specific pre-registered trend claims, who would learn which of their results survive. The audience is real and identifiable; what is weaker is how much it would change, since ARIC already made the general point and it did not propagate.
Significance — fecundity: opens (a) what the clinical analogue of a reference station actually is — co-catchment site, EQA peer group, within-patient serial results, or banked aliquots — and which is strictly better, a comparison no source makes; (b) whether estimated breakpoint offsets can reconstruct missing laboratory history, i.e. whether the data can recover the metadata that P1 says is usually lost; (c) whether homogenization-adjustment uncertainty can be propagated into prevalence estimates the way climatology propagates it into temperature trends, which nothing in EHR research currently does; (d) whether a non-commutable method change should be modelled as a new series to be chain-linked (the official-statistics answer, query 9) rather than an offset to be subtracted; (e) whether detection sensitivity sets a floor on the smallest secular trend that is claimable from routine data at given site density.
Significance — anchoring: the ARIC recalibration result is an unexplained and un-generalised anomaly sitting in the trend literature; the IDMS creatinine-standardization era boundary and the MDRD/CKD-EPI equation changes are known discontinuities handled ad hoc; Poh's unexecuted back-calibration is a named open problem; the reproducibility of EHR-derived trend claims is a live concern; and the capped-value problem (eGFR reported as exactly 60 or 90) is a censoring artefact that interacts with every detection method and is currently handled by deletion.

Reason for silence: a disciplinary boundary compounded by an incentive gap, with a third contributing mechanism of missing metadata. Comparability over time is owned by clinical-chemistry metrology, which acts forward — standardization and commutability requirements are imposed on new methods — and which treats historical non-comparability as a reason not to analyse old data rather than a defect to repair; the people holding the long multi-site records are EHR-research methodologists whose quality vocabulary (conformance, completeness, plausibility, per Kahn et al. 2016) has no notion of an offset to be estimated against a reference, so the operation is not expressible in their framework; and the homogenization literature lives in a journal set (J Climate, Int J Climatol, ASCMO, WMO guidance) that neither community reads, confirmed by queries 2 and 7 returning climatology exclusively. The incentive gap is the sharper half: a homogenization result mainly invalidates other investigators' published findings and produces no new analyte, cohort or instrument, so no one's grant is served by it — ARIC performed the work only because an internal cross-visit comparability obligation compelled it, and did not generalise the method or press the implication onto the wider literature. The third mechanism is material rather than social: P1 — instrument and calibrator change logs are generally not retained, so the station-metadata half of the climatological method has no clinical counterpart to retrieve, and an investigator who tried would stop at the feasibility step. This is a credible mechanism, which matters here because Gate 2 graded novelty Low: the silence is explained, but what is silent is the narrow residue (limb (b) and the neighbouring-site reference design), not the broad question, which was not silent.
