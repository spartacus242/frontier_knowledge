## (2) The two quoted strings — both LOCATED in §8 Threats to Validity; one verbatim, one verbatim-with-a-misattaching-elision

### Quote 1 — entry: "even dynamic compilation may cause performance changes after long periods of repetitive execution ... or due to artifacts such as deopt loops"
Location: §8 "Threats to Validity" (no subsection). Full sentence, RECONSTRUCTION from 4 consecutive served segments (junctions "after long| periods", "to trigger| compilation", "for example in| applications"; 55 words; nothing completed by inference):

> "We note, however, that even dynamic compilation may cause performance changes after long periods of repetitive execution, due to high invocation thresholds used to trigger compilation, or even indefinitely repeating performance changes, for example in applications that generate code, or due to artifacts such as deopt loops."

What the entry's ellipsis elides: ", due to high invocation thresholds used to trigger compilation, or even indefinitely repeating performance changes, for example in applications that generate code,".

Grammar on both sides of the cut. "may cause" takes two coordinated objects: (i) "performance changes after long periods of repetitive execution, due to high invocation thresholds used to trigger compilation"; (ii) "or even indefinitely repeating performance changes, for example in applications that generate code, or due to artifacts such as deopt loops". "deopt loops" is the second example cause under (ii), the INDEFINITELY REPEATING case. It is not offered as a cause of (i), the late one-off change. The entry's splice attaches "or due to artifacts such as deopt loops" to object (i) and drops the head "indefinitely repeating performance changes" that the phrase actually modifies. Does the elision change the claim? It MISATTACHES but does not INFLATE: the full sentence places deopt loops under changes that repeat indefinitely — closer to the candidate's "non-convergence" than the late-change reading the splice suggests. Adversary 2's file carries the full sentence correctly; the shortening is the entry's.

Opening words and what they scope: "We note, however, that" — a caveat on the immediately preceding sentence, which treats compilation as a warm-up phenomenon: "Some, such as dynamic compilation, are likely to exhibit initially high arrival rate, which should gradually subside, and are best handled as warm up." The paragraph is about the ARRIVAL RATE of performance-change mechanisms, in the context of the paper's statistical model and its warm-up handling (§6). Modality: "may cause" — the clause ASSERTS a POSSIBILITY. It does not report that deopt loops were observed in the paper's data, and no deopt count, deopt rate or deopt-attributed share is printed anywhere served. The following sentence widens the list of indefinitely acting mechanisms away from the compiler: "Other mechanisms, such as the memory layout changes described in Section 3, can also keep triggering performance changes indefinitely, simply because execution will likely involve cycles of allocation and (potentially copying) garbage collection." Then: "Additionally, mechanisms beyond the scope of our experiments, such as the scheduling algorithms employed on heterogeneous multicore processors, can also interact with the benchmark workload in complex ways leading to perpetual performance fluctuations."

The entry's gloss "walks that back" (i.e. walks back §3) is interpretive: §8 qualifies the treatment of compilation as warm-up-only; it does not retract the §3 finding about the one dissected change. "Names the mechanism as live" is fair only in the sense "named as possible, not excluded".
