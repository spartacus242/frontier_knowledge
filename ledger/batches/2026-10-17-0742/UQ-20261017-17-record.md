Domain(s): Medicine — acute pain management and analgesic-trial methodology (target field). Adjacent: patient-reported-outcome psychometrics (response-shift methodology, longitudinal measurement invariance), survey-response measurement / health econometrics (reporting heterogeneity).

Question (canonical):

V1 (as intake): Within a single acute inpatient pain episode, does a patient's mapping from experienced pain onto the 0-10 numeric rating scale change as the episode proceeds (recalibration), and what share of the measured within-episode NRS reduction — and of apparent attainment of titration targets such as NRS <= 4 or the conventional 2-point/30% MCID — is recalibration rather than change in experienced pain?

V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, adopted as canonical; the single reformulation permitted at Gate 1, made only for well-posedness — it pins the population, the operationalisation of "recalibration", the anchor set and the two estimands, none of which V1 fixed):
In adult inpatients with an acute pain episode scored serially for >= 48 h (post-surgical or acute medical), with a then-test administered at a randomly assigned day within the episode and a pre-registered external anchor set fixed before data collection (PCA demands/opioid-equivalent consumption, quantitative sensory testing, and a functional milestone such as time to first ambulation):
 (a) does a longitudinal measurement-invariance model of the serial 0-10 NRS, with the anchors as the invariance reference, reject intercept (scalar) invariance across the episode — i.e. is there a drift in the NRS value a patient reports at fixed anchored pain;
 (b) under a pre-declared operationalisation of recalibration (then-test difference, or the fitted intercept drift), what fraction of the mean observed within-episode NRS reduction is recalibration rather than change in anchored pain; and
 (c) at the NRS <= 4 titration target and at the 2-point / 30% MCID, what is the misclassification rate of "target attained" / "MCID achieved" induced by that recalibration?
Both estimates must be shown not to be explained by recall error, via the randomised then-test timing and by refitting (a)/(b) on anchor data alone without then-test input.

Alternative formulations:
- When a post-operative patient's score falls from 8 to 4 over two days, how much of the fall is the pain changing and how much is the patient's internal "10" changing?
- Is the acute-pain NRS longitudinally invariant over hours to days within one episode, and if not, what is the resulting bias in NRS-change endpoints and in protocols that titrate opioids to a fixed NRS threshold?

Motivating gap (source method: presupposition inversion):
The inverted settled claim is that a falling NRS means improving pain — which presupposes that the patient's mapping from sensation to number is fixed over the episode. Recalibration response shift is an established construct in health-related quality of life and has already been demonstrated for bodily pain in surgical patients at the 3-month scale (Visser et al. 2013, below). It has not been examined on the hours-to-days timescale, on the single-item 0-10 NRS, or with a dosing decision attached to the number. Two adjacent findings sharpen the gap rather than close it: the acute-pain field has run large serial-PROM studies in exactly this window and asked only about responsiveness, not invariance (PROMPT NIT-1, BJA 2024, days 1/3/7, n = 2,661), and it has documented that a fixed NRS cut-off already misclassifies treatment need cross-sectionally (van Dijk et al. 2012: at NRS > 4, 17% over-treated / 5% under-treated against the patient's own verbal rating). Neither asks whether the scale itself moves during the episode.

Presuppositions:
1. Experienced pain and its rating are separable in principle, so that "recalibration" denotes something. CONTESTED, and the principal dissolution risk: with no external anchor the question collapses into "pain is what the patient says it is". Mitigated, not removed, by the pre-registered anchor set: recalibration is then defined relative to a declared anchor, so the estimand is anchor-relative and must be reported as such.
2. A then-test (or an equivalent longitudinal invariance model) can distinguish recalibration from recall error. CONTESTED — this is the standing criticism of then-test designs, and is why V2 requires randomised then-test timing plus an anchor-only refit.
3. A non-recalibrating anchor exists over the episode. CONTESTED — opioid consumption, QST and functional milestones are each affected by treatment and by the episode itself; the design must pre-declare the anchor set and report sensitivity of (b) and (c) to anchor choice.
4. The NRS is used as if interval-scaled with stable anchoring in titration protocols and trial endpoints. ESTABLISHED (published acute-pain titration protocols with NRS thresholds; the 2-point/30% MCID convention).
5. Episodes with >= 24-72 h of serial scoring are common. ESTABLISHED (routine 4-hourly inpatient pain charting; PROMPT NIT-1's day 1/3/7 schedule is evidence the window is routinely instrumented).
6. Patients can give a then-test rating without being cued by the clinical context into a socially desirable answer. CONTESTED — the then-test is administered by or near the team treating them.
7. (Added at Gate 1) Floor/ceiling and the "worst pain imaginable" anchor do not themselves produce the whole effect — i.e. an observed intercept drift is not merely a patient whose worst-ever experience was reset by the index event. This is a mechanism of recalibration, not an artefact, but must be modelled separately because it predicts a specific direction and time course.

What would count as an answer:
A within-episode then-test combined with a longitudinal measurement-invariance analysis of serial NRS records against a pre-registered external anchor: an estimate of the recalibration component of observed NRS change, expressed as a share of total observed change, plus the misclassification rate of "titration target attained" and "MCID achieved" under recalibration. Settling it also requires showing the recalibration estimate is not recall error, through a design that separates them (randomised then-test timing; or an anchor-based invariance model fitted without then-test data). A null — scalar invariance holds within the acute episode — is a clean answer and retires the concern for both titration protocols and NRS-change endpoints. Because (b) is operationalisation- and anchor-dependent, an answer must report the share under at least two pre-declared operationalisations.

Significance — consequence:
If yes and the share is material: two things that are currently read as measurements become partly measurements of the measuring instrument. (i) NRS-change endpoints in acute analgesic trials — difference in mean NRS change, proportion achieving a 2-point/30% MCID — carry a bias whose sign depends on whether recalibration runs with or against true improvement; if recalibration inflates apparent improvement in both arms it attenuates between-arm contrasts and helps explain the low sensitivity-to-change PROMPT NIT-1 measured (median 0.22), and if it differs by arm (plausible where arms differ in opioid exposure or in how much severe pain the patient has experienced) it biases the treatment effect itself. (ii) Protocols that titrate opioids to a fixed threshold would be acting on a moving target: a patient reaching NRS <= 4 partly by rescaling gets de-escalated while anchored pain is unchanged, and the reverse patient keeps receiving opioid. That is a direct opioid-stewardship consequence, not an abstract one. If no — scalar invariance holds within the episode — the concern is retired for both uses, which is worth having in writing given P1 and P2 make the worry respectable, and the response-shift literature's encroachment on acute endpoints can be bounded rather than argued about.

Significance — audience:
Acute pain services and the authors of inpatient pain-assessment and opioid-titration protocols (who choose the threshold and the cadence); acute analgesic-trial methodologists and core-outcome-set groups (IMMPACT/ACTTION, the PROMPT/standardised-endpoint collaborations that ran NIT-1), who would have to decide whether an NRS-change primary endpoint needs an anchored co-primary; regulators assessing acute analgesic endpoints; and the response-shift/PRO psychometrics community, for whom a negative result bounds the construct's timescale and a positive result opens an acute-care application area. The concrete action in each case is specifiable: add an anchor or a then-test to the acute protocol, re-derive the MCID, or stop worrying.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: whether anchored or calibrated acute scales (vignette-anchored, QST-referenced, forced-choice) measure change better than the free NRS; whether the MCID should be re-derived on an invariance-corrected scale, and whether the familiar 2-point/30% convention is partly a recalibration artefact; whether recalibration is itself a treatment effect worth having (a patient who rescales may be functioning better) and therefore whether the "bias" framing is right; whether recalibration differs by opioid exposure, by whether the patient's worst-ever pain was set by this episode, and by first versus repeat episode; and whether the same drift affects the other bedside single-item scales scored on the same cadence (dyspnoea, nausea, sedation).

Significance — anchoring:
Touches three standing anomalies rather than inventing one: the documented mismatch between NRS thresholds and patients' own bearability/treatment preference (P3); the low measured sensitivity-to-change of acute postsurgical PROMs in a large multicentre study (PROMPT NIT-1) with no mechanism offered; and the open methodological dispute about whether then-test recalibration estimates are recall artefacts, which the candidate's randomised-timing design would inform on a timescale where recall is short.

Reason for silence:
Primarily a disciplinary boundary, with an incentive/taboo component and a data component.
(i) Boundary: response-shift methodology is owned by HRQoL and psycho-oncology outcomes research, where the native object is a multi-item questionnaire administered at clinic visits months apart in a trial design, and the native method (then-test, SEM invariance) presumes a research encounter. Acute pain measurement is owned by anaesthesia and nursing, where the NRS is treated as a vital sign to be charted and acted on — the fifth vital sign framing — and scale properties are discussed as validity/responsiveness, never as invariance. Query 9 found the method family thriving in psychometrics and ILD methodology, and queries 1-6 found the acute-pain literature in the same window asking only responsiveness; the two literatures use different words for the same object and do not meet in the acute window.
(ii) Incentive/taboo: the governing clinical norm in acute pain is McCaffery's "pain is whatever the experiencing person says it is, existing whenever they say it does", adopted precisely as a corrective to decades of documented undertreatment. Asking whether the patient's number means the same thing today as yesterday reads, inside that field, as doubting the patient — a professionally and politically costly question to pose, and one whose positive answer could be misused to withhold analgesia. That is an incentive gap, not an absence of thought: the same field cheerfully publishes misclassification arithmetic when the doubt is aimed at clinicians rather than patients (P3).
(iii) Data/instrument: the dense serial NRS record needed to fit an invariance model within an episode is a by-product of EHR flowsheets; it only became extractable at scale recently, and a within-episode then-test requires interrupting a ward round, so neither half of the design was cheap before.

Scores: Well-posedness 2/3 · Significance 2/3

Well-posedness 2/3 — justification: limb (a) and limb (c) are fully specifiable once a design is fixed, and a null is informative, which clears the Gate 1 bar. Two points keep it off 3/3. First, limb (b)'s "share" is not design-independent: the number depends on which operationalisation of recalibration (then-test difference vs fitted intercept drift) and which anchor is pre-declared, so the question as asked has a family of answers rather than one, and V2 has to require reporting under at least two. Second, presupposition 1 is contested and the question is anchor-relative in principle, not just in practice — a critic can always say the anchor moved instead of the scale, and nothing in the design closes that off, only bounds it. The reformulation already spent at Gate 1 was used to pin population, operationalisation, anchors and estimands; no further reformulation is available.

Significance 2/3 — justification: the audience is real and the action on a positive answer is concrete (threshold protocols, MCID re-derivation, anchored co-primary endpoints), and a null is also actionable. Held off 3/3 because the one published magnitude in a comparable design is small — P1's recalibration added 4.4% of explained variance over three months — so a within-episode effect could plausibly be small enough that no protocol would change, and the significance case is therefore conditional on an effect size nobody has measured.
