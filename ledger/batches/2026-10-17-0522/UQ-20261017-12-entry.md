Domain(s): Biology — preclinical animal behaviour / neuroscience phenotyping methodology (markerless pose estimation, behaviour classification). Method-owning neighbours: epidemiological measurement-error theory; econometrics of ML-generated variables; plant high-throughput phenotyping.

Question (canonical): For pre-declared automated phenotyping pipelines (>=2 pose estimators, >=2 behaviour classifiers) applied to >=3 manipulations that visibly change the animal's image (sedation/ataxia, diet-induced obesity, nerve or spinal injury, a coat-colour- or body-size-divergent line), with blinded multi-rater human labels at pre-registered per-group counts and restricted to behaviour categories with documented inter-rater reliability: (a) does per-group sensitivity/specificity (discrete behaviour calls) or the per-keypoint error distribution (pose) differ between treated and control animals under a pre-specified test of equality; and (b) where it does, how large is the bias in the pipeline's effect estimate after standard differential-misclassification correction, relative to that estimate's published confidence interval and to the assay's typical effect size? [Tightened at Gate 1, reformulation 1 of 1, well-posedness only. Intake version: "is pose and behaviour classification error independent of experimental group, and how large is the differential-measurement-error bias it imposes on reported effect sizes?"]

Alternative formulations:
1. Does an automated behaviour classifier's accuracy depend on the treatment the animal received, and if so, how much of a published behavioural effect is instrument error rather than behaviour?
2. What is the group-stratified error of automated phenotyping pipelines, and what bias does the field's implicit assumption of non-differential error impose on its effect estimates?

Motivating gap: Presupposition inversion on the claim that learned pipelines (DeepLabCut, SLEAP, MoSeq-class, commercial home-cage systems) made behavioural measurement objective. The instrument is a learned function of the animal's image and the manipulations under study change the image, so error may be differential by group — indistinguishable in the output from a real effect and invisible in the pooled accuracy figure the field reports. Gate 2 anchored the negative on Bhola & Yoo 2025 (systematic review, 67 rodent pose studies) reporting no group-stratified accuracy. GATE 4 WITHDREW THAT ANCHOR: two in-field sources reporting group-stratified instrument error were found (see priors P1, P2), so the gap as written ("no group-stratified accuracy anywhere") is false.

Presuppositions:
1. Blinded multi-rater human labels can serve as a per-group reference standard. [CONTESTED — and weakened further at Gate 4: MARS reports human pose annotation error is large and context-dependent ("only 52.3% fell within a 5mm radius of ground truth in front-view frames"; "quality of human pose annotation is a limiting factor"), so a differential reference standard is an identification problem, not only a precision ceiling. An IRR ceiling does not fix it.]
2. Group-stratified ground truth is obtainable at usable precision. [Established; pre-registered per-group label counts plus a power statement.]
3. Published studies do not already report group-stratified classifier error. [FALSE AS WRITTEN — refuted at Gate 4 by Geuther 2019 (coat-colour-stratified tracking error, main text) and MARS (stratified pose/classifier comparison for cabled vs uncabled videos). Survives only if reworded to "no study estimates per-group error for a biological *treatment* grouping of a learned pose/behaviour pipeline against blinded human labels and propagates it into the effect estimate" — a twice-narrowed residue.]
4. The induced bias is computable by standard measurement-error correction. [MISSPECIFIED for this regime — MARS documents classifier precision/recall rising with bout length, so error depends on the realized outcome, not only on group; per-group Se/Sp does not then identify the bias. Pose limb worse: a keypoint error distribution must propagate through a nonlinear feature pipeline into bout/syllable calls.]
5. Pooled training across groups reduces but does not equalise per-group error. [CONTESTED; part of what the question tests.]
6. The scored behaviours are defined well enough that "error" exists. [CONTESTED for soft ethogram categories; restricted to documented-IRR categories.]

What would count as an answer: Per-group sensitivity/specificity or per-keypoint error distributions for the pre-declared pipelines and manipulations on group-stratified blinded multi-rater labels; a pre-specified test of equality; and, where differential, a corrected effect estimate placed against the published estimate and the assay's typical effect size, with the IRR ceiling attached to every figure. Null branch informative: per-group differences bounded below the level that would move estimates beyond their CIs would license current pooled-validation practice.

**The MARS claim is confirmed, on the second attempt.** A first checker could not open the paper at all (elifesciences.org returns a JavaScript "Client Challenge") and found nothing of the kind in the Caltech repository excerpt it did reach, which left the judge's load-bearing claim unverified. The bioRxiv v1 preprint main text carries it for both stages. Pose: "the presence of a microendoscope or other cabled device only marginally affected the performance of pose estimation" (Fig 4B-C). Classifier: "We do not observe a difference on individual videos with vs without cable when controlling for mean bout length" (Extended Data Fig 7, test set 2). So MARS does ask the candidate's limb (i) under an implant-status grouping, against labels from multiple annotators, with bout length controlled — exactly as the Gate 2 regrade said. The support is qualitative: no cable/no-cable values, confidence intervals or p-values came through, and they would sit in Fig 4 and ED Fig 7, which were not read, as were the Methods, the supplements and the published eLife text. The paper's annotator agreement is reported against Human #1 as precision and recall — mounting 96.7+/-0.9 and 96.9+/-0.6, attack 83.7+/-1.3 and 86.2+/-2.5, close investigation 89+/-0.9 and 80.5+/-1.8, per-video F1 from 0.543 to 0.959 — and it states that "the mean annotator F1 score was well predicted by the mean bout duration of annotations in a video", which is the bout-length control's basis. The only other stratification is by view (annotators 86.2% within 5 mm top versus 52.3% front; MARS 92% versus 67%); there is no breakdown by coat colour, strain, lighting, camera or arena.
**The Geuther figures are real but mis-described, and the description is corrected.** The 14% and under-4% numbers are not tracking error by coat colour measured against ground truth. They are **relative error in total distance travelled**, from a comparison of Ctrax against the neural network, and they are confounded with the arena: "higher levels of error were seen in albino mice in the open-field arena with a white floor (14%)" against "showed errors less than 4%" for black, grey and piebald mice in the white open field, with black mice reported at 27% in the 24-hour arena and 10% in the KOMP2 arena. The paper separately reports IoU against hand annotation by environment group. So Geuther evidences group-stratified error in an automated behavioural read-out — which is what the kill needs — but not in the form the entry stated, and coat colour is not cleanly separable from arena.
