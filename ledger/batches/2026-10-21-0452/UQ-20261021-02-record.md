Source method: Presupposition inversion. Settled claim: the clinician recorded on a prescription indexes that clinician's prescribing preference. Untested presupposition: the recorded prescriber is the person who chose.

Question (canonical) — V1, as supplied at intake:
In health systems whose order records distinguish the entering user, the authorising prescriber and any cosigner, and whose audit logs timestamp each action, what share of prescriptions in pre-declared drug classes were decided by someone other than the recorded prescriber, and by how much does that misattribution attenuate prescriber-preference instrumental-variable estimates and dilute prescriber-targeted feedback effects re-estimated on the same cohort?

Question (canonical) — V2, the one reformulation permitted at Gate 1, for well-posedness only (this is the version graded):
Taking the decision-maker as ascertained by a declared gold standard on a sample (direct observation or blinded note/record review, with a reported agreement statistic) and the EHR order-and-audit-log fields as a candidate *proxy* for it rather than as its definition: (a) under a pre-declared attribution rule, what is the misclassification rate of "recorded prescriber" as an indicator of the decision-maker, per pre-declared drug class, with the share of orders for which no single decider is identifiable reported separately; (b) what is the sensitivity/specificity of the audit-log proxy against that gold standard; and (c) on one cohort, one estimand and one comparator, what is the signed change in a prescriber-preference IV estimate (and in its conditional F / partial R-squared) and in a prescriber-targeted feedback effect when exposure is reassigned from the recorded prescriber to the attributed decider, against a pre-declared equivalence margin?
Reason for reformulation: V1 presupposes that audit-log fields *identify* the decider. The one paper that has actually measured this quantity needed direct ward observation to ascertain the decider (Ross et al. 2012, below); an order entered by a junior after a consultant's verbal bedside decision appears in the log only as the junior entering. The log records entry, authorisation and cosignature — it does not record deciding. V2 makes the gold standard primary and the log a proxy to be validated, which is what makes the question answerable. V1 is retained in the record.

Alternative formulations:
- Does the name on the prescription belong to the person who chose the drug, and does it matter to the studies and the interventions that assume it does?
- Treating "recorded prescriber" as a mismeasured indicator of the decision-maker, what are its misclassification rate and the resulting bias in prescriber-preference IV first stages and in prescriber-feedback effect estimates?

Significance — consequence: If the decider-vs-recorded discordance measured by Ross et al. (61% for new inpatient medicines) holds at any comparable magnitude in the outpatient and class-specific settings where prescriber-preference IVs are built, then a non-trivial share of the instrument's variation is not the instrumented clinician's preference, the first stage weakens, and — because delegation correlates with service, time of day and patient complexity — the misclassification is differential, so the bias is not reliably toward the null. If instead discordance in the IV-relevant settings is small (outpatient initiation of a chronic drug plausibly differs sharply from a ward round), then a widely used instrument is validated on an axis never checked, and prescriber-targeted feedback keeps its denominator. Either way the answer is actionable; what the answer is NOT is unprecedented in kind.
Significance — audience: (1) pharmacoepidemiologists using prescriber/physician-prescribing-preference IVs, who would add a first-stage sensitivity arm instrumented on the attributed decider or shift to a practice-level instrument; (2) stewardship and opioid audit-and-feedback programmes that mail clinicians their own prescribing — Drug Topics independently confirms this audience treats prescriber attribution as consequential for pay-for-performance; (3) value-based-payment attribution designers. Audience is real; its appetite is partly answered by the fact that it has had Ross et al. since 2012 and has not acted, which is itself evidence about how much it cares.
Significance — fecundity: whether to prefer practice- or team-level preference instruments over clinician-level ones; whether feedback should be addressed to deciders, teams or units; whether audit-log fields can be validated into a routine decision-attribution variable; whether prescriber-level quality measures need an attribution-error correction analogous to measurement-error corrections in claims-based profiling.
Significance — anchoring: instrument-validity debates around prescriber-preference IVs; the judge/examiner-design diagnostics literature (Kolesár et al.; CRAN `ivcheck`), where the analogue — is the recorded decision-maker the actual one — appears not to be a named diagnostic; "incident-to" billing attribution in MACRA/MIPS; the emerging use of EHR audit logs as a research substrate.

Reason for silence: A disciplinary boundary with a one-way citation flow, plus a framing mismatch at the moment of publication. The fact was established by medical-education and prescribing-safety researchers using ward observation and published in a medical-education journal, and its authors framed the consequence for the audience they were writing to — prescribing-error-rate studies stratified by grade of doctor — not for instrumental-variable identification. The pharmacoepidemiology methods community that depends on prescriber identity does not read that journal for identification threats, and the quantity has no vocabulary in its own literature (there is no term of art for "misclassified instrument identity"), so there was nothing to search for. This is a credible mechanism, but note what it explains: it explains silence on limb (c), NOT on the premise, which was never silent. That asymmetry is exactly why the novelty grade is Low rather than Medium — the ledger's rule is that absent a credible reason for silence the novelty finding is suspect, and here the credible reason only covers the residue.
