Question (canonical) — V2, the Gate 1 reformulation (the one graded; V1 below, both retained):
Restricted to label/result pairs for which a consensus disconfirming rule exists and is computable from routine data — (a) COPD code + post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC >= 0.7, (b) hypothyroidism code + normal TSH recorded >= 6 weeks after the last levothyroxine supply, (c) coeliac code + negative serology AND negative duodenal biopsy with documented gluten-containing diet — and with the outcome declared in advance as a two-arm endpoint (primary: a recorded retraction event — problem-list removal, "resolved"/inactivated status, or an exclusion/"no longer has" code — in systems that emit such events; fallback, declared before any data pull and reported separately: cessation of disease-specific prescribing for >= 180 days as functional retraction): what is the 24-month cause-specific hazard and cumulative incidence of retraction after the first disconfirming result, against matched person-time in labelled patients with no such result, and what share of each group is still receiving disease-specific therapy at 12 and 24 months? Asthma + negative bronchial challenge and heart failure + normal natriuretic peptide with normal ejection fraction are carried as pre-specified SECONDARY pairs only, because a negative test there does not exclude past disease; penicillin allergy + negative challenge is the positive control.

V1 (intake formulation, failed Gate 1 on two counts — "objectively disconfirming evidence" was undefined for two of its five pairs, and the outcome was stated as retraction without declaring which data model emits it): In routine care records, once a chronic-disease diagnosis has been entered, what is the rate at which it is retracted after objectively disconfirming evidence for that diagnosis appears in the same record, and does retraction change the disease-specific treatment the patient goes on receiving?

Alternative formulations:
- Are clinical diagnoses falsifiable in practice — what is the hazard of a label being removed once the test that defines it comes back against it?
- When the chart already contains the result that rules a condition out, how long does the condition stay on the problem list and on the prescription?

Motivating gap (as intaken, with Gate 2 corrections marked):
A reported-but-uninterrogated anomaly: repeated studies find a large minority of chronic-disease labels unsupported by the criteria that define them. VERIFIED at Gate 2, with the citation corrected: Aaron et al., JAMA 2017;317(3):269-279 is titled "Reevaluation of Diagnosis in Adults With Physician-Diagnosed Asthma" (not "...of Asthma..."), and ruled out current asthma in 33.1% (95% CI 29.4-36.8) of 613 completers; de-labelling there was a research intervention and the paper did not measure whether community clinicians later changed the label or stopped therapy. The intake's framing — "nobody asks what routine care does when the disconfirming result is already sitting in the record" — is FALSE for the index pair. Fortis et al., Respir Care 2016;61(9):1192-1200 asked exactly that for COPD and reported both limbs. The gap that survives is narrower: no multi-pair, time-to-event, comparator-controlled version exists, and the recorded-removal-event endpoint has not been measured anywhere I could find.

Presuppositions:
P1 Dated diagnosis histories are retrievable INCLUDING removal/resolution events — CONTESTED, and the single weakest point. Claims data emit no removal event at all; EHR problem lists vary. The Gate 2 informatics sweep found work on problem-list completeness and review rates (additions and duplicates) and none counting deletions as events, which is consistent with the dependent variable being largely absent from the data models researchers use. V2 therefore declares the prescribing-cessation fallback in advance and reports it separately; it is a weaker measure of the same thing.
P2 For each label there is an objective disconfirming result under an agreed rule — CONTESTED; V2 answers this by demoting asthma and heart failure to secondary pairs and keeping only the three pairs with a computable rule as primary.
P3 The disconfirming result was visible to a clinician — CONTESTED where results arrive from external labs or are filed without display; partially addressable via result-acknowledgement audit fields where they exist, otherwise a named limitation.
P4 A diagnostic label is intended to be falsifiable — CONTESTED; "history of" retention may be deliberate convention, so a null hazard may reflect convention rather than inertia. Not resolvable from record data alone; V2 pre-commits to reporting the null as ambiguous between the two readings rather than as inertia.
P5 Retraction is clinically preferable in some pairs — CONTESTED; the question is descriptive and must not be scored as if retraction were the good outcome.
P6 Confounding by record continuity is addressable — patients with disconfirming tests have longer, denser records; matching on person-time, visit density and look-back is required, and prevalent-user/immortal-time structure makes this a design problem, not a covariate problem.

What would count as an answer:
A pre-registered multi-database cohort (e.g. CPRD or OpenSAFELY, a US EHR network, a Nordic registry) reporting per pair: 24-month cumulative incidence of recorded retraction after the first disconfirming result with matched no-result comparator person-time; the share still on disease-specific therapy at 12 and 24 months in each group; the penicillin-allergy pair as positive control; and the prescribing-cessation sensitivity analysis where removal events are not emitted. A hazard indistinguishable from comparator with therapy continuing answers it one way; a materially raised hazard with therapy stopping answers it the other. A finding that removals are never recorded falsifies P1 and sends the question back to Gate 1 rather than answering it.

Significance — consequence: If the retraction hazard is indistinguishable from matched no-result person-time and therapy continues, then every code-defined chronic-disease denominator — QOF-style registers, EHR phenotype libraries, risk-adjustment and HCC-style payment models, trial recruitment by code — carries a false-positive fraction that is not transient but absorbing, and the de-labelling trial literature needs a maintenance arm rather than a one-off reassessment (which is exactly what the penicillin-allergy relabelling experience suggests). If the hazard is materially raised and therapy stops, then the one-third figures measure a queue being cleared rather than a permanent stock, and the overdiagnosis reading of those prevalence studies weakens. Deflated by prior A: for COPD the answer is already roughly known in the direction of persistence, so the marginal consequence is the generalisation and the event-level measurement, not the sign.
Significance — audience: EHR-phenotyping and pharmacoepidemiology methodologists (who would have to carry a non-decaying false-positive term in code-based definitions); primary-care quality bodies that already own "resolved"/exclusion coding; de-labelling trialists in respiratory, endocrine and allergy medicine; diagnostic-safety researchers, for whom this is a measurable behavioural outcome rather than a case-report theme; payers using diagnosis-based risk adjustment.
Significance — fecundity: whether retraction hazard varies by who filed the result (own practice vs external lab) tests P3 and makes result-acknowledgement auditing an exposure; whether retraction is faster where the EHR emits a removal event turns the data model into a treatment; whether "history of" conversion rather than deletion is the dominant route would make a new, measurable intermediate category; whether retraction predicts downstream harm or benefit is the normative follow-on the candidate deliberately refuses.
Significance — anchoring: the misdiagnosis-prevalence anomaly (Aaron 2017; COPD spirometry series); the problem-list-accuracy literature's known asymmetry between additions and deletions; penicillin-allergy delabelling and relabelling; code-based phenotype validity and chronic-condition flag persistence in claims.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism, and it is partly falsified):
Claimed: a disciplinary boundary plus an incentive/data-model gap. The stock is measured by respiratory, endocrine and gastroenterology clinical-epidemiology groups funded to improve diagnostic accuracy, which pays for prevalence of misdiagnosis; the flow is a property of the record, which belongs to clinical informatics, whose published quantities are problem-list completeness, review rates and duplication — all presence-side, because meaningful-use and coding revenue reward codes being there and nothing rewards their removal. In claims there is no removal event at all, so the dependent variable is absent from the data model most researchers use; and "history of" retention is the legally and clinically safe default, so retraction is not treated as a behaviour that could be measured. Gate 2 finding against this: Fortis et al. asked it in 2016 in exactly the clinical-pulmonology register the mechanism says would not, and the question has gone unrepeated rather than unasked. Under the LEDGER's own rule, a reason for silence that the record itself falsifies is a signal to distrust the novelty claim — which is where this entry lands.
