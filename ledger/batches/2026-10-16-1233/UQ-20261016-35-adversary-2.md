## Restatement in my own words

A biobank tells researchers to delete the records of participants who revoke consent. Three questions. (a) *Before* the deletion happens, do the revokers look different from the stayers on the variables the cohort is actually used to study? (b) If you take a fixed list of the cohort's headline numbers — a few GWAS hits, a few hazard ratios, one risk score's AUC — and recompute each one just before and just after each batch of revocations, how far does each number move, judged against its own Monte Carlo noise and against thresholds that would change a decision? (c) Do those movements pile up in the direction that (a) predicts, i.e. is it drift rather than jitter? Plus a governance rider: because the pre-deletion frame is destroyed, only the custodian, at the moment it presses delete, can ever compute any of this.

So, stripped down: *mandated retrospective deletion removes a non-random slice of an already-analysed corpus; how much does that move the published numbers, and can the removed slice be characterised before it vanishes?*

### What the opened pages say (quotes)

**Elmas, "The Impact of Data Persistence Bias on Social Media Studies", WebSci '23 (arXiv:2303.00902).** Data vanish because "the platforms suspend users or users themselves remove their data"; "account suspension is the biggest factor in tweet removals", deletions second "at 34% of removals". It performs the candidate's **limb (a)** — it characterises the vanished slice on the very construct the corpora are used to measure: "The mean political orientation score of the control dataset decreased from 0.15 to -0.23", read as Twitter having "purged many polarized Republican-oriented users in 2020"; "the upper quartile of the control dataset decreased from 1.5 to 0.2"; missing tweets were disproportionately counter-group/pro-side slogans. It performs the candidate's **limb (b)** — it re-collects the datasets of prior studies and reproduces a published framing analysis on the degraded frame, reporting "data turnout" of 70% down to 15% and concluding for the framing study that "it does not introduce a dramatic change to the relative ranks of frames" (threat frames' turnout "critically low" at 45.6%). It states the candidate's **irreversibility rider** — reproducers using shared IDs "will not be able to retrieve the data that do not exist on the platform", and removed accounts can make data "impossible to analyze retrospectively". It also *defeats* the strong form of that rider by showing the pre-deletion frame can be retained: the Internet Archive Twitter Stream Grab is one in which "the tweets that are later deleted are not removed".

**Tazare et al., BJGP Open 2024, "NHS national data opt-outs: trends and potential consequences for health data research".** Asks the health-data version of the candidate's question explicitly in its title. "Trends in opt-outs in England were described by age, sex, and region", "increasing the percentage of opt-outs in England from 2.77% to 4.97% of the population" (females 3.02%→5.53%, males 2.51%→4.41%; largest relative rise at 40–49, 2.89%→6.04%; "no clear trend between those with the highest opt-out percentage and IMD rank"). It recomputes no real estimate (a simulation illustrates opt-out selection bias; a power example), and it says the limb-(a) comparison was not available to it: "further information on clinical and sociodemographic characteristics of patients opting out is not available", and "once an individual has opted out, their data is no longer available to researchers who wish to study these mechanisms."

**PMC6287151, Table 1 (DOI 10.1155/2018/8267948, 26 Nov 2018) — the record's named unverified residue, now verified.** Caption: *"Characteristics of participants who declined the consent versus those who continued in the cohort."* Columns: *"Declined consent (n = 54)"*, *"Continued in the cohort (n = 809)"*, *"p value"*; variables mean age, women, primary-school education, stroke or severely disabled; all p between 0.182 and 0.892. This is limb (a), published, in a biomedical cohort. Caveat I could not resolve with the budget left: the page does not say whether "declined the consent" means decline at a re-consent step or withdrawal after participation, and the journal name is not on the table page.

**CPRD approved study.** "The aim of the proposed work is to assess the representativeness of the CPRD primary care database over time", "a series of consecutive monthly cross-sectional descriptive reports", "data from each monthly database build, January 2018 – March 2020" — i.e. monitoring frame drift across the opt-out rollout, benchmarked to national statistics rather than recomputing estimates.

**Prior spot-checks.** N2 (Ursin et al. 2020) is real — returned at query 6 under its exact title at PMC7538395 and link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11019-020-09969-w. N1 (arXiv:2011.14999) and N3 (the UKB-RAP community article) I did **not** open myself; I take no load-bearing claim from either, and nothing below rests on my own version of a robustness theorem.
