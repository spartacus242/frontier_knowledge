Domain(s): Medicine — rare-disease diagnostics; clinical epidemiology; health-systems performance measurement. Method-owning field: truncated time-to-event biostatistics / back-calculation and nowcasting.
Question (canonical): For a rare-disease population with datable symptom onset, does the reported fall in time-to-diagnosis survive estimation from an incident symptom-onset frame with right truncation and delay-dependent inclusion modelled explicitly — i.e. is the diagnostic odyssey measurably shortening, or is the apparent improvement an artefact of counting only patients who were eventually diagnosed?
Alternative formulations:
- Is the headline rare-disease diagnostic-delay figure (~5 y) and its reported decline identified at all from prevalent diagnosed cohorts, and what does a truncation-corrected back-calculation give for recent onset cohorts?
- Everyone says rare-disease patients wait about five years and that the wait is falling, but the number counts only people who got diagnosed — if the still-undiagnosed are accounted for, is the wait actually falling?
- (Gate 1 reformulation, retained) In one named source enumerating symptom-onset cohorts rather than diagnosed patients, estimate F_c per onset cohort under explicit right truncation with a pre-stated delay-dependent inclusion model, report the naive diagnosed-only estimate beside it on the same records, and decompose any difference into truncation artefact, case-mix change and change in the diagnosis hazard.
Motivating gap: IRDiRC (one year) and Rare 2030 (six months) targets are scored against delay figures computed on already-diagnosed samples — Faye et al., Eur J Hum Genet 2024;32:1116-1126 (4.7 y mean; states the indicator excludes those still waiting and "does not allow to monitor the evolution of TDT over time") and Benito-Lozano et al., Orphanet J Rare Dis 2022 (10.1186/s13023-022-02530-3; −5.1%/yr joinpoint trend, own most-recent segment discarded because recent-onset patients "have not yet had the opportunity to be diagnosed"). Right truncation and advocacy-panel recruitment bias push in opposite directions, so even the sign of the published trend is unestablished. (Both motivating sources were intake-reported, not re-opened at Gate 2; neither was graded as a prior.)

Verification (post-batch, fresh-context checker, log UQ-20261016-61-verification.md): the Gate 2 kill is CONFIRMED verbatim, and the routing ground with it.
All three strings appear in Feldtkeller & Rudwaleit, Ann Rheum Dis 2014;73(12):e77, each checked singly and then jointly: "The authors neglected in their analysis and interpretation all the patients with recent symptom onset who were not yet diagnosed and are therefore not part of their database."; "the average delay in diagnosis found in the DANBIO register seems to be a realistic approximation only for former disease-onset decades, that is, for patients with symptom onset 20-40 years ago"; "However, in order to demonstrate this reduction reliably, we need another 20 years passing by." The letter proposes and applies no correction: it names the missing observations as "diagnoses to be expected in the future", records that Fries et al. called the problem "right censoring", prefers to call it "an incorrect analysis and incorrect data interpretation", and offers waiting as its only remedy. Its unit is individual AS patients grouped by year of symptom onset, its endpoint onset-to-diagnosis time, and the quantity it disputes is the registry's average delay by onset cohort, which it says is biased for recent cohorts. So the question is asked in print and is not answered there, which is the asked-but-open shape exactly; the Gate 2 Low grade and the routed row rest on the same confirmed sentences.
Birrell et al. 2012 (doi 10.1515/1948-4690.1055) is confirmed as a Bayesian back-calculation on England-and-Wales HIV/AIDS diagnosis surveillance "augmented by observed CD4 count levels at diagnosis", jointly estimating incidence, diagnosis rate, time-to-diagnosis trend, time-since-infection and undiagnosed prevalence, and reporting "a decreasing trend in the time to diagnosis and stable levels of incidence in recent years"; its details come from the abstract page only. The CD4 level is what makes that estimator identify both quantities at once, and rare disease has no counterpart clock — which is why P5 survives verification rather than being softened by the precedent, and why the routed row is narrowed to the limb that needs no incidence-anchored frame.
Caveat recorded: the verbatim matches are the page summariser's report, not raw page text.
