## Restatement in my own words (not the generator's framing)

Treat each deep long-read library as a random draw from one gene's set of distinct splice-junction chains. Correct the observed frequency spectrum (above all the singletons) for technical artefacts, using artefact rates measured per read class on spike-ins and technical replicates. Then (a) run an unseen-species estimator (Chao/Good-Turing/coverage-based extrapolation) to say, per gene with an interval, how many biologically real junction chains exist but were not seen; (b) check its forecast of how many new chains a further independent library will show against that library; (c) decide whether the estimated per-gene total settles to a finite, depth-independent number under every plausible artefact model, or keeps growing with depth — in which case "isoforms per gene" is a property of the experiment, not of the gene. The record's own alternative formulation #1 states the headline plainly: is "the number of isoforms of a gene" an estimable quantity with a finite asymptote, or an unbounded function of sequencing depth?

Abstract: "we propose that most alternative splicing events are the result of noise in the splicing process"; "the number of isoforms and their abundance can be predicted by a simple stochastic noise model"; "Based on simulation of sampling of virtual cDNA libraries"; "we estimate that error rates range from 1 to 10% depending on the number of introns and the expression level of a gene". Scope: EST data, 2009; no Chao/Good-Turing/unseen/rarefaction terms; no reverse-transcription artefacts discussed; no explicit finite-vs-infinite statement.

PICKRELL et al. 2010 (opened, publisher). Results: "We see no sign that our identification of isoforms is near saturation"; "deeper sequencing of transcriptomes will likely continue to identify additional low-abundance isoforms"; Fig S1 legend: "Identification of isoforms is not at saturation" (subsampled junction-spanning reads "at varying read depths"). Abstract: "approximately 150,000 previously unannotated splice junctions"; "We estimate that the average intron has a splicing error rate of approximately 0.7%". Results: "this suggests that approximately 2% of transcripts from the average gene are mis-spliced"; "the major splice form accounts for 98.4% of reactions involving each splice site"; unannotated junctions "account for only 1.7% of all junction-spanning sequencing reads"; "one reasonable concern is that we might identify spurious junctions due to mapping or sequencing errors" answered by GT-AG enrichment and frame periodicity. Discussion: "we extrapolate that the majority of different mRNA isoforms present in a cell are not functionally relevant"; the splicing code "defines a probability distribution for each pre-mRNA on a large number of possible splice forms". Strongest single killing point: the question's own alternative formulation #1 — finite asymptote versus unbounded function of depth — was asked and answered in the candidate's own field by Melamud & Moult 2009 ("the number of isoforms and their abundance can be predicted by a simple stochastic noise model" with "the number of observed EST as a surrogate for expression rate"; "only a fraction of all isoforms have so far been observed") and Pickrell et al. 2010 ("Identification of isoforms is not at saturation"), both opened at the publisher; what remains is an estimator choice.

What would reopen it: showing that the per-gene unobserved count under a pre-declared, chemistry-independent reality criterion is NOT predictable from the per-intron error rate and depth alone — i.e., that the stochastic-noise model fails as a generative model of the frequency spectrum — would make limb (a) a new question rather than a re-measurement.
