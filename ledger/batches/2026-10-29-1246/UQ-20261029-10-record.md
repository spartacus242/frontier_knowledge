Question (canonical, as submitted): Is the failure of just-in-time-compiled programs to reach a steady state caused by the profile-guided recompilation feedback loop — does an execution whose compilation decisions are frozen after a declared point converge, while the same (VM, program) pair with the loop live does not?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — the one permitted, for well-posedness only; both versions stand on the record): For a (VM, program) pair set generated by a stated rule — every pair in the published VM × benchmark matrix of Barrett et al. (2017) whose VM has a current release that can pin a per-method compilation plan recorded from a pilot execution — and at a fixed in-process iteration count, what is the difference in the proportion of process executions classified non-convergent by a changepoint rule declared before any execution is timed, between three arms run on one machine in randomised order: (A) live profile-guided recompilation; (B) plan recorded in a pilot execution and pinned from iteration 1 with adaptive recompilation disabled; (C) sham freeze — the same plan pinned only from an iteration after the last compilation observed in the pilot?

**What the repair itself presupposes** (tested as hard as the clause it replaces, per the 2026-10-22 18:33 rule):
- (i) That at least two VMs in that matrix have a *current* release able to pin a recorded per-method plan. **Unverified at my read level.** Of the two replay implementations I opened: Jikes RVM (Georges et al. 2008, Feb-2007 build) does pin per-method optimisation levels from an advice file and runs its timed iteration "with adaptive (re)compilation turned off"; GraalVM's 2025 replay prototype (Pečimúth et al.) replays *compilations* offline — "The dynamic compiler is invoked directly without the need to execute the original application" — so it does **not** supply arm B. I opened no HotSpot or OpenJ9 flag documentation, so I make no claim, in either direction, about `-XX:CompileCommand`, `-XX:+ReplayCompiles`, tiered-compilation flags or OpenJ9 AOT. I do **not** assert the universal "no production VM can do this": that would be an unenumerated universal on a kind of artifact.
- (ii) That a plan recorded in a pilot execution remains a well-defined plan for a VM that, in arm B, no longer collects the profiles its later decisions consume. Pečimúth et al. supply a first-hand caution on exactly this: "the replay file may not contain all the required information when the replayed compilation diverges."
- (iii) That "the last compilation observed in the pilot" is stable enough across executions to define arm C. Because compilation timing is itself nondeterministic across executions, arm C is defined from the pilot and not from the paired live run; the counterfactual "a point after which no recompilation would have occurred" is otherwise per-execution and unobservable in advance.

Alternative formulations (as submitted): Is long-run performance instability in managed runtimes an intrinsic instability of profile-guided recompilation, or is it exogenous to the compiler (collection, operating system, hardware)? / What share of non-converging (VM, program) executions is converted to converging by pinning the compilation plan, against a sham-freeze control?

Significance — consequence: If freezing the plan converts a large, sham-controlled share of non-convergent executions to convergent, the anomaly is self-inflicted by the adaptive optimiser and the target becomes a specific dynamic — profile drift, deoptimisation cycling, inlining cascades — rather than "noise"; VM implementers gain a bug class, and long-running services gain a reason to pin plans deliberately. If there is no drop, the cause is located outside the compiler, which redirects a decade of warmup folklore toward allocator, kernel-scheduler, frequency and layout effects and makes the compiler's exoneration a published fact rather than an untested assumption. Either way an explicitly undetermined cause (P1 §5.2) becomes determined in direction.
Significance — audience: VM implementers (HotSpot, OpenJ9, GraalVM, V8, PyPy) who currently triage such reports anecdotally — query 4 returned a hotspot-dev thread titled "Unexplained Jumps In Response Time 'Fixed' By Disabling Tiered Compilation" and JDK-7159766 "Tiered compilation causes performance regressions", which is this intervention performed informally in the field (returned, not opened, so I cite them as evidence that the practice exists and not for any technical content); the performance-engineering research community (ICPE/SPEC, JMH maintainers) whose steady-state detection rules presuppose convergence is attainable; and operators of latency-sensitive long-running services.
Significance — fecundity: once the loop's causal share is known, the next questions become askable and are not now — which component of the loop (profile invalidation, speculation failure, OSR, code-cache pressure) carries it; whether non-convergence is a bounded-oscillation or a drift phenomenon; whether a pinned plan is a usable tail-latency control in production; whether the same contrast separates VMs by design (tracing vs method-based, as in PyPy vs HotSpot).
Significance — anchoring: P1's unresolved hypothesis about GC/JIT events; the replay-compilation methodology dispute P2 belongs to (whether pinning a plan buys validity or buys bias); and the field reports that disabling tiered compilation "fixes" response-time jumps, which is a folk version of arm B with no control arm and no classification rule.

Reason for silence: a disciplinary boundary plus a split instrument, not inattention. The community that documented the phenomenon (VM measurement) treats compilation nondeterminism as a **nuisance to be controlled for measurement validity**, which is exactly P2's framing, rather than as an **independent variable to be manipulated**; so the plan-pinning machinery was built in a research VM (Jikes RVM, 2007) for comparison rigour and debuggability, and the anomaly was documented a decade later in production VMs that the pinning machinery was never carried into — the two halves of the experiment live in different VMs and different decades, and P3 shows the modern replay effort aimed at compiler debugging with the application not even executed. Add that the vocabulary of arms, pairing and sham controls belongs to experimental science rather than to systems benchmarking, where flipping a flag is normally a speed comparison and nobody would think to add a placebo arm.
