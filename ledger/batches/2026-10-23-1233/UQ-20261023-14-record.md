Question (V1, as received): During externally datable GNSS disturbance epochs, what is the distribution — across independently operated receiver and clock models in the deployed installed base — of the maximum time error actually delivered to dependent infrastructure, and what fraction of that installed base exceeded the time-error limit assumed by the standard governing its application?

Question (canonical, V2 — ONE reformulation taken at Gate 1, for well-posedness only; both versions recorded):
Over a frame declared before any data pull — all (operator, clock-or-receiver-model) strata for which (a) a time-error, phase-offset, holdover-state or alarm record is obtainable at >= 1 sample / 15 min for >= 30 consecutive days spanning a declared disturbance epoch, (b) the record carries a model or holdover-oscillator-class identifier, and (c) the stratum's application is governed by one of the limit-bearing documents given by the enumeration rule below — and with disturbance epochs declared ONLY from evidence generated outside the units under study (national regulator interference bulletins, NOTAMs, ADS-B-derived interference products, GPS NAGUs / Galileo NAGUs and constellation or ground-segment anomaly notices): what is the distribution of maximum |time error| delivered to the dependent application, per epoch and per model stratum (median and upper quantiles, with the number of contributing units named), and what fraction of units exceeded the numeric limit its governing document states, with a Clopper-Pearson interval and with the share of the nominal denominator lost to missing records reported?

Diff V1 -> V2: V1 left four things undeclared — the frame ("the deployed installed base" is not a set), K and N (how many models, how many operators), the limit set ("the standard governing its application" names no documents), and the record cadence that makes "maximum time error" a measurable rather than a notional quantity. Alternative formulations:
- When GNSS is jammed, spoofed or misbehaving, how far do real deployed clocks actually drift, and how many of them break the time-error limit their application assumes?

Motivating gap: a large, geographically distributed, independently operated population of GNSS-disciplined clocks now logs time error or holdover state continuously (utility PMU and substation clocks in NASPI-adjacent archives; telecom PRTC/ePRTC and PTP boundary clocks; public time servers), while GNSS interference has itself become continuously mapped and externally datable from ADS-B-derived interference products, regulator bulletins and NOTAMs — so exposure can be defined WITHOUT using the receivers' own output. The pairing of a dated exposure series with a fleet of delivered-time-error logs is the new instrument. ## Gate 3 — Significance

Consequence: if the exceedance fraction is materially above zero, the limits in ITU-T G.8271.1/G.8271.2 and the substation and synchrophasor profiles are being specified against a network-engineering budget that the disturbance environment routinely overruns, and the quantity that resilience requirements (holdover oscillator class, assisted PRTC, terrestrial backup) should be sized against is an operating distribution nobody has. Evidence that this is the live design question and that it is currently answered from test conditions: every resilience document my searches returned is either a vendor design deck (ITSF/WSTS ePRTC and hardened-GNSS material, queries 1, 4, 7) or a simulated/bench measurement (P3), and the one operator presentation in the intake measures a terrestrial alternative path with no disturbance performed at all. If the exceedance fraction is bounded near zero with stated power, that is equally consequential in the other direction: it would license the current limits and reclassify GNSS-timing resilience spending as insurance against a tail that the installed base's own holdover already absorbs — and it would turn P1's alarm-and-holdover record from an alarming anecdote into a demonstration that the protective mechanism worked.
Audience: ITU-T SG15 Q13 (which owns the G.827x time-error limits the second limb scores against), IEEE PSRC and IEC TC57 (C37.238, 61850-9-3), the NASPI Time Synchronization Task Force — which is demonstrably the acting community, because it commissioned and published exactly the shape of study P2 is, for a different epoch class — and telecom sync operators plus utility protection engineers, who would act by changing holdover-class procurement and alarm thresholds rather than by publishing. Secondarily the regulators and EU bodies now producing the exposure side (Council doc ST-9188-2025, returned at query 9).
Fecundity: once a delivered-error distribution exists by model and oscillator class, several questions become askable that cannot be asked now — whether multi-constellation capability reduces delivered error under real disturbance or merely changes its failure mode (P3 asks this of one receiver in simulation); whether the MTIE alarm thresholds that actually fired in P1 are the right detectors for the errors that matter; whether standard limits should carry an exposure-conditioned term the way observation-error models are now being asked to; and the inverse question, whether a fleet of delivered-time-error logs is itself an independent interference detector.
Anchoring: the GNSS-timing resilience debate, which currently prices vulnerability from test conditions; the unanswered part of the 26 January 2016 post-mortem (P1 ends with the qualitative "Long holdover with low grade oscillators could well lead to service impact in telecom networks" and no distribution); NASPI's own leap-second work, which left the fraction limb unprinted; and the legal-metrology practice of in-service conformance surveillance (query 12), which has the method and not the instrument class.

Reason for silence: an INCENTIVE AND DISCLOSURE GAP WITH A NAMED CONVENTION, which is the mechanism I can source rather than assert. The acting community's own flagship fleet report states that it reports "without identifying either device vendors or models nor the affected companies" (P2, Section 1) — the two join keys the question needs, model and operator, are removed at publication by policy, because the reporting exists to share lessons without naming vendors or exposing utilities. The complementary half is that the only model-resolved record I found is a vendor's own support-desk estate (P1), i.e. commercially owned and published as a case study rather than as a surveyed population with a denominator. Reinforced by a disciplinary boundary: the method — surveying a deployed instrument population for exceedance of a maximum permissible error — is mature and named in legal metrology (OIML G 20, WELMEC 4.2, national in-service inspection regimes, query 12), and clocks delivering time to infrastructure fall outside its instrument scope, so the community with the method has no mandate and the communities with the data have a convention against publishing the keys. And partly new instruments: continuous externally datable interference mapping is recent, so the exposure half of the pairing did not exist when P1 and P2 were written.
