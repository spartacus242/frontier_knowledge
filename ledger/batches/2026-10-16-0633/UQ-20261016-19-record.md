Domain(s): Biology / biomedicine — research methods and metascience; instrument and facility telemetry (shared research infrastructure)

Question (canonical, Gate 0 intake version):
Using core-facility electronic run and booking records (iLab, PPMS, LIMS — retained for years because they are billing records) linked to the figures eventually published from those projects, how many runs of a given assay were performed per published run, and how does that attempt-to-publication ratio vary by assay type, laboratory, and the direction of the published result?

Question (canonical, Gate 1 reformulation — ONE reformulation used, for well-posedness only; this is the version carried forward):
In a defined corpus of shared-facility assay records (one or more core facilities using iLab/PPMS/LIMS, with per-run time stamps, user, project/fund code and sample count), restricted to *linked project-assay units* — a (laboratory, project/fund code, assay type, sample class) cell whose runs fall inside a project window ending at the submission date of a paper that the facility's own acknowledgement/citation tracking attributes to that project — define the attempt-to-publication ratio R = (runs logged in the cell) / (runs whose output is traceable to a figure or supplementary panel of the linked paper, counted by the paper's own stated n of independent experiments for that panel). Then: (i) what is the distribution of R overall and per assay type; (ii) how much of its variance sits between laboratories versus between projects within a laboratory (random-effects decomposition); (iii) is R associated, in a pre-registered test, with the direction of the linked published result (coded as "supports the paper's stated hypothesis / non-null in the claimed direction" versus "null or contrary"), adjusting for sample count, assay type and calendar time; and (iv) does the association survive a negative control of assays whose runs are inherently repeated for calibration or QC (e.g. daily cytometer CST/bead runs, MS system-suitability injections), where R must be large for reasons that carry no reporting content?

Alternative formulations:
- What is the file-drawer ratio *inside* the laboratory — how many times was the experiment run before the version that appears in the paper?
- Can billing-grade facility logs reconstruct the unreported attempt count behind a published figure, and is that count associated with whether the published finding is positive?

Motivating gap:
NEW INSTRUMENT. Selective reporting has only ever been measured from published artefacts (p-curves, excess-significance tests, registry-to-publication comparisons, image forensics) or from self-report (surveys, interviews). Shared-facility management systems now hold per-run, per-user, per-sample, time-stamped records going back years *because finance requires it for cost recovery* — a record of the research process at a granularity that did not exist before facility billing was computerised, and that nobody has turned on reporting behaviour. Neves & Amaral state the blocking problem outright — detecting unreported experiments "usually requires information readers don't have" — and then propose prevention (predefined exclusion criteria) rather than measurement. The candidate proposes the measurement, deliberately posed as a ratio and its correlates, never as misconduct detection.

Presuppositions:
- P1 (contested): facility records are retrievable and linkable to projects and then to papers — requires institutional cooperation plus a linkage route (fund/grant number, acknowledgement text, PI and date window). Partly supported: facilities already run acknowledgement/citation tracking for their own renewal reporting, and RRID-for-core-facility and equipment-discovery work (USEDiT) exists to make equipment-to-paper attribution machine-readable; but no published study has used the linkage for this purpose.
- P2 (CONTESTED): a logged run is attributable to a published figure. Method optimisation, training runs, other projects of the same lab, and instrument-fault reruns all confound it; the Gate 1 reformulation narrows the estimand to runs of the same assay on the same sample class inside one project window and reads the denominator off the paper's own stated n.
- P3 (CONTESTED — the crux): a high rerun count indicates selection rather than legitimate optimisation or technical failure. The question therefore reports R and its correlates and tests only the result-direction association; intent is never inferred. The direction test is what carries the inferential load, because optimisation and instrument faults should be independent of which way the eventual result points.
- P4 (contested, real feasibility constraint): an ethical and consent path exists for analysing billing records that identify individual researchers — plausibly only via aggregation and institutional review, which may force the unit of analysis up to the laboratory (and would then cost the within-laboratory variance component in (ii)).
- P5 (contested): facility-mediated assays are representative of the lab's experimental work at all. Benchtop work leaves no telemetry, so the measurement is confined to instrument-mediated assays (flow cytometry, sequencing, mass spectrometry, imaging, cell sorting); the restriction must be stated, not hidden.
- P6 (contested): retention periods actually cover the publication lag. A 3-7 year financial retention window may or may not reach back past typical submission-to-publication plus project-to-submission lag; this is checkable per facility before any analysis and determines the usable corpus.

What would count as an answer:
A distribution of attempt-to-publication ratios over a defined corpus (one or more facilities; N linked project-assay units and their matched papers), reported per assay type with a within-laboratory variance component, plus the pre-specified result-direction test and the calibration-assay negative control. An answer that R is near one for most assay types and heavy-tailed for a few named ones is as informative as a large mean. A demonstration that the linkage cannot be made at acceptable error rates (quantified: false attribution rate of runs to projects, and of projects to papers, against a hand-audited subsample) is a feasibility verdict, not an answer to the question, and must be reported as such.

Novelty — how this differs from each:
- vs Neves & Amaral 2020: same phenomenon, opposite move. They argue the attempt count is unobservable to readers and therefore propose process reform; the candidate claims the count is in fact observable for instrument-mediated assays, from records kept for billing, and asks for its distribution and its correlation with result direction. Theirs is a prescription with no data; the candidate is an estimand with a stated corpus, variance decomposition, pre-registered direction test and negative control.
- vs Kos-Braun et al. 2020: same setting (core facilities), different epistemics. Theirs is facility staff answering questions about quality practices; the candidate treats the facility's transaction log as a measuring instrument on the users' reporting behaviour, and the unit of analysis is a (laboratory, project, assay, sample class) cell linked to a specific paper, not a facility's self-description.
- vs Magas 2026: both take run-level attempts seriously as data, but the source, the estimand and the audience differ. The ELN proposal harvests attempt narratives from notebooks (self-curated, sparse, voluntarily open, no retention mandate) to train and evaluate AI scientific debugging; the candidate uses billing-grade facility telemetry (mandatory, complete for the instrument, time-stamped, retained for finance reasons) to estimate an attempt-to-publication ratio and test an association with published result direction. Nothing in it computes a ratio or touches publication linkage. This is the closest hit found and the reason novelty is Medium rather than High.
- vs GMP audit-trail enforcement: the mechanism is a direct precedent — regulators already read instrument logs for unreported runs — but the object, the estimand and the purpose are different. Enforcement is per-firm, aimed at a compliance verdict on specific analysts, inside a regime with statutory inspection authority and no publication in sight. The candidate has no authority, aggregates to a population, deliberately refuses the intent inference, and its outcome variable is the direction of a published scientific claim. Its existence is also evidence for the reason-for-silence mechanism below rather than against novelty of the question.

Significance — consequence:
If R is near one for most instrument-mediated assay types, then experiment-stage selection is small where it can be seen, and the reform case shifts toward analysis-stage selection and toward benchtop work — a redirection of effort that currently rests on nothing but inference from published artefacts. If R is heavy-tailed in named assay types, the question localises the file drawer to specific techniques, which is directly actionable: reporting requirements and predefined exclusion criteria can be demanded for those assays rather than for everything. If R is associated with the direction of the published result, that is the first process-side evidence of experiment-level selection that does not run through the published artefact or through self-report, and it would calibrate p-curve and excess-significance estimates against an independent measurement; if it is not, the artefact-side literature loses a mechanism it has assumed, and the burden moves back to analysis-stage choices. A feasibility verdict (linkage impossible at acceptable error rates) is also consequential: it tells the integrity and open-science community that this attractive-looking data source cannot bear the weight, before more institutions build governance around it.

Significance — audience:
Core-facility directors and shared-resource managers (they hold the records, already run acknowledgement tracking for renewals, and are already being asked for rigour statements — the UW Vision Research Core rigour statement and the NIH rigour policy are the live context); research-integrity and research-quality offices, which currently have no process-side metric at all; journals and funders setting replicate-reporting rules, who would gain an auditable denominator for the "n independent experiments" line; meta-researchers who have been confined to artefact-side inference; and facility-funding bodies, who already mine the same logs for utilisation and cost recovery and would learn that the logs carry scientific as well as financial information. Each of these would act differently: facilities on what they log and disclose, journals on what they require, meta-researchers on which estimates they trust.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: calibration of artefact-side selective-reporting estimates against a process-side measurement; the cost of a published figure in instrument hours and facility spend, i.e. research-waste accounting with a real denominator instead of an extrapolated one; whether attempt count predicts later replication failure or retraction; whether a facility-issued "runs performed" provenance line on a figure changes how readers and reviewers weigh it (a testable intervention); whether R responds to preregistration, registered reports or predefined exclusion criteria, which turns Neves & Amaral's prescription into something measurable; and the comparison of facility-mediated with benchtop work, which defines the limits of all telemetry-based metascience.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the file-drawer problem and publication bias; the open question inside the reproducibility crisis of where selection actually happens (experiment stage versus analysis stage), which artefact-side methods cannot separate; the explicit unobservability claim in Neves & Amaral 2020; instrument-PID, RRID-for-core-facility and equipment-attribution infrastructure (USEDiT), which exists for credit and would be repurposed for measurement; GMP audit-trail practice, which proves the measurement is physically possible; and research-waste quantification.

Reason for silence:
Two mechanisms, both structural rather than conceptual. (a) Custodianship and incentive boundary: the records live in finance and administration systems owned by facility managers and billing offices, built and justified for cost recovery, and have never been catalogued as research data. Meta-research works on published artefacts and registries because those are public and need no institutional cooperation; the only people who can reach the logs are the same people whose users and funders they depend on, and any analysis of them reads as surveillance or misconduct screening of named colleagues. That turns a measurement into a governance problem that must be solved before it can even be proposed — which is exactly why the one adjacent study of core facilities (eLife 2020;9:e62212) asked facility leaders questions instead of reading their logs. The GMP precedent sharpens this: where an institution already holds inspection authority, the identical instrument is used routinely, so the academic silence is about authority and consent, not about the idea. (b) New instrument: continuous, computerised, per-run facility records only became general through the 2010s with iLab/PPMS-class adoption, so only now does a retention window plausibly reach back past a publication lag (P6) — the data needed to ask this question has existed for less than one research generation, and existed first in the finance office rather than the lab.
