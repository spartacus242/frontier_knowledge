# UQ-20261016-21 — independent verification of the load-bearing prior/kill

Fresh-context checker, neutral framing, handed the claim and its source but not the reviewer's reasoning. 11 queries, 0 refused.

## Claim under test: `UQ-21-neoprom`

## Verdict: **PARTLY_CONFIRMED**

## What the sources actually say

The claim's descriptions of the two oximeter sources are accurate. Its conclusion — that these make the proposed study a known question — is not supported.

1. NeOProM, Askie et al., JAMA 2018;319(21):2190-2201 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6583054/). Oximeter software era was PRE-SPECIFIED, not post hoc. Methods list "type of oximeter software (original vs revised)" as the last of nine pre-specified subgroup factors (the others: gestational age <26 vs >=26 weeks; inborn/outborn; any antenatal corticosteroids; sex; small for gestational age; multiple birth; vaginal/cesarean; intervention commenced <6 vs >=6 hours), tested "by including a treatment x subgroup interaction term in the model," "with no adjustment for multiple comparisons." Five trials contributed; three "changed their oximeters to incorporate the revised oximeter software" (BOOST II UK, BOOST II Australia, Canadian Oxygen Trial), while SUPPORT (1316) and BOOST NZ (340) used original software only; 74 Canadian Oxygen Trial infants were exposed to both and are excluded from the relevant figure. The interaction attaches to DEATH by corrected age 18-24 months, not to the primary composite: original software RR 1.06 (95% CI 0.91-1.23), P=.47; revised RR 1.38 (95% CI 1.14-1.68), P=.001; "P = .03 for interaction subgroup difference." So the claim's "P = .03 for death" is correct and correctly attributed. For the primary outcome the paper reports no between-group difference by "oximeter software type" and gives no interaction p-value. Discussion: "in the prespecified subgroup analysis of original vs revised oximeter software," and "There has been considerable debate among the study investigators" over "whether the change in oximeter software was responsible for this result" — i.e. the authors treat the mechanism as unsettled, and name "the oximeter software change on mortality" as future research.

CAVEAT ON QUOTING: the page-reading tool returned short fragments under a per-quote character limit rather than whole sentences. The figures, p-values, subgroup list and the quoted phrases above are reliable; I did not obtain the full unbroken sentence for the P=.03 result.

2. BOOST II, NEJM 2013;368:2094-2104 (https://portaldeboaspraticas.iff.fiocruz.br/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/nejmoa1302298.pdf). The claim's quotation is accurate: the abstract states "There was heterogeneity for mortality between the original algorithm and the revised algorithm (P=0.006)," continuing "but not for other outcomes." Mechanism: "standard Masimo Radical oximeters returned fewer oxygen-saturation values in the range of 87 to 90% than expected"; "we found that there was a shift up in the oximeter-calibration curve between 87% and 90%," which "caused values ranging from 87 to 96% to read 1 to 2% higher"; "Masimo supplied software with a revised calibration algorithm that eliminated the problem." Rollout: "between December 2008 and May 2009, oximeters in the United Kingdom and Australian trials were changed to the new calibration algorithm"; "The New Zealand trial oximeters were not changed because recruitment had nearly finished." Split: 1261 (51.5%) original algorithm, 1187 (48.5%) revised. Non-significant heterogeneity for ROP P=0.91, NEC P=0.59, IVH P=0.48, other brain injury P=0.34, PDA P=0.83, oxygen dependence at 36 weeks P=0.83, BPD P=0.59. The tool also read Figure 3 as labelling death at 36 weeks "P=0.001 for heterogeneity" — treat that as a lower-confidence figure reading, not text.

3. BMJ Open 2025;15(6):e097097 (https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/15/6/e097097). This does NOT support the claim's framing. It is a systematic review with thematic synthesis (PROSPERO CRD42023427704) of studies citing IDEAL/IDEAL-D publications, searched in Web of Science and Scopus in July 2023 — 104 studies (87 reports, 17 protocols) from 1,071 records, 425 modifications in 76 studies. What it counts is predominantly SURGICAL, not device-technical: surgical procedure modifications 283 (66.6%), device modifications 94 (22.1%) — and those device modifications cover "components, number used, size, and how the device is applied," with no mention of software or firmware — plus patient selection 48 (11.3%). Record source is published reports and protocols only; registries, regulatory records and manufacturer records are not used, and grey literature and non-English publications were excluded. It does NOT link modifications to estimated treatment effects: only 44 studies (42.3%) "described implications," meaning benefits and/or drawbacks, and no risk-of-bias assessment was performed. SMART is the "Surgical ModificAtion Reporting Tool" (Bristol), a reporting checklist — not an effect-estimate analysis.

ANSWER, LIMB 1 (how often a mid-enrolment manufacturer firmware or calibration revision can be established from manufacturer, regulatory or recall records, across device-mediated RCTs generally): NOT asked and answered anywhere I could find. Nothing in 11 queries surveys device-mediated RCTs for mid-enrolment revisions using manufacturer, regulatory or recall records. The nearest adjacent work is weaker on every axis: BMJ Open e097097 counts modifications from published IDEAL-citing reports, not regulatory records; the Keele/Collins-Woolley wearables work (https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/output/414733/investigation-of-wearable-health-tracker-version-updates, protocol at https://www.protocols.io/view/version-reporting-of-wearables-in-clinical-trials-dqiw5ufe.pdf) extracts version reporting from ClinicalTrials.gov records, protocols, SAPs and linked publications — it records which versions trials report, explicitly does not track change over the enrolment window, does not use manufacturer or regulatory sources, and does not estimate treatment effects. Even in NeOProM and BOOST II themselves, the revision was established from the trialists' own conduct records, not from manufacturer or regulatory filings.

ANSWER, LIMB 2 (does the treatment effect differ by device era by more than the trial's own MCID, beyond the oximeter family): NOT asked and answered beyond the oximeter trial family. The oximeter family (BOOST II 2013, NeOProM 2018, plus the Stenson BOOST II Australia/UK oximeter-change paper) is the only device-era treatment-effect stratification I located, and even there the comparison is framed as a subgroup interaction p-value, not against a pre-declared MCID. The meta-epidemiological literature on trial-level characteristics associated with effect estimates (e.g. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0159267, https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-6-201209180-00537) covers allocation concealment, blinding, registration, sample size, funding and trial age — publication year as a proxy for era (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0895435622000361) is the closest analogue, and it is not device-specific. I found no cross-device MCID-referenced device-era analysis.

## Effect on the ledger verdict

The ledger verdict should change. The claim is factually right about what NeOProM and BOOST II report — pre-specified subgroup, five trials, P=.03 for death at 18-24 months, and the verbatim BOOST II heterogeneity sentence — so any ledger entry doubting those specifics should be corrected to accurate. But the verdict that the proposed study is therefore "a known question rather than a new one" does not stand and should be reversed to "not established; the proposed question remains open," for four reasons. (1) Scope: NeOProM is one device family, one manufacturer, one revision event, in one clinical domain; the proposal is about device-mediated RCTs generally. A single instance does not answer a prevalence question. (2) Record source: the proposal's first limb is specifically about establishing revisions from manufacturer, regulatory or recall records. NeOProM and BOOST II established the revision from their own trial conduct, and the only census-type study offered (BMJ Open e097097) draws on published reports and protocols via Web of Science and Scopus, uses no regulatory or manufacturer records, does not cover software or firmware, and does not link modifications to effect estimates. The third cited source therefore does not do the work the claim assigns it. (3) Metric: NeOProM reports an unadjusted interaction p-value with "no adjustment for multiple comparisons" among nine pre-specified subgroups; the proposal asks whether the difference exceeds the trial's own MCID, which no source I found addresses. (4) The NeOProM authors themselves treat the question as unresolved — "considerable debate among the study investigators" over whether the software change caused the result, and they name it as future research. That is the opposite of a settled question. Residual uncertainty: my searches were English-language web searches and the BMJ Open and PMC readings came through a page-summarising tool with a per-quote character cap, so I could not verify full sentences in those two; a dedicated regulatory-database or FDA-submission literature search could still surface prior work on limb 1.
