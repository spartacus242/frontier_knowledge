## Gate 0 — Intake

Question (canonical), V1 as submitted:
In hospitals that log ward-level and network-leg oxygen flow and pipeline pressure at minute resolution alongside EHR records of each patient's prescribed oxygen device and flow, does the oxygen delivered at the bedside depart from the prescribed setting as a function of concurrent aggregate demand on the same network leg, and does that departure explain a measurable share of within-patient variation in a pre-declared oxygenation endpoint (SpO2 time-in-target, FiO2 shortfall, device escalation)?

Question (canonical), V2 — the one reformulation permitted at Gate 1, for well-posedness only; V2 is the version graded:
On a declared set of hospital wards served by identified medical-gas pipeline legs, with (a) leg flow and leg/terminal pressure logged at <=1 min resolution, (b) delivered flow measured at instrumented terminal units on a sampled subset of outlets, and (c) each patient-hour's prescribed device and flowmeter setting retrievable from the EHR: (i) is delivered-minus-set flow at an outlet (secondary: delivered-minus-intended FiO2) a function of EXOGENOUS leg load — concurrent leg flow generated by patients OTHER than the index patient, expressed as a fraction of that leg's engineering design flow, with the design flow as a breakpoint declared before any data pull; and (ii) among patients carrying continuous SpO2, what share of within-patient variation in a pre-declared oxygenation endpoint (SpO2 time-in-target; FiO2 shortfall; device escalation) is attributable to that exogenous load, estimated with within-patient contrasts and a negative-control leg?
(V2 changes only: exposure defined to exclude the index patient's own draw; the primary measurand named as delivered-minus-set flow at an instrumented terminal unit rather than the ambiguous "oxygen delivered"; the clinical limb restricted to patient-hours with an outcome sampled fast enough to resolve a load episode. No change of subject.)

Alternative formulations:
- Lay: Is a patient's oxygen dose partly set by how much oxygen the rest of the hospital is using at that moment?
- Technical: Using pipeline flow and pressure telemetry as the exposure and per-patient oxygenation as the outcome, estimate the load-dependent delivery shortfall and its clinical footprint, with the leg's engineering design flow as the pre-declared breakpoint.

Source method: Silence mapping — the engineering side is dense with how-answers (pipe sizing, diversity factors, consumption dashboards, alarm levels derived from pipe capacity) and the clinical side is dense with how-answers (targets, devices, escalation); the joining why-question (does one patient's delivered oxygen depend on the others' concurrent draw?) is where the two literatures stop.

P3. Delivered flow at the bedside can be measured or validly inferred — CONTESTED. Terminal-unit flows are not normally logged; V2 therefore requires instrumented outlets on a sample, or inference from leg flow and device mix with its error characterised. P4. Pipeline supply can actually reduce delivered flow at the outlet within the load range observed — CONTESTED, and the presupposition most able to dissolve the question. NOT established that regulated delivery at the outlet falls before a hard limit; V2's null-with-detection-bound clause is what keeps the question answerable if it does not.
P5. The oxygenation endpoint is sampled often enough to resolve load episodes — established where continuous SpO2 exists; CONTESTED on general wards with intermittent observations. V2 restricts the clinical limb accordingly.
P6. Concurrent load is not simply a proxy for ward acuity — CONTESTED, the main confounding threat; handled by exogenous (other-patient) load, within-patient contrasts and a negative-control leg, not by assertion.

## Gate 1 — Well-posedness

What would count as an answer:
A load-response curve of delivered-minus-set flow (secondary: FiO2 shortfall) against exogenous leg demand as a fraction of that leg's design flow, measured on instrumented terminal units in >=2 hospitals across >=1 high-demand period, with the engineering design flow declared as the breakpoint before any data pull; plus the estimated share of within-patient variation in the pre-declared oxygenation endpoint attributable to other patients' concurrent load, from within-patient and negative-control contrasts. A flat curve through and beyond design flow, reported with the minimum shortfall the instrumentation could have detected, answers the question negatively and retires a concern surge planning currently carries unquantified. Both branches are reportable, which is what makes this a question rather than a thesis.

Not a thesis in disguise: the question's negative answer is a publishable, decision-relevant result (it would let demand-management plans stop rationing on an unmeasured hypothesis), and the intake does not assert a direction.

Well-posedness score: 3/3 (after the single permitted reformulation). Residual weakness: the primary measurand requires instrumentation that does not exist by default, so the design's feasibility, not its definition, is where it is fragile.

## Gate 3 — Significance

Consequence — if YES (delivery degrades with load): a flowmeter setting stops being a dose under surge, which changes three things that are currently decided on pipe geometry alone. (i) MGPS alarm thresholds, today set from "pre-determined levels as calculated by pipe design and industrial copper pipe capacity flow rates" (Leicester, intake), acquire a patient-outcome basis and can be set at the load where delivery starts to fall rather than where copper runs out. (ii) Demand-management plans of the St Helier type gain a target: restrict on the legs and at the loads where shortfall is measurable, instead of hospital-wide. (iii) HSSIB's R/2021/134 HTM revision would have a measured exposure-response to write design flow against, where its own investigation had only an unresolved single case. If NO (flat through and beyond design flow, with a stated detection bound): a concern that surge planning and the COVID-era alerts carry unquantified is retired, and the regulated-delivery assumption that lets clinicians treat the wall outlet as an infinite source is, for the first time, evidence rather than custom.
Audience: (a) critical-care and respiratory clinicians titrating to SpO2 targets, who would learn whether their delivered dose has a hospital-wide covariate; (b) hospital authorised persons for medical gases and the medical gas committees HSSIB wrote its recommendations to, who own the telemetry but have no patient endpoint; (c) NHS England estates policy, mid-revision of HTM 02-01 under R/2021/134; (d) oxygen-system programmes sizing PSA plants and distribution in low-resource hospitals (PATH's PSA-disrepair work and the Nigerian oxygen-systems programme of P2 show both the sizing audience and that its current exposure variable is capability, not concurrent load). Each of these would act on a number they do not have.
Fecundity: once a load-response curve exists — is a leg's effective capacity a function of device mix (HFNC versus low-flow), so that the same litres are more or less damaging depending on who is drawing them? Does ward oxygen flow telemetry work as a leading indicator of deterioration clusters, making the exposure double as surveillance? Should alarm levels be clinically rather than hydraulically derived? And a measurement question for the trial literature: if delivered FiO2 drifts with ward load, oxygenation-target trials run through surges carry an exposure-measurement error nobody has bounded.
Anchoring: HSSIB's explicitly unresolved attribution and its finding that the guidance "is outdated and does not reflect developments in oxygen therapy" (summariser-relayed fragment, opening clipped); the NHSE/I alert that some hospitals drew more than their design flow; the flow-metrology finding that meters have "good precision and poor accuracy" against their own setting, which bounds how small a load effect could be seen; the oxygenation-target literature's assumption that a prescribed setting is a delivered dose.
Significance score: 3/3 — a named, currently-mid-revision policy process and two named operational audiences would act either way, and the negative answer is as usable as the positive one.

## Reason for silence

Primary mechanism: a disciplinary boundary that coincides with an instrument-ownership boundary. The exposure lives in the estates/BMS telemetry stack, whose endpoint is the pipe — its alarms are derived from copper capacity, its reports are by ward, department and leg, and its stated aim is to act before there is any issue to patient care rather than to measure patient care. The outcome lives in the EHR, whose exposure variable is the prescription. No institutional actor owns the join: the medical gas committee is an engineering governance body with no outcome data, and the clinical oxygen literature treats the wall outlet as a boundary condition rather than a shared resource. HSSIB came closest and shows the boundary operating in the open — it investigated a pressure drop and two desaturations together, then scoped clinical management out of the report and issued engineering and governance recommendations only.
Secondary mechanism: the measurement at the join does not exist by default. Terminal-unit delivered flow is not logged anywhere in routine practice (hence V2's instrumented-outlet requirement, and hence a commercial patent for point-of-delivery monitoring), so even in a hospital holding both halves there is no dataset to analyse — a new-instrument silence on top of the boundary silence.
Not claimed: that nobody has ever worried about this. The worry is documented (alerts, AARC, HSSIB). What is silent is the quantity.
