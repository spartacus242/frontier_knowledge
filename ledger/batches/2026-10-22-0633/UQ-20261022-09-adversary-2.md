## Restatement in my own words

The record offers two wordings, and they are not the same question.

**V1 (canonical, as intaken).** Take people using a commercial automated insulin delivery (AID) system whose cloud logs capture (a) whatever the controller did to itself and (b) every human change to the pump's settings, with timestamps. For one such human settings change, how much does the person's glucose control actually change afterwards — and how much of that is the *edit's* doing rather than the controller's own concurrent self-adjustment?

**V2 (the single permitted Gate 1 reformulation, and the version the record scores).** Narrow it to systems whose controller genuinely adapts its own profile (MiniMed 780G-class auto-basal, Omnipod 5-class adaptive basal), in ordinary clinic care rather than a trial; take vendor cloud logs that separately timestamp the controller's adaptation and the human edits and say who made each edit; pre-register one design (target-trial emulation against matched no-change person-time, or a regression discontinuity at visit dates) and one metric for "how much the controller adapted"; then estimate, per settings class (basal, correction factor, target, carb ratio, activity mode), the within-person effect of an edit on TIR 70–180 and TBR <54 at 2, 4 and 12 weeks, with the controller's concurrent adaptation entered **as a covariate**, plus negative controls and two feasibility denominators.

So: V1 asks "does changing the settings help?"; V2 asks "does a human edit buy anything on top of a machine that is already retuning itself, and for which settings?" The whole claim to novelty lives in V2's extra clause. That clause is where I attacked.

## The mechanism check: is the controller's adaptation a covariate at all?

**Source C — Omnipod 5 algorithm, manufacturer HCP page.** READ LEVEL: **full page**.
**REGIME:** population = Omnipod 5 users in automated mode; unit = the algorithm's internal adaptive state; operating condition = after ≥48 h wear and the first Pod change; instrument = **manufacturer education page, a description of a mechanism, not a measurement**; threshold = n/a; category = device mechanism. **Publishes no measured quantity — it DEFINES the mechanism.** Quoted as served:

- *"The Omnipod 5 algorithm is driven primarily by total daily insulin (TDI)."*
- *"With each Pod change, the algorithm utilizes the updated TDI, based on the user's actual insulin delivered."*
- Changes to programmed basal rates or basal constraints have **no impact on the algorithm** after initiation; the programmed basal matters only for the pre-first-Pod TDI estimate. Stronger bolus settings may raise TDI, which in turn raises automated delivery.

Two consequences, both fatal to V2 as worded:

1. **The adaptation is downstream of the edit.** The adaptive state is recomputed from *insulin actually delivered*, and insulin actually delivered is a consequence of the human edit (the page says so explicitly for bolus settings). So "the adaptation the system performed during the same period" is, at least in part, an effect of the exposure. V2 instructs that it be entered "as a **pre-declared covariate**." Subtracting a consequence of the edit from the edit's effect does not isolate the edit's contribution — it removes part of it. The estimand V1/V2 names is therefore not the effect of the edit. This is a definitional point about what the phrase "over and above" picks out given the mechanism, not a statistical theorem, so I am not resting it on an uncited result. (I would *label* it over-adjustment on a post-treatment variable, but I opened no methods source in this run and so do not cite that framing as evidence.) Note that the record's own Gate 1 raised exactly this trichotomy — "covariate? mediator? competing exposure?" — and V2 then picked *covariate* without argument.
2. **One of the candidate's exposure classes is specified null a priori.** Basal-rate edits are the most frequently made class (O'Malley: 69% of CLC encounters, 80% of SAP). On an Omnipod 5-class controller the manufacturer states they do nothing after initiation. So for the candidate's "and for which settings?" limb, the answer for the largest class is available from a vendor web page without any study.

**Weakness of this leg, stated plainly:** Source C is a vendor education page, not a peer-reviewed or regulatory mechanism description, and it covers **Omnipod 5 only**. I have no sourced mechanism for 780G auto-basal and do not assert one. V2 names two classes; this leg is sourced for one of them.

### Multi-leg disclosure

My kill is multi-leg and each leg is sourced separately.

- **Leg 4a** (no public instrument carries the fields) rests on `aidR`'s two format vignettes, read in full, corroborated by three real CareLink CSV headers.
- **Leg 4b** (authorship is not a log property) rests on the NCT05204134 protocol, quoted, plus the enumerated absence in 4a.
- **Leg 4c** (adaptation is downstream of the edit, so "over and above" is incoherent) rests on the Omnipod 5 HCP page, quoted.
- **Leg 2** (V1 is asked and answered) rests on O'Malley 2021, opened and quoted by me at abstract level.

**The leg I would stand on if the others fell: 4c.** 4a and 4b are data-availability findings, and a manufacturer with internal data could in principle defeat both; 4c is a defect in the estimand itself that better data does not repair. Its own limit, stated: it is sourced from a vendor education page and for **Omnipod 5 only**, so a sourced demonstration that 780G auto-basal adapts independently of user edits would confine 4c to half of V2's eligible population — and that is the single thing that would most weaken this log.

### What would overturn this log

A read of PMC10698772 (still unidentified); a 780G mechanism source showing auto-basal adaptation is not a function of delivered insulin; or an actual 780G/Omnipod 5 **vendor research** export, as opposed to the self-service export, shown to carry timestamped settings edits with authorship and a logged adaptation state. Any of those would reopen attack 4.
