Question (canonical, Gate 0 as intaken):
Computed with the safeguards discipline of material-balance accounting — an inventory-difference statistic, a propagated measurement-and-documentation variance, and a stated detection probability at a stated false-alarm rate — what is the smallest sustained controlled-substance diversion rate that a hospital's reconciliation and analytics can detect per dispensing unit per month, and how does that minimum detectable rate compare with the quantities involved in adjudicated diversion cases?

Question (canonical, Gate 1 reformulation — ONE reformulation used, for well-posedness only; both versions stand on the record):
For a named dispensing unit (one ADC-served ward or OR suite) and a named drug-formulation class (e.g. injectable fentanyl), define the monthly inventory difference ID = (beginning inventory + receipts) - (recorded administered + documented waste + returns + ending inventory) from cabinet transaction logs, administration records and waste attestations. With a propagated uncertainty sigma_ID in which every term is declared either a measured variance or an interval bound (witnessed waste being unmeasured by construction), what is the smallest sustained monthly removal M (mg, or doses) at which each of three specified tests — (a) the unit's current discrepancy-review practice, (b) a reproducible vendor alarm rule, (c) a safeguards-style sequential test on cumulative ID (CUMUF / Page-type) — attains 95% detection probability at a false-alarm rate of one per unit-year; and how does M compare with per-month removal rates reconstructed from adjudicated diversion cases?
Why the reformulation: the intake version left the estimand under-determined in three ways — "can detect" named no test, no detection probability or false-alarm rate was fixed per unit time, and "diversion rate" did not say sustained-removal-per-unit-month in mass or dose units. The reformulation fixes the test set, the (Pd, Pfa) pair, the accounting identity and the units. The substantive question is unchanged.

Alternative formulations:
- What is the detection limit of hospital drug-diversion surveillance, stated as the smallest diversion detectable at a fixed false-alarm rate, rather than as a classifier's performance on cases that were already found?
- Can an inventory-difference statistic and its sigma, as used for nuclear material accountancy, be computed for a hospital opioid inventory — and what does it imply about how much can be removed without the accounts noticing?

Motivating gap:
Published and marketed diversion detection is framed as classifier performance against already-identified cases. The AJHP 2022 machine-learning study (opened this run) reports 96.6% sensitivity and a 160-day mean earlier-detection gain across 22 KNOWN incidents, and states in its own limitations "Fourth, we tested sensitivity, not specificity" — so it bounds nothing about what is missed, and reports no alert rate, no diversion magnitudes and no detection probability as a function of diverted quantity. Meanwhile incident reports describe multi-year diversions found by accident (a DEA probe at one hospital put the missing fentanyl solution at roughly 21 gallons), and the only published statements about how much goes undetected are opinion surveys of executives. Hospitals are legally required to keep these accounts (21 CFR 1304-style records); no detection performance is specified for them and none has been published.
The interface problem is real rather than decorative. Safeguards accountancy assumes bulk material measured with quantified instrument uncertainty; hospital records mix countable units, partially administered doses, and "waste" attested by a human who may be the adversary. The dominant error term is therefore documentation error of unknown distribution under partial adversarial control — which safeguards deliberately removes from the material balance and handles in a separate containment-and-surveillance layer. Transplanting the statistic forces a model (adversarially-controlled attestation terms inside the balance) that neither field has. The size of that term is already known to be large in the target field: Epstein et al. (A&A 2016, opened) measured controlled-substance reconciliation error rates of 15.8% at baseline and 8.8% -> 5.2% after near-real-time reporting, and noted "Most reconciliation errors are clerical."

Presuppositions:
1. [established at many sites, contested in general] Cabinet transaction logs, administration records and waste attestations are retrievable at per-transaction, per-user, per-unit granularity. Supported by Epstein 2016, which captured 145,642 ADC transactions with 24 missing, and by the AJHP 2022 transaction-level pipeline.
2. [contested — principal feasibility risk, partially relieved this run] Quantities are recoverable for at least a few adjudicated cases. Query 8 found public-record magnitudes (DEA probe quantity at Cheshire Medical; DOJ/DEA settlement at Palomar Health), so the comparison limb is not hypothetical, though per-month rate reconstruction from charging documents remains the hard part.
3. [contested] Per-dose administered amounts carry a quantifiable uncertainty distribution; part of the record (witnessed waste) is attestation only and therefore unmeasured by construction, which may force the budget to be stated as a bound rather than a variance.
4. [contested] A sustained-rate estimand is the right one; burst diversion needs a different statistic, and which regime dominates is itself unestablished.
5. [flagged] The safeguards formalism's assumptions (independent errors, known variances) need not hold for the transplant to be informative, provided the result is presented as a conservative bound.
6. [contested — significance limb] Someone would act on a published detection limit: hospital diversion programmes, accreditation bodies, DEA/state boards, or purchasers of analytics.
7. [established] Records are partly under the control of the person the statistic is meant to detect — the structural difference from both nuclear accountancy and retail shrinkage, to be modelled rather than assumed away.
8. [new, flagged] The question presupposes a non-degenerate answer exists. If the attested-waste term dominates, the honest answer is "no finite detection limit at the stated Pfa," which is an answer and a policy-relevant one, not a failure of the question.

What would count as an answer:
A computation on real records from at least two institutions producing (i) a documented uncertainty budget for every term of the inventory difference, separating measured from attested quantities and stating which terms are bounds rather than variances; (ii) an operating-characteristic curve — detection probability against sustained diversion rate at a fixed false-alarm rate per unit-month — for current reconciliation practice, for a vendor alarm rule where it can be reproduced, and for a safeguards-style sequential test; (iii) that curve placed against per-month quantities from adjudicated cases. Negative result admissible and informative: a demonstration that the attested fraction of outflow makes sigma_ID unboundable below some large M.
The two possible answers are sharply different policy conclusions: if the minimum detectable rate exceeds the rate in most adjudicated cases, the accounting system is a formality and detection is in practice down to witnesses and chance; if it does not, detection failures are organizational rather than statistical and the remedy is operational, not analytic.

Significance — consequence:
A quantitative answer forces a binary policy conclusion that is currently undecided by evidence. If the minimum detectable sustained rate for a ward-month exceeds the per-month rate in most adjudicated cases, then the legally mandated accounting is a formality with respect to the threat it is nominally for, and improvements must come from containment-and-surveillance analogues (blinded physical counts at randomised cadence, assayed waste sampling, two-person unobservable-step removal) rather than from more analytics on the same records; procurement claims of "highly effective AI detection" become unsupportable without a stated Pfa. If it does not exceed them, then the recurring multi-year undetected cases are organizational failures — alerts not worked, discrepancies closed without investigation — and the remedy is staffing and workflow, with the detection limit available as an accreditation metric. Either answer redirects spending that currently flows on vendor sensitivity claims alone.
Significance — audience:
(i) Hospital diversion-prevention programmes and pharmacy directors, who must choose between analytics subscriptions and physical-control measures and today have no comparable number for either; (ii) ASHP and the diversion-prevention guideline writers, who could specify a target detection limit rather than a list of practices; (iii) accreditors (Joint Commission) and state boards, who currently audit process compliance with no performance standard — the precedent for such a standard exists in another custody domain (EPA's statistical inventory reconciliation performance requirements for underground storage tanks are written as a minimum detectable rate at specified detection and false-alarm probabilities; seen in search results, not opened, so cited as form not number); (iv) DEA registrants and plaintiffs' counsel, for whom a published detection limit is evidence about what an institution could have known; (v) purchasers and vendors of diversion analytics, who would acquire a comparable figure of merit.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens a design question that cannot currently be posed: what monitoring configuration (count cadence, blinded recount fraction, waste-assay sampling rate, per-user stratification depth) achieves a target detection limit at acceptable false-alarm load — the safeguards "inspection goal" problem, newly askable for pharmacy. Opens the adversarial-attestation statistics problem: how to bound an inventory difference when a term is attested by the adversary, which is a general problem for any audited account with self-reported consumption (laboratory controlled substances, veterinary practice, cannabis seed-to-sale, chemical weapons precursor declarations). Makes the burst-versus-sustained regime question measurable. Invites a waste-assay validation experiment (measure what witnessed waste actually contains) that would convert the largest bound into a variance.
Significance — anchoring:
Anchors to the published anomaly that 5-16% of controlled-substance reconciliations are erroneous (Epstein 2016) while diversion is nonetheless said to be detected by these records; to the gap between vendor sensitivity claims and the AJHP paper's own admission that specificity was untested; to the repeated accidental discovery of multi-year diversions; and to the structural precedent in safeguards that material balance alone was found inadequate at scale, which is a directly transferable anomaly rather than an analogy.

Reason for silence:
Three mechanisms, none of them "nobody thought of it".
(1) Disciplinary boundary with an unusual asymmetry: the people who own the method (safeguards statisticians, metrologists, auditors) have no access to and no reason to read hospital cabinet logs, while the people who own the data (pharmacists, nurses, compliance officers, diversion-programme staff) are not trained in detection-limit or uncertainty-budget formalism. Query 2 and query 10 show the method literature is dense and wholly self-referential; query 1 and query 3 show the data literature speaks only in classifier and compliance vocabulary. Neither side's indexing terms reach the other.
(2) Incentive and liability gap: a computed detection limit is adverse discoverable evidence. For a hospital, publishing that its accounts cannot detect removal at the rate it is being sued over converts an unquantified process defence into a quantified one; for an analytics vendor, an OC curve names what the product misses and would replace "highly effective" marketing with a number. Regulation reinforces this: controlled-substance recordkeeping is mandated in detail while no detection performance is specified for it, so nobody is obliged to compute one and the first mover bears the whole cost.
(3) The absence of a sanctioned object to compute: unlike a leak rate or a MUF, there is no accepted definition of "the diversion rate of a ward-month", because the outflow terms include an attestation that is not a measurement. The formalism needed (a balance with an adversarially-controlled unmeasured term) does not exist off the shelf in either field, so the question is not merely unasked but currently unanswerable with borrowed machinery — which is also why it is a research question rather than a calculation.
