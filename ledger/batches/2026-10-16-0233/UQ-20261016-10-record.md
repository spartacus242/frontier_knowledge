Question (canonical) [R1, adopted at Gate 1]:
For a named pathogen and a fixed calendar window, over the enumerable fleet of nucleic-acid amplification tests holding regulatory authorisation (pre-specified source set: WHO emergency-use listing / prequalification dossiers and public reports, FDA EUA and 510(k) decision summaries, EU IVDR and common-specification filings, and national reference-laboratory protocol registries), with each assay's amplification target localised at the finest resolution the public record supports (exact oligo coordinates where disclosed; else a named gene/ORF interval; else recorded as "unresolved") and weighted by its share of tests actually performed (pre-specified reconstruction hierarchy: national laboratory reporting, procurement and tender awards, manufacturer shipment disclosures, donor-funded volume records):
(a) what is the volume-weighted concentration of target regions across the pathogen genome, reported as a Herfindahl-Hirschman index and its reciprocal (the effective number of independent targets), computed on a pre-specified genomic binning and with the unresolved fraction propagated as interval bounds; and
(b) under a stated site-specific substitution/recombination arrival model fitted to observed genomic diversity in the window, together with a per-chemistry mismatch-to-dropout failure model, what is the probability that a single circulating event degrades at least X% of volume-weighted global capacity, for X on a pre-specified grid (e.g. 10/25/50%)?

Question (V0, intake — superseded):
For a given pathogen's deployed global diagnostic fleet, weighted by test volume, how concentrated are the nucleic-acid amplification targets across the pathogen genome, and what is the probability that a single circulating substitution or recombination event degrades a specified fraction (e.g. at least 25%) of global testing capacity?

Why reformulated (well-posedness only): V0 left four things undetermined that decide the number — which assays constitute "the fleet", at what genomic resolution a "target" is individuated, how volume shares are reconstructed, and the binning on which a concentration index is computed. R1 fixes all four by pre-specification and makes the unresolved proprietary fraction an explicit interval rather than a silent omission. The estimand is unchanged.

Alternative formulations:
- What is the effective number of independent diagnostic targets in worldwide use for this pathogen, and what systemic single-mutation failure risk does that concentration imply?
- Is the world's molecular testing capacity for a pathogen a monoculture, and what is the measurement that would show it?

Motivating gap:
The methodological literature on choosing primer/probe sets and checking them in silico against circulating diversity is dense, and it contains documented cases of single substitutions degrading commercial assays, plus decades-old advice to design to multiple targets. Intake supplied PMC8597629 (single-point N-gene mutations degrading a dual-target SARS-CoV-2 assay; partial ORF1ab failure with Omicron BA.2.12.1) and PMC3859022 ("Towards Multitarget Testing in Molecular Microbiology") as instances. NOTE: this agent could not verify either citation — see "Novelty — sources actually opened". Taken as given, every such statement is scoped to one assay or one panel. The fleet-level quantity is absent: no reported measure of how concentrated targets are across the assays actually in use, and no capacity-weighted probability that one event removes a large share of testing simultaneously. That is the quantity a procurement tender, a prequalification body or a preparedness plan needs, and per-assay multi-target advice cannot supply it: every manufacturer can satisfy "use two targets" while the whole fleet converges on the same two, which is precisely the correlated-failure case.

Presuppositions:
1. Deployed assays' target regions are knowable at useful resolution — CONTESTED. Much primer/probe design is proprietary; achievable resolution may be genomic region rather than exact sequence. Handled, not assumed away: resolution is recorded per assay and the size of the unresolvable fraction is itself a reportable finding.
2. Test volumes by assay are reconstructible well enough to weight the fleet — CONTESTED. Sources (procurement records, national laboratory reporting, manufacturer disclosures, prequalification listings) are each partial; R1 fixes a reconstruction hierarchy and requires coverage to be stated.
3. Site-specific mutation-arrival probabilities can be estimated from observed genomic diversity — PARTLY ESTABLISHED. Selection, recombination and lineage structure make arrival strongly non-uniform, so the estimate is explicitly conditional on a stated evolutionary model rather than on a uniform-rate assumption.
4. Assay failure is approximately a threshold function of mismatch number and position — PARTLY ESTABLISHED. Dropout depends on chemistry and cycling, so a per-assay or per-chemistry failure model is required; no single universal rule is assumed.
5. Diagnostic capacity is substitutable at short notice — CONTESTED, and deliberately placed outside the estimand: it belongs to the consequence argument (how bad a given concentration is), not to the measurement.
6. The question presupposes nothing about the answer's direction: low concentration with negligible systemic risk is an admissible and closing answer.
7. Added at Gate 1: a concentration index on genomic bins is a meaningful summary of correlated-failure exposure. Weakly established — it is a standard move in industrial organisation and portfolio risk, but its validity here depends on the binning choice, which R1 pre-specifies and whose sensitivity must be reported.

What would count as an answer:
A reconstructed fleet table (assay -> target region(s) -> volume share) with explicit coverage, per-assay resolution, and the unresolved proprietary fraction stated; a concentration statistic (HHI and effective number of targets) with uncertainty propagated from the unresolved fraction as bounds rather than a point estimate; and a site-resolved probability that one substitution or recombination event degrades at least X% of capacity under a stated evolutionary model and a stated per-chemistry failure model. Retrospective validation is required as a falsification test: the estimator must assign non-negligible prior probability to the known dropout episodes used as positive controls (S-gene target failure; ORF1ab dropout). Three outcomes all answer and close it: high concentration with a quantified systemic risk; a defensible finding of low concentration; or a demonstration that the proprietary and volume-unresolved fractions are large enough that no bounded estimate is possible, with the bound on the bound stated.

Significance — consequence (argued):
If concentration is high — say an effective number of independent targets between 2 and 4 across a fleet carrying most of global volume — then a specific, currently unpriced failure mode becomes quantified: one arrival event can remove a quarter of testing capacity within the weeks it takes genomes to spread, during precisely the period when testing is load-bearing. That converts target diversity from a design virtue into a procurement and licensing variable: tenders can require that an awarded portfolio span disjoint genomic regions, prequalification can decline to list a product that piles onto an already-saturated region, and preparedness plans can hold reserve assays chosen for genomic distance rather than for brand. If concentration is low, a standing anxiety — audible after S-gene target failure but never measured — is defused, and the existing regime (per-assay in-silico checking plus multi-target design advice) is shown sufficient without portfolio-level intervention; that is a real result, because it stops a plausible but costly regulatory addition. If the proprietary fraction defeats the measurement, the finding is itself actionable: it identifies target-region disclosure (not sequence disclosure) as the specific transparency requirement that would make the system's risk observable, which is a narrower and more defensible ask than general primer disclosure.
The asymmetry that makes this worth measuring rather than guessing: the three outcomes imply three different actions, and no one currently knows which obtains.

Significance — audience (named, with the action each would take):
- WHO prequalification and emergency-use listing, and FIND: a portfolio-level listing criterion; concretely, a target-region field in the dossier and a published fleet concentration statistic per priority pathogen.
- National procurement agencies and global funders of diagnostics (the bodies buying HIV viral load, TB, HCV and respiratory-panel volume): target-diversity requirements or lot-splitting rules in tenders — a change in award criteria, not merely in advice.
- IVD regulators (FDA, EU notified bodies under IVDR): the review unit today is the single assay; a fleet statistic is the input that would justify a horizontal requirement or a post-market diversity surveillance condition.
- National reference laboratories and pandemic-preparedness planners: choose reserve and confirmatory assays by genomic distance from the installed base, and know what fraction of capacity to plan to lose.
- Pathogen-genomics surveillance groups: a prioritised watchlist of genome sites where a substitution has outsized operational consequence — a different ranking from the immunological or clinical watchlists maintained now.

Significance — fecundity (new questions that become askable):
- What is the optimal diversification of a national or global diagnostic portfolio, given target concentration, switching cost and replacement lead time? (A portfolio-optimisation question that cannot be posed without the concentration measurement.)
- Does fleet concentration itself create selection pressure, by making detection ascertainment non-uniform across the genome — can a diagnostic monoculture select for its own blind spot?
- The same statistic for other modalities: epitope concentration across the deployed antigen-test fleet; primer-scheme concentration across the deployed sequencing fleet (where shared amplicon schemes suggest concentration may be far higher than for PCR).
- Cross-pathogen comparison: is concentration a property of the pathogen's genome (conserved-region scarcity) or of the market (copying the first published protocol)? Comparing a pathogen with an abundant conserved genome against one with scarce conserved regions separates the two.
- Does the first-published protocol's target region predict the fleet's eventual concentration — a measurable path-dependence claim about how diagnostic markets form under emergency.

Significance — anchoring (existing problems and anomalies it touches):
Documented dropout episodes (S-gene target failure; ORF1ab dropout) sit in the literature as curiosities and as useful surveillance side-effects, never as realisations of a measured systemic risk — the candidate supplies the frame in which they are data rather than anecdotes, and uses them as positive controls. It touches the preparedness literature's open problem of diagnostic surge capacity, which counts tests and instruments but not target architecture. It touches the per-assay/system-level mismatch in IVD regulation, where approval is granted one product at a time and no body holds the aggregate. And it imports a mature measurement apparatus (concentration indices, correlated-failure modelling) into biosecurity, where the analogous imports for crop monoculture and for financial systemic risk both produced policy instruments.

Reason for silence:
Primarily a disciplinary boundary with no owner for the measurement. The quantity is an industrial-organisation statistic (concentration of a shared input across a supplier population) computed on a molecular-biology object (genome coordinates); the people fluent in the first do not hold assay target data, and the people fluent in the second work at the unit of one assay. Reinforced by an institutional fact that makes the gap structural rather than accidental: the review and publication unit is the assay. A regulator evaluates one submission, so a fleet-level statistic can never appear in any single review, however many reviews occur; a manufacturer knows its own assay and not its competitors'; an academic in-silico-evaluation group has access to published protocols and so publishes per-assay or per-panel analyses. The aggregate is nobody's deliverable.
Secondarily an infrastructure and access mechanism: assembling the measurement requires regulatory summaries across jurisdictions, published in-silico evaluations, procurement and volume records, and reverse engineering where sequences are proprietary — a multi-month data-assembly task with no funder whose remit it matches, and partly obstructed by trade-secret protection of exactly the field (oligo coordinates) the statistic needs.
Thirdly, a weak incentive gap: a high-concentration finding implicates named marketed products, and the parties holding the volume data are the parties the finding exposes. This is the weakest of the three and should not be leaned on.
This is a mechanism, not "no one thought of it". But a credible reason for silence does not substitute for the novelty search that was refused: if a rerun finds the question asked, the mechanism was simply wrong.
