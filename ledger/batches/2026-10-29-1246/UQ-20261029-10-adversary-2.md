## Restatement (my own words, before reading the record's framing back)

Managed-language runtimes (JVM, V8, PyPy, LuaJIT, HHVM) recompile code *while the program runs*, using profiles the running program itself produces. Barrett et al. (2017) showed that a large share of (VM, benchmark) pairs never settle to a flat per-iteration time, and left the cause open. The candidate asks: **is that failure to settle produced by the recompile-from-live-profile loop itself?** The proposed way to find out is to run the same program on the same VM three ways on one machine — (A) loop live; (B) the compiled-code plan copied from an earlier pilot run and frozen from the first iteration with adaptive recompilation off; (C) the same plan frozen only after the point where the pilot stopped compiling, as a placebo for "freezing per se" — classify each process execution convergent/non-convergent with a rule fixed in advance, and report **how much lower the non-convergent share is in B than in A**, with C showing that the drop is not an artefact of pinning.

In one line: *does switching off the compiler's own feedback make an unsettled program settle, and by how much, against a placebo?*

## The one source my attacks rest on, with its REGIME and its printed numbers

**Antoch, Binder, Bulej, Farquet, Horký, Prokopec, Rosà, Tůma — "Experimental Evaluation Methodology for the Era of No Steady Performance", PACMPL 10(OOPSLA1), art. 128, April 2026, DOI 10.1145/3798236.** READ LEVEL: **full text via WebFetch's summariser, two passes**; the second pass served §3/§8 and Table 1 as short ordered consecutive segments.

**REGIME:** population = Renaissance benchmark suite workloads on **Oracle Java 11**; unit of analysis = one *run* of a benchmark (and, within a run, one repetition); operating condition = long runs, GC forced between repetitions, live JIT compilation throughout, one machine class; instrument = counts of dynamic-compilation events and of GC events per repetition, plus their own changepoint-style classification with "categories from [3]" (Barrett's classes); threshold = that classifier; category = steady / not-steady share of runs, and confidence-interval coverage.

**Reconstruction from 7 consecutive summariser segments** (labelled as a reconstruction; verified by the grammatical junctions and by the parenthesis closing at segment 5 — I did not complete any cut string by inference):

> "We check whether the change is associated with the usual culprits such as dynamic compilation (we record the count of compilation events and the processor time consumed by dynamic compilation during each repetition) or garbage collection in response to memory shortage (we record the count of garbage collection events during each repetition and force garbage collection between repetitions). No extra dynamic compilation event and no extra garbage collection cycle occurs regularly around the time of the change." — §3

**Opening words, and what they scope:** "We check whether **the change** is associated with…" — the object is *the sudden performance change* they study inside long runs on Renaissance/Java 11, not warmup at large and not Barrett's non-warmup classes. The clause **denies** a regular association between compilation events and the change. It is an **event-count correlation**, not an intervention.

**And the formulation the same paper hedged, which I am required to quote in preference to the bare one** — from §8, Threats to Validity (reconstruction from 6 consecutive segments):

> "We note, however, that even dynamic compilation may cause performance changes after long periods of repetitive execution, due to high invocation thresholds used to trigger compilation, or even indefinitely repeating performance changes, for example in applications that generate code, or due to artifacts such as deopt loops."

**Opening words:** "We note, **however**," — this explicitly walks back the §3 negative and names *deopt loops* as a mechanism that can drive performance changes **indefinitely**. The clause **asserts** that the candidate's hypothesised mechanism is live. So this paper cuts **both** ways, and I book it both ways.

**Does it publish the candidate's quantity? NO — and I name what it does print instead.** Table 1 caption, verbatim: "Workload classification with categories from [3]. The Repetitions columns give the average repetitions executed per run and the average repetition where steady state was reached. The (Not) Steady columns give the share of runs classified into their respective categories." Headers: `Benchmark | Runs | Total | Steady | Flat | W-Up | S-Dn | Stdy | Overall`.

- **Numerator class:** runs of a Renaissance workload on Oracle Java 11 falling in a Barrett-derived class. **Denominator:** *runs*, per benchmark — not process executions pooled across VMs, and not arm-stratified.
- **Overlap, both directions.** What Antoch's class **misses** that the candidate includes: every non-Java VM in Barrett's matrix (PyPy, V8, LuaJIT, HHVM, JRuby+Truffle, Graal), every Barrett microbenchmark, and — decisively — **any frozen-plan arm at all**. What it **includes** that the candidate excludes: Renaissance workloads, which are not in Barrett's published matrix and so are outside the candidate's generating rule.
- My first pass's summariser reported the `Stdy` column as "appears to be the not-steady share, though the header is ambiguous", with values 0–18%. The second pass's caption resolves the grouping ("Steady | Not" group headers) but I still cannot certify which leaf column is which. **I therefore cite no number from Table 1 and ground nothing on it.** That is a fact about my read, not about the paper.

So Antoch et al. **ANNOUNCE and TEST** the candidate's causal hypothesis observationally, and **PRINT** a single-arm share on a different denominator; they do not **MEASURE** the candidate's between-arm difference. A citation to an event-count correlation is not a between-arm contrast. **No Low novelty grade is available on this paper.**
