Question (canonical) — V2, the one Gate 1 reformulation, taken as canonical:
In hospital systems where infusate history can be attributed to a SPECIFIC peripheral intravenous catheter (line-level medication-administration documentation, or smart-pump-to-line association), fitting a cause-specific nested frailty survival model for catheter failure with random effects for patient, inserter, ward and device lot and a per-admixture infusate term: (a) on the log-hazard scale, what is the variance partition coefficient of the infusate component for each failure mode (phlebitis, infiltration/extravasation, occlusion, dislodgement, infection), with an interval — i.e. a declared DESCRIPTIVE variance decomposition, with any causal reading of "attributable" demoted to a secondary analysis under a declared identification strategy for confounding by acuity; and (b) does the incumbent binary access rule (osmolarity above about 600 mOsm/L, or pH outside roughly 5–9, implying central access) discriminate and calibrate against (i) the fitted infusate-attributable hazard and (ii) extravasation injury as a co-primary endpoint, as well as an index estimated from the same data under internal–external cross-validation across systems?

Question — V1 (intake wording, recorded per Gate 1):
Of the variance in peripheral intravenous catheter failure, what share is attributable to the specific drugs and fluids actually infused through each catheter — reconstructable per catheter from linked medication-administration, smart-pump and vascular-access records — and do the pH and osmolarity thresholds that currently decide peripheral versus central access discriminate that failure risk as well as an index derived empirically from the same data?

Reason for the reformulation (well-posedness only): V1 left three things unpinned that decide whether any evidence could settle it — the scale and estimator of "share of the variance" (undefined for a time-to-event outcome), the conflation of "attributable" (causal) with "variance share" (descriptive), and which metrics the threshold rule is to be judged on. V2 fixes the scale (log hazard, cause-specific nested frailty, VPC with interval), separates the descriptive primary from the causal secondary, and names discrimination plus calibration against two endpoints. No substantive limb was added or removed.

Alternative formulations:
1. Is the peripheral-versus-central access decision rule calibrated against the catheter failures it exists to prevent?
2. How much of peripheral IV failure is caused by what was put through the line rather than by the line, the patient, the inserter or the ward?

Motivating gap:
The silence is in the shape of the literature. Peripheral IV cannulation is the commonest invasive procedure in hospital care and failure before completion of therapy runs at roughly a third to a half of catheters. The field is dense with "how" — securement, dressings, flushing, clinically-indicated versus routine replacement, insertion technique, vessel-health-and-preservation frameworks — and close to silent on what the EXPOSURE is. Meanwhile the rule deciding whether a drug may enter a peripheral vein at all is a pair of fixed numeric thresholds on the infusate, reproduced across infusion-therapy standards without field calibration against failure. Gate 2 confirmed the gap from inside the field's own pharmacy half: Manrique-Rodríguez et al. (Drugs in R&D 2021, read in full) apply exactly these thresholds as a three-tier risk framework over 307 admixtures, place 40% (123/307) in the high-risk tier, and state in their own words that "human tolerance of pH and osmolarity has not been well studied", that "most of the recommendations on this topic are based on a low level of evidence", and that the osmolarity threshold "above which a drug is not considered optimal for peripheral infusion ... [is] not well defined". They measure the exposure half carefully and never touch the outcome half.
The intake's declared near-neighbour (the "factors associated with phlebitis in peripheral venous catheters" genre, which carries "irritant drug" as one covariate among many) was re-tested at Gate 2 queries 1, 6 and 9 and still estimates no attributable share and no rule calibration. A stronger neighbour than the declared one was found and is recorded below (Kia et al. 2023).

Presuppositions (status after Gate 1/2):
1. Infusate history is attributable to a SPECIFIC catheter — CONTESTED, and the decisive feasibility presupposition. Most systems record administration to a patient, not to a line. Gate 2 sharpened this rather than resolving it: Kia et al. 2023 work from smart-pump event logs that carry drug, concentration, rate and care unit but no catheter identity, which is the regime the question has to escape. Sites with line-level documentation, or a validated pump-to-line association, must be identified before any data pull.
2. Failure modes are recorded and distinguishable — CONTESTED. Phlebitis scales are subjective and failure is often documented only as removal with no reason. Outcome measurement error will be absorbed into the residual of any variance partition, which biases the infusate VPC downward; this is a well-posedness cost, not only a feasibility one, and is why well-posedness is scored 2/3.
3. pH and osmolarity are retrievable per product administered — ESTABLISHED, and now evidenced: Manrique-Rodríguez et al. publish measured pH and osmolarity for 307 admixtures (106 drugs, 183 concentrations) and show the values move with diluent and final concentration, so the values must come from the administration record, not the monograph.
4. The incumbent thresholds are intended as predictions of catheter FAILURE rather than engineering conventions encoding extravasation-injury SEVERITY — CONTESTED, and the strongest reframing available to a critic. Partly corroborated against it: Manrique-Rodríguez et al. tier on pH, osmolarity AND vesicant status together and note pH "is not considered a restrictive factor on its own", which is the severity reading. The design therefore carries extravasation injury as a co-primary endpoint so the rule is judged against what it may actually be for.
5. Device lot and inserter identity are recoverable as variance components — CONTESTED for lot.
6. Catheter failure is a patient-relevant endpoint — ESTABLISHED (re-cannulation, interrupted therapy).
7. Infusate assignment is not wholly confounded by patient acuity — CONTESTED; sicker patients get both the irritant drugs and the fragile veins. Handled in V2 by making the primary estimand descriptive and requiring a declared identification strategy for any causal reading.

What would count as an answer:
A pre-registered analysis in at least two hospital systems with line-level infusate attribution, fitting a cause-specific hierarchical survival model for catheter failure with random effects for patient, inserter, ward and device lot and a per-admixture infusate term, reporting: (a) the variance partition on the log-hazard scale, with the infusate share and its interval, by failure mode; (b) discrimination and calibration of the incumbent pH/osmolarity threshold rule against the infusate-attributable component and, separately, against extravasation injury; (c) an empirically derived per-admixture peripheral-vein-burden index with internal–external cross-validation across systems; and (d) the number and direction of access decisions that would change under that index. A small infusate share, or a demonstration that the incumbent thresholds already capture what there is to capture, answers the question negatively — and that negative is the more useful result for the standards bodies, because it would retire a recurring criticism of the rule.

Significance — consequence:
If the infusate share is substantial AND the incumbent thresholds discriminate it poorly, the access rule is miscalibrated in a measurable direction and the deliverable is a replacement index with a counted number of decisions that would flip — in both directions. Drugs now barred from peripheral veins that carry little measured failure burden would stop generating central lines, each of which carries its own thrombosis and bloodstream-infection hazard; drugs now passed as peripherally acceptable that carry a high burden would be flagged. The quantity that makes this consequential is already published: 40% of 307 standard adult admixtures fall in the high-risk tier under the current rule (P3), so the rule is binding on a large fraction of hospital infusions, and a recalibration moves a lot of traffic. If the infusate share is small, or the thresholds already capture what there is to capture, the answer retires a recurring criticism of the rule and redirects the field's effort to the patient, inserter and ward components — and the negative is cheap to act on, because it tells standards panels to stop revisiting the numbers. P1's directional hint (medication type below rate and care unit, for pump occlusion) makes the negative a live possibility rather than a rhetorical hedge, which is what keeps this a question rather than an agenda.

Reason for silence:
Primary mechanism is a data-architecture boundary, and it is demonstrable rather than asserted. The exposure variable the question needs — what went through THIS catheter — does not exist in most hospital records, because medication administration is documented to the patient, not to the line. The systems that do hold drug, concentration and rate at high resolution are smart pumps, owned by biomedical engineering and vendors and not joined to vascular-access records; P1 is precisely that regime, a careful analysis of pump logs with no catheter identity, and it is the shape the literature takes when the join is missing.
Second mechanism is a disciplinary boundary that runs straight through the question: the exposure half belongs to hospital pharmacy (compounding, pH, osmolarity, vesicant lists) and the outcome half to nursing and vascular-access research (phlebitis scales, securement, replacement trials), and the documents that join them are consensus standards written by panels rather than analyses run on data — P3 is the pharmacy half done well, the phlebitis-risk-factor genre is the nursing half done well, and the threshold rule sits in the gap with a Delphi behind it.
Third is an incentive gap: nobody owns the question. A negative result retires a rule and sells nothing; the one party with a commercial interest in the rule (device vendors publishing infusate decision aids) benefits from the rule as it stands, since it routes patients to central devices.
The method is not the obstacle and cannot be offered as the reason: query 8 found nested/shared frailty models and variance partition coefficients to be mature, named, off-the-shelf machinery in survival biostatistics, simply never pointed at this object.
