Question (canonical, Gate 1 reformulation — this is the version to carry forward):
In a cohort of deceased-donor kidney (or liver) grafts with retrievable, time-resolved container or perfusate temperature records spanning cross-clamp to reperfusion, linked to registry outcomes: does adding a pre-specified kinetic thermal-dose exposure — an Arrhenius integral D = integral exp(-Ea/R*T(t)) dt over the storage interval (equivalently, duration combined with the ICH-Q1A mean kinetic temperature of that interval), with a pre-specified explicit sub-zero injury penalty term — to a risk model that already contains cold ischemia time improve discrimination and calibration for delayed graft function and 1-year graft function in a nested-model comparison; and does the cold ischemia time coefficient attenuate when the dose is entered (the proxy/mediation limb)?

Question (Gate 0 intake version, recorded as required):
Does a kinetic thermal-dose exposure computed from logged organ-transport temperature histories — the mean-kinetic-temperature construct that pharmaceutical stability science (ICH Q1A) uses in place of elapsed time — predict graft outcome better than cold ischemia time, and is part of cold ischemia time's apparent effect a proxy for uncontrolled temperature excursions?

Reason for the one permitted Gate 1 reformulation (well-posedness only): the intake version was not answerable as written on three counts. (i) Mean kinetic temperature is a *temperature*, not a dose — it is a weighted average that discards duration, so "MKT in place of elapsed time" is a category error; the exposure has to be the time integral of the rate factor (or MKT paired with duration). (ii) "Predict better" was unoperationalised; the reformulation fixes nested-model discrimination and calibration against a model containing CIT. (iii) Ea and the sub-zero penalty must be fixed in advance, or the comparison is a search over a model family rather than a test. No scope, claim or domain was changed.

Alternative formulations:
- Is preservation injury a function of elapsed time or of an Arrhenius-weighted temperature-time dose, and does organ-acceptance and risk-adjustment practice therefore use the wrong exposure variable?
- Given time-resolved temperature records from transport containers, how much of the between-graft variation currently attributed to cold ischemia time is variation in storage temperature history instead?

Motivating gap:
Cold ischemia time is a time-only exposure. It is the standard preservation covariate in transplant risk models and a central input to organ-acceptance and logistics decisions, and it presupposes storage at an effectively fixed temperature. Instrumented studies contradict that presupposition in both directions. Villa et al. 2006 (Transplantation 81:483-486, verified below) put thermistors in the parenchyma of six human liver allografts and found graft temperature settling near 0 C rather than the assumed 4 C. Lima et al. 2023 (Braz J Transplant 26:e0423, verified below) took infrared readings at four points in 39 deceased-donor kidney transplants and found only ~64% of back-table readings inside 0-4 C, most transport-box readings *below* 0 C, and no reading inside 0-4 C at the start of warm ischemia or before reperfusion. Pharmaceutical stability science abandoned time-only accounting decades ago because degradation rate is exponential in temperature, and encodes that in a regulatory guideline (ICH Q1A mean kinetic temperature). Transplantation measures the temperature for quality assurance and keeps the time-only exposure for outcome modelling.

The interface is a real problem rather than a nominal one, and that is part of the question: preservation injury is not one first-order reaction; sub-zero excursions cause ice/chill injury exactly where Arrhenius weighting predicts the least damage (Villa's near-0 C finding and Lima's sub-zero transport readings make this concrete, not hypothetical); warm excursions interact with reperfusion signalling rather than simply accumulating; and a logger reads the container, not the parenchyma.

Presuppositions (status after Gate 1):
1. Time-resolved transport temperature records exist, are retrievable at scale, and are linkable to registry outcomes — CONTESTED, and the binding constraint. Logging is a QA practice of particular centres, couriers and container/perfusion-device vendors; it is not a registry field. Perfusion-machine logs (e.g. LifePort, 10 s sampling, as Adani 2020 confirms) are the most plausible existing source at scale, which biases any first cohort toward machine-perfused organs — i.e. toward the arm where temperature is already controlled, the opposite of where the excursions are. Feasibility is the first thing a design must establish.
2. Container or perfusate temperature approximates parenchymal temperature with a characterisable lag — CONTESTED, and Villa 2006 is the evidence that the gap is not negligible: measured parenchymal temperature sat near 0 C under protocols nominally delivering 4 C. Without a lag model the exposure is measured with structured, not classical, error, which biases toward the null in a way that makes a null result weak.
3. Graft outcome is sensitive to preservation at the margin after case-mix adjustment — ESTABLISHED for CIT in registry analyses, though effect sizes are heterogeneous across registries and eras (itself a datum the candidate explains if temperature variance is doing part of the work).
4. An Arrhenius / mean-kinetic-temperature functional form applies across the 0-10 C band for a multicellular organ — CONTESTED. The model almost certainly needs an explicit sub-zero penalty and may fail; failure is informative, but see presupposition 6.
5. Temperature history is not merely a marker of logistics quality (courier handling, transfer delay, box-packing discipline, centre throughput) which independently predicts outcome — CONTESTED, and the main confounding threat. Needs instruments or design control; without them the mediation limb is uninterpretable even if the prediction limb is positive.
6. NEW, added at Gate 1: Ea (or any equivalent temperature-sensitivity parameter) is identifiable in observational data. Over a 0-10 C band the Arrhenius factor varies only a few-fold, dose is near-collinear with duration, and ICH's convention fixes the activation enthalpy at 83.144 kJ/mol by fiat for exactly this reason. The test is therefore of a *pre-specified* dose, not an estimated one; a null for one choice of Ea and penalty form is not a null for the family. This is the strongest well-posedness deduction.
7. Cold ischemia time is measured accurately enough to serve as comparator — PARTLY ESTABLISHED; clamp and reperfusion times are registry fields with known imprecision, and a differential-measurement-error critique applies symmetrically (a hand-entered CIT versus a machine-logged dose).
8. REVISED from intake: "cold ischemia time is an allocation variable" is OVERSTATED. CIT is not an explicit term in the major allocation algorithms; what is true, and sufficient for the question, is that CIT drives offer-acceptance and logistics decisions, that allocation geometry (circles, proximity) is designed around it, and that it is a standard covariate in risk models and trial adjustment. Significance should rest on acceptance/risk-adjustment, not on an allocation-formula claim.

What would count as an answer:
A cohort of grafts with retrievable time-resolved temperature records linked to registry outcomes (delayed graft function; 1-year eGFR or graft survival), with a pre-registered analysis that (a) fixes Ea, the sub-zero penalty form and the container-to-parenchyma lag model in advance, (b) enters the kinetic dose alongside CIT in a nested model and reports discrimination and calibration gain with confidence intervals, (c) reports the change in the CIT coefficient on adding the dose (the mediation limb) under a stated confounding structure, and (d) reports sensitivity across a pre-specified grid of lag and Ea assumptions. A null — no discrimination gain, unchanged CIT coefficient, stable across the sensitivity grid — answers the question as decisively as a positive and justifies keeping the time-only exposure. A result that is positive only for some points on the grid is a non-answer and must be reported as such.

Significance — consequence:
If yes (dose adds discrimination and the CIT coefficient attenuates): the field's standard preservation exposure is partly a proxy, and three things follow concretely. Temperature logging and transmission become worth mandating, because a logged dose would be a better acceptance and risk-adjustment variable than a clock reading — and unlike CIT it is modifiable by better containers rather than only by faster couriers. Historical CIT coefficients become partly uninterpretable, because they absorb era- and route-specific thermal variance, which would explain some of the known heterogeneity in CIT effect sizes across registries. Trials and risk scores adjusting for CIT alone are residually confounded by thermal history. If no (no gain, stable coefficient, robust across the sensitivity grid): that is a positive result for current practice — it says preservation injury in the realised range of temperature histories is adequately captured by duration, that logging need not become a registry field, and that the pharmaceutical analogy genuinely fails for a multicellular organ. Either way the decision "should temperature history be a reportable exposure?" gets evidence instead of inference from a different industry.

Significance — audience:
Would act: (a) OPOs, couriers and container/perfusion-device vendors — a positive result turns logging from optional QA into a reportable exposure, and gives vendors an outcome-linked endpoint rather than a compliance band; (b) registry custodians (SRTR/UNOS, Eurotransplant, NHSBT) — the decision is whether to add a temperature-history field, which is a schema change they make rarely and on evidence; (c) transplant epidemiologists and trialists who adjust for CIT as a covariate, and the builders of DGF and graft-survival risk models; (d) the preservation-temperature research community currently running setpoint trials, for whom a dose metric is the natural secondary analysis of their own logs. Not claimed: that allocation algorithms would change, since CIT is not an explicit allocation term (presupposition 8). The audience claim is narrower than intake's and better supported for it.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: does machine perfusion's outcome benefit partly reduce to thermal stabilisation rather than to perfusion per se — testable by comparing dose between SCS and HMP arms of existing trials. What is the shape of the injury-versus-temperature curve below 4 C, where Villa's near-0 C finding puts most real organs, and is there a sub-zero penalty term with an estimable threshold. Is the optimal setpoint organ-specific because the dose-response differs, which would reframe the 10 C trials as estimating one point on a curve. Does a dose metric reorder the historical literature on "safe" CIT thresholds. And methodologically: can a pre-specified-Ea dose be used as an exposure anywhere else in medicine where a nominally fixed-temperature storage step precedes a measured outcome (tissue banking, gametes, cell therapy products, blood components).

Significance — anchoring:
Touches: the open optimal-preservation-temperature problem (4 C versus ~10 C, in trial now); the long-standing heterogeneity of CIT effect estimates across registries and eras; the DGF prediction problem, where models plateau on registry fields; the mechanistic dispute over whether cold injury is rate-suppressed metabolism, chill injury, or reperfusion-coupled signalling, which the functional-form limb bears on directly; and, on the pharmaceutical side, the existing published critique of MKT's own validity — if MKT is contested in the field that invented it, importing it demands the explicit functional-form test the candidate builds in.

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary-boundary plus data-layer mechanism, with an incentive gap on top, and it is specific rather than generic. The three competences sit in three places that do not share an artifact. (1) Temperature in organ transport is owned by quality assurance and logistics — OPO operations staff, couriers, container and perfusion-device vendors. Their output is a compliance judgement ("was the box in band?"), and their data stay in device memory or a QA file. Adani 2020 is the proof: 10 s temperature telemetry existed in the study's own machines and never reached the study's own regression. (2) Outcome modelling is owned by transplant epidemiologists who work from registry extracts and can only use variables that exist in the schema. Temperature history is not a registry field, so for that community it is not an available exposure — the limiting factor is a schema, not an idea, and nobody writes a paper about a variable they cannot pull. (3) The kinetic-dose construct is owned by regulatory pharmaceutical stability science and chemical kinetics, whose practitioners do not read transplant outcome literature; MKT reaches clinicians only as a cold-chain compliance rule for drugs and vaccines, not as a modelling tool. On top of these, a mild incentive gap: logged temperature histories document protocol excursions, which makes pooling them into a registry liability-adjacent for centres and couriers — a reason to keep the data local that does not apply to clock times, which are already reported. The bridge the candidate needs is cheap (one existing formula, one existing data stream) but it crosses two boundaries at once, which is the characteristic signature of a question that stays unasked while both halves are routine.
