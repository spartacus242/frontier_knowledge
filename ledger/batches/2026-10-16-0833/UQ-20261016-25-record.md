Domain(s): Medicine — clinical pharmacology, pharmacoepidemiology, drug regulation (adjacent: oncology pharmacy, health economics, operations research, veterinary medicine)

Question (canonical, Gate 0 as intaken):
Because a molecule's marketed tablet strengths define a lattice of achievable daily doses, what fraction of the population-level variation in prescribed dose is imposed by that lattice rather than by patient characteristics, and when an individual strength is discontinued, does the resulting shift in the dose distribution produce measurable changes in clinical outcomes or adverse events?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — the one permitted reformulation, for well-posedness only; both versions retained):
For a pre-specified set of chronically dosed oral drugs, define the *achievable lattice* empirically as the set of daily doses reachable from marketed strengths including scored-tablet halves and multi-tablet regimens observed at ≥1% frequency in dispensing data, and define each patient's *unconstrained target dose* under a pre-registered, drug-specific target-dose model (guideline/label titration target, weight- or eGFR-adjusted where the label specifies it, carried as an interval where the guideline gives one):
(a) what share of the variance of observed dispensed daily dose is attributable to rounding of the unconstrained target onto the achievable lattice, as against variance in the unconstrained target itself, and what share of patients have an unconstrained target (or target interval) containing no achievable lattice point;
(b) for discontinuations of individual strengths (not whole-drug shortages) that survive a pre-specified exogeneity screen, what is the interrupted-time-series / difference-in-differences effect on (i) the dispensed-dose distribution and (ii) pre-registered clinical and adverse-event endpoints, judged against an equivalence margin fixed in advance?
Why reformulated: the intake's "fraction of variation imposed by the lattice" names no counterfactual and so no estimand; the reformulation fixes the counterfactual (an explicit target-dose model), fixes the lattice as empirical rather than catalogue-derived, and makes the null interpretable. It does not broaden or narrow scope.

Alternative formulations:
- Is the dose a patient takes chosen by the prescriber or by the manufacturer's catalogue of strengths, and what happens to patients when the catalogue changes?
- Treating strength discontinuations as natural experiments on dose, how much do a drug's available strengths matter to outcomes?
- (third, added at Gate 1) Is the marketed strength ladder a clinically consequential design object, or is it slack that prescribing absorbs without outcome cost?

Motivating gap (as intaken, source method: silence mapping):
Guidance on *how* to dose is dense and specific (titration steps, target doses, renal adjustment) while the question of *why the choosable strengths are what they are* — a commercial and manufacturing artefact — appeared to have no literature and no outcome review. Strength-level discontinuations are recorded in national marketing-status registers and look like a near-ideal natural experiment on dose, yet appeared unused as exposure events. Adjacent literatures that do exist (oncology dose banding/dose rounding, pill splitting, bunching at kinks and notches in economics) all stop short of the population-dose question for chronic oral therapy. Novelty was flagged UNVERIFIED at intake.
Gate 2 finding on the gap: **the gap as stated does not hold.** The "restrict the strength ladder and observe the effects" question was published in the candidate's own field in 1996, and strength-level delisting has twice been used as an ITS exposure event in pharmacoepidemiology (both in opioids, Ontario). What survives of the gap is narrower and quantitative: no published variance decomposition of dispensed dose into lattice-rounding and target-dose components, and no published figure for the share of patients whose target dose is unreachable on the achievable lattice, for any drug or national dataset I could find.

Presuppositions (status after Gate 1):
1. Dispensed daily doses are retrievable at scale, and the marketed-strength list with marketing-status dates is retrievable. CONTESTED as to completeness — strength-level histories exist in national registers (FDA marketing-status/NDC files, EMA Article 57) but coverage and date accuracy must be validated before use. Unchanged by Gate 2.
2. CONTESTED AND LOAD-BEARING: strength discontinuations occur often enough, and for reasons unrelated to effectiveness or safety, to serve as instruments. Demand-driven withdrawal confounds with patient mix; safety-driven withdrawal invalidates the instrument. A pre-specified exogeneity screen and a count of surviving events are required. **Gate 2 adds a second failure mode the intake missed:** the two executed examples of this design are *payer delistings chosen precisely because high doses were thought harmful* (Ontario high-strength opioids), i.e. the real-world supply of strength-removal events is enriched for exactly the endogeneity that invalidates the design. The screen must therefore be shown to leave a non-trivial sample, not merely specified.
3. CONTESTED: prescribers round to the nearest reachable dose rather than splitting or combining tablets. Partially relaxed by defining the lattice empirically (scored tablets, observed multi-tablet regimens). Retained as contested.
4. Dose has a non-flat exposure–response relationship over the perturbed range for at least some drugs studied. If flat, the outcome limb is null by construction — still an answer, hence the pre-stated equivalence margin.
5. Outcome and adverse-event endpoints are ascertainable in the same data as the dispensing records (claims/EHR linkage).
6. NEW, introduced by the Gate 1 reformulation and load-bearing: an *unconstrained target dose* is well defined per patient. For many chronic oral drugs the guideline target is an interval or is defined by titration-to-response rather than by a computable number, so the decomposition in limb (a) is identified only relative to a chosen target-dose model and is not unique. This is the reformulation's cost and the main well-posedness deduction.
Not presupposed: that the lattice is badly chosen, or that regulators should mandate strengths.

What would count as an answer:
Two parts, both pre-registered. (i) A variance decomposition of dispensed daily dose into lattice-rounding and target-dose components for a pre-specified drug set under a stated target-dose model, with sensitivity across alternative target-dose models, plus the share of patients whose target is unreachable on the empirically defined achievable lattice. (ii) For strength-discontinuation events passing the exogeneity screen, an ITS or DiD estimate of the shift in the dose distribution and of pre-registered clinical and adverse-event endpoints, against an equivalence margin fixed in advance, so a null is interpretable rather than merely unpowered. An answer must either show outcome movement attributable to the lattice or bound it below the margin; a heaping plot showing doses piled at marketed strengths is a descriptive premise, not an answer.

Significance — consequence:
If a large share of dose variance is lattice-imposed *and* outcomes move at strength-discontinuation events, the strength ladder becomes a regulated object: discontinuation notification requirements (which in the US already exist for some products under FDASIA-era reporting) would acquire a clinical rationale, and approval or formulary listing could be conditioned on ladder density. If the variance share is large but the equivalence-bounded outcome effect is null, the finding is that prescribing absorbs lattice coarseness without clinical cost — which retires a live worry in paediatric and geriatric dosing and positively justifies manufacturer discretion over strengths. Both branches are decision-relevant. The honest discount: because P1–P3 already show that removing strengths shifts dose, the marginal decision change comes from the outcome and variance magnitudes, not from the existence of the effect.
Significance — audience:
Regulators who already hold the strength-level marketing-status data (FDA CDER marketing-status and shortage staff; EMA Article 57 holders) and who currently treat strength discontinuation as a supply-logistics event rather than a clinical exposure; P&T and formulary committees, who make exactly the P2/P3 kind of delisting decision and today justify it on cost and misuse grounds without a dose-granularity evidence base; generic manufacturers choosing which strengths to carry; guideline writers who set titration steps against a ladder they do not control; and paediatric/renal dosing specialists, for whom unreachable target doses are a daily problem. These are real committees making repeated decisions, so the audience limb is not inflated — but P2/P3 show they already act in this space, which is evidence both that they would act and that the question is not new to them.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens: optimal strength-ladder design as a granularity/lot-sizing problem; whether a scored tablet substitutes for a strength (empirically testable against the lattice definition); whether minimum ladder density should be an approval condition for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs; how the lattice interacts with weight-band and eGFR-band dosing; and whether paediatric formulation incentives should be framed as ladder density rather than as age-appropriate form.
Significance — anchoring:
Touches drug-shortage epidemiology, the dose-banding/dose-rounding debate in oncology pharmacy, pill-splitting safety, narrow-therapeutic-index bioequivalence policy (the EMA levothyroxine strength-specific BE guidance found at query 8 shows regulators already reason per-strength), and the paediatric age-appropriate-formulation problem.

Reason for silence:
Candidate mechanism — **disciplinary boundary plus an object with no owner**: the strength ladder is set by manufacturing and commercial decisions, so clinical pharmacology owns exposure–response but not the ladder, health economics owns price but not granularity, and regulatory science owns bioequivalence *per strength* rather than the ladder as a designed whole. A secondary, weaker mechanism is data: machine-readable strength-level marketing-status histories are recent. **However, Gate 2 undercuts the silence premise itself.** Ain 1996 and the two Ontario studies show the question was asked and the design executed, so what needs explaining is not silence but low citation density and failure to generalise across drugs — a much weaker claim, and one the ledger's own rule treats as grounds for suspecting the novelty finding. Here the novelty finding is already Low, so the two are consistent: the correct reading is that there was no silence to map, only a thin and drug-specific literature.
