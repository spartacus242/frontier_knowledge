## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Take biology papers whose code and data are archived well enough that you can re-run them and get their published numbers back. Now deliberately break one thing in the pipeline — an off-by-one coordinate, a flipped strand, a join that silently duplicates rows, a wrong unit, a wrong contrast — choosing the breakages from a pre-registered list weighted by how often such bugs really occur (bug-fix commits, corrections, issue trackers). Re-run. Two things can happen that matter together: the run finishes and nothing the pipeline itself prints (no crash, no failed threshold, no obviously wrong QC plot, as judged by blinded raters) betrays the fault; and the paper's headline number moves past the paper's own line (sign flip, crossing its own significance threshold, top-k reshuffle, interval no longer covering the original estimate). The question asks for the probability of that conjunction per injected fault, whether it is driven more by the kind of analysis than by the authors' software hygiene, and whether two simultaneous faults stay bounded by the one-fault estimate (the coupling effect). In one line: what is the kill rate of biology's accidental test suite — its own QC output — when the thing being scored is the paper's conclusion rather than the code?

### Pages opened (4 fetches)

1. **https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27100** — the record's COULD NOT READ item, **now READ at abstract depth** (the record's failure was the `/pdf/` route; `/abs/` answered on the first call). Four attributes: UNIT = ML research *repositories*, 39 frozen cases (not papers, not tools). INJECTED OBJECT = four reproducibility-relevant mutation classes — random seed, dependency pin, data split, cross-validation fold count. TEST SUITE / COMPARATOR = **each repository's own existing validation workflow**; a mutation is "detected" only if that workflow catches it, and the abstract states that "Existing repository validation workflows may execute successfully without detecting changes to such choices." ENDPOINT/ESTIMAND = detection proportion by that existing workflow. Numbers: 13 of 39 cases yielded primary outcomes, 24 after a bounded restoration procedure, 1 confirmed-equivalent mutation excluded → 23 confirmed non-equivalent, of which **validation workflows detected 2 (8.7%, this sample only)**. It does not re-run published results and does not touch any paper's claim; it explicitly declines to conclude the repositories are irreproducible.
2. **https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.03991** ("The challenge of reproducible ML: an empirical study on the impact of bugs") — bugs **observed, not injected** (737 PyTorch release-note bugs → 18 analysed as buggy/fixed build pairs); endpoint = model-performance difference by Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney; **no detection mechanism assessed**; 1 of 18 significant, and the authors conclude "we cannot conclude that our sample of bugs from PyTorch has an impact on model performance." Not prior art: no silence construct, no claim threshold, observational.
3. **https://arxiv.org/pdf/1010.1092** (Baggerly & Coombes, "Deriving chemosensitivity from cell lines: forensic bioinformatics and reproducible research in high-throughput biology", AOAS 2009) — re-analyses four *published* analyses. Error types: off-by-one row offsets ("Poor documentation hid an off-by-one indexing error affecting all genes reported"), sensitive/resistant label reversals ("at least one sensitive/resistant label reversal for every drug checked more than once"), duplicated test samples with conflicting labels, probesets absent from the platform, treatment confounded with run date, a heatmap belonging to the wrong drug. On silence: "These problems were hidden well enough to survive two explicit corrections." On consequentiality: a reported ROC AUC of 0.943 against the authors' own approximation of 0.348, "indicating if anything performance worse than chance" (**their approximation, carried as such, not a re-derived figure of mine**). And the line that bears directly on the candidate's fault catalogue: "the most common errors are simple (e.g., row or column offsets); conversely, it is our experience that the most simple errors are common."
4. **https://qspace.library.queensu.ca/items/80f7b8df-8ae7-489a-b5f1-a287c3821779** — spot-check of P3. **Real and as described**: Daniel Hook, "Using Code Mutation to Study Code Faults in Scientific Software", MSc (Computing), Queen's University, 2009-04-22; abstract describes Mutation Sensitivity Testing, which "focuses on assessing the mutation sensitivity of a test set", and the ~80%-of-expected-output oracle-uncertainty threshold. The record's characterisation is accurate and its abstract-depth caveat is honest.

**Prior spot-checks:** P1 (Chen et al., BMC Bioinformatics 2009;10:24) independently resurfaced with that exact title in D8; P3 verified in full above; P2 (Kiar et al., PLOS ONE 2021 / bioRxiv 2020.10.15.341495) not independently re-opened — fetch cap reached — so I neither confirm nor dispute the record's reading of it beyond noting its bibliographic plausibility. **Gap in the record's Gate 2 that I am recording as a finding:** Baggerly & Coombes (AOAS 2009) is named only as a genre in the "Reason for silence" field and is nowhere graded as a prior with its unit/endpoint/comparator/estimand. It is in the candidate's own field, it is about exactly the candidate's fault classes, and it documents the silence and the consequentiality qualitatively. It is not prior art on the estimand (case studies of four papers, no rate, no injection), but it should have been in the nearest-priors list.
