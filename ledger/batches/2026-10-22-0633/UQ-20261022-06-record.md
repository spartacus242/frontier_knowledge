Question (canonical) [intake version, V0]: In clinical laboratories whose analyser/middleware logs retain every measurement made on a specimen, what share of released patient results is not the first valid measurement of that analyte on that specimen (having been replaced by a rerun, a dilution, a delta-check or critical-value repeat, or a cross-instrument confirmation), and how much do population-level estimands computed from released results — reference-interval limits, prevalence of threshold-crossing abnormality, and the within-person delta used for monitoring — differ from the same estimands computed from first measurements?

Question (canonical) [R1, ADOPTED at Gate 1 — the one permitted reformulation, for well-posedness only; both versions recorded]: In >=2 accredited clinical laboratories running different middleware vendors and different repeat rule sets, under a rule declared before any data pull for which runs count as the "first valid measurement" (QC-failed, calibration-failed, clot/haemolysis-flagged and out-of-linearity runs excluded, with pre-specified sensitivity arms that vary each exclusion): (a) per analyte, what share of released results is not the first valid measurement of that analyte on that specimen, decomposed by the rule that caused the replacement (rerun, dilution, delta-check repeat, critical-value repeat, cross-instrument confirmation); (b) for those analytes, how do the paired first-measurement and released-result distributions differ; and (c) recomputed on both substrates, do the reference-interval limits, the prevalence of threshold-crossing abnormality, and the within-person monitoring delta differ by more than a materiality bound fixed in advance (reference-limit shift vs the analyte's published allowable bias; reclassification rate across the analyte's clinical decision limit; sign and size of the induced change in the monitoring delta) — with the share of candidate laboratories whose logs could not supply superseded measurements at all reported as a primary result?
Reason for the reformulation: V0 is answerable in more than one direction depending on undeclared choices ("first valid", which estimands, what counts as a difference). R1 fixes the declaration order, the decomposition by rule, the materiality bound and the feasibility denominator, and makes the null a reportable finding. No change of object.

Alternative formulations:
A1 (lay): How much of what a laboratory reports is a second opinion it gave itself, and does that change the numbers everyone downstream computes from its results?
A2 (metrological): What is the magnitude and direction of the within-laboratory selection effect that repeat-and-replace rules impose on the distribution of released results, and which released-result estimands are biased by it beyond a pre-declared materiality bound?

Motivating gap: Laboratories publish repeat-and-replace behaviour as operational quality-management fact (autoverification pass rates, delta-check and critical-value repeat policies, dilution and linearity rules), and the question the own-field literature puts to those rules is whether the repeat confirms the original. Verified at Gate 2 by opening the intake's named nearest hit at full text: Niu et al. 2013 asks exactly that and nothing about the released-result distribution. Meanwhile reference intervals (increasingly derived indirectly from laboratory/EHR data), EHR-derived phenotype prevalence, biological-variation estimates and monitoring deltas are all computed on released results — i.e. on the output of a within-laboratory selection rule that, in the sources opened here, nobody has characterised as a selection rule in this population or on this unit. One source opened here does characterise the mechanism as selection, but on blood donors, a fingerstick instrument and a variance-component estimand (see P2).

Significance — consequence: If the share is material and the estimands move beyond the pre-fixed bound, then three things computed daily on released results carry an unadjusted within-laboratory selection bias: indirectly derived reference intervals (a method family in active growth), EHR/biobank phenotype prevalence at decision limits, and the monitoring delta — and the bias is non-random by construction, concentrated at exactly the extreme values where clinical decisions are made and where reruns and dilutions are triggered. Guidance would need a "which measurement" clause and extracts would need a measurement-sequence field. If the answer is null — small share, or estimands equal within the bound — that is a positive licence for a substrate currently assumed unbiased with no evidence either way, and it retires a whole class of speculative objection to EHR-derived laboratory estimands. Either direction also yields the feasibility census, which is independently useful: nobody has published how many laboratories can even retrieve a superseded measurement.
Significance — audience: (i) laboratory directors and clinical chemists, who own the repeat rules and the middleware and could change rule sets or retention settings; (ii) the bodies that write reference-interval guidance (IFCC C-RIDL; the CLSI EP28/C28 line) and the authors of indirect-RI methods, who would have to specify which measurement enters the estimate; (iii) EHR-based epidemiologists and biobank lab-data curators (UK Biobank primary-care/hospital extracts, All of Us, the OMOP Measurement table), who would add a provenance field or a sensitivity arm; (iv) biological-variation estimators (the EFLM BV database), whose within-subject CVs are computed on released values and are the most direct casualty of a selection acting on measurement error. Each of these has a concrete artefact it would edit.
Significance — fecundity: does the direction of the selection differ by analyte and by rule type (dilution vs delta check vs critical repeat)? Does between-laboratory variation in autoverification rates induce between-site heterogeneity that current analyses read as biology or as population difference? Significance — anchoring: the validity problem of indirect reference intervals; EHR data-quality frameworks of the conformance/completeness/plausibility type, which have no construct for "which of several measurements of one specimen"; the downward bias of naive variance estimates under conditional repetition, now sourced to P2 at full text; the unresolved OMOP question of duplicate measurements from different measurement samples (surfaced by Q8, thread not opened); and the standing own-field debate on whether critical-value repeats are worth their cost (P1, and the unopened CLN/CAP Today commentary), which this question reframes from cost to distributional consequence.

Reason for silence: A disciplinary boundary with an incentive component, and NOT a new-instrument story. The repeat-and-replace rules live in quality management, whose governing frameworks (ISO 15189, CAP checklists, IFCC-derived quality indicators) ask a per-result question — is the value released for THIS patient correct — under which a replaced result is a success, not a selection event. The three downstream estimands live in reference-interval statistics and EHR epidemiology, where the released result is the atom and its intra-laboratory provenance is invisible because standard extracts carry one row per released result with no measurement-sequence field. So the laboratory holds the log but not the estimand, and the epidemiologist holds the estimand but not the log, and no one owns the join. The incentive component: the only audit frame the own field has built around repeats is cost and turnaround (P1's "unnecessary"; the unopened CLN piece titled "When Less Is Better"), which is answerable without ever characterising the released-result distribution, and a laboratory publishing that a measurable share of its released results are second opinions it gave itself invites an accreditation and medico-legal question it has no reason to volunteer. Deliberately NOT claimed: that the data only just became available. Middleware audit trails are not new, which is itself evidence that the silence is a boundary effect rather than an instrument effect — and that makes the novelty finding more credible, not less, since a boundary gap can persist indefinitely while an instrument gap closes.
