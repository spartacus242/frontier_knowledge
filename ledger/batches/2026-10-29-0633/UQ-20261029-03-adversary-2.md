## 1. Restatement in my own words

Take micrometre-sized grains of ordinary water ice. Below about 150 K the ice you make is not clean hexagonal ice (Ih): it is stacking-disordered, carrying cubic (Ic-like) stacking sequences that are frozen in place because the layers cannot reshuffle at that temperature. Warm it and the stacking sequence relaxes toward hexagonal. The question asks: **does that stacking sequence change how strongly two ice grains hold onto each other?** Concretely, press two grains together at a fixed temperature in 70–150 K for a fixed time under a fixed load, pull them apart, record the force per contact area it took; do it for a stacking-disordered batch and for a hexagonal batch whose grain size and specific surface area have been matched; report the ratio of the two mean strengths, with the stacking state read on the same grains before and after each pull. Pre-declared: a factor 1.5 counts as an effect, a ratio within 20% of unity counts as a null, ≥50 contacts per arm, ≥3 temperatures. Second limb: hold a stacking-disordered sample at one declared temperature in 170–200 K while the cubic sequences anneal out, and see whether contact strength moves relative to an already-hexagonal sample held at the same temperature for the same time (the paired control is there because at 170–200 K ordinary neck growth is itself fast).

So in one line: **is ice–ice cohesion a function of stacking order, on top of temperature, time, load and grain size — and is the stacking relaxation itself a mechanical event at the contact?**
