## Gate 0 — Intake

Question (canonical, V1 as submitted):
For procedures with per-case outcomes in registries, does a proceduralist's recency of performing that procedure — time since their last case, and case count in the preceding 30 and 90 days — predict patient outcome after conditioning on cumulative and annual volume, and what recency interval would an aviation-style currency rule have to specify to keep expected outcome within a pre-stated margin of that operator's own best performance?

Question (canonical, V2 — the one Gate 1 reformulation, used for grading):
In ONE named registry with operator identifiers and dated per-operator case histories, fitting patient outcome on a pre-declared recency exposure (log days since that operator's previous case of the same procedure; counts in the preceding 30 and 90 days) with operator fixed effects and adjustment for trailing-365-day and cumulative volume, case mix and calendar time: (a) is the recency coefficient distinguishable from zero, and (b) taking as each operator's own reference the fitted outcome at the shortest pre-declared gap stratum (e.g. ≤7 days) rather than an unstated "best performance", what is the gap length — with confidence interval — at which fitted expected outcome degrades by a pre-stated absolute risk difference, or is no such gap identified within the observed support of gaps?

Why reformulated (well-posedness only): V1's reference class, "that operator's own best performance", is the minimum of a noisily fitted within-operator curve and is upward-biased by construction; V1 also leaves the margin, the registry and the outcome unnamed, so limb (b) could not be settled either way. V2 fixes the reference to a pre-declared shortest-gap stratum and forces the margin and support to be stated. Both versions recorded, as required.

Alternative formulations:
- Do proceduralists lose measurable skill between cases, and how fast — is there an empirical half-life of procedural competence visible in patient outcomes?
- Is "currency" (recency of practice), the quantity aviation licenses on, an identifiable determinant of patient outcome in procedural medicine, separable from case volume?

Motivating gap (as submitted, with one correction established at Gate 2):
Aviation licenses on recency, not accumulation: takeoffs/landings within the preceding 90 days, instrument approaches in a rolling window, or the privilege lapses regardless of lifetime hours. Medicine privileges proceduralists on cumulative and annual volume thresholds and on time-limited certification. CORRECTION: the submitted claim that medicine "contains no recency term anywhere" is false as stated. The Medical Board of Australia operates a *Recency of practice* registration standard (under public review as of 2025-12-05, https://www.medicalboard.gov.au/News/2025-12-05-Have-your-say-on-the-review-of-the-Recency-of-practice-registration-standard.aspx), as do the other Ahpra boards. What survives of the gap is narrower and still true of the sources opened: those standards are generic, hours-based and multi-year, are not procedure-specific, and are not calibrated against any patient-outcome curve.

Presuppositions (status after Gate 2):
1. At least one registry holds operator identifiers with complete dated per-operator case histories — ESTABLISHED, and demonstrated in use: PHC4 Pennsylvania discharge data supplied surgeon licence numbers plus a days-since-last-procedure field (Hockenberry & Helmchen 2014).
2. An operator-fixed-effects design identifies a within-operator recency effect — CONTESTED, and the contest is now evidenced rather than hypothetical. In P1 the recency of *unrelated* procedures (any inpatient procedure) carried a *larger* mortality coefficient than recency of the same procedure (CABG): +0.067 pp vs +0.032 pp per day for in-hospital mortality. The candidate pre-declares exactly that contrast as its falsification test; it has been run, and it came back in the direction that fails the test. P1 instead reads it as evidence that general surgical skill, not procedure-specific skill, is what depreciates. The candidate's design cannot pre-declare this test as a clean negative control.
3. Outcome is measured on a timescale over which recency could act — CONTESTED for long-horizon endpoints (10-year revision); unproblematic for the endpoints the priors used (1-day, in-hospital, 30-day mortality).
4. Case-mix adjustment adequate at operator-episode level — CONTESTED, the standing weakness of volume-outcome work; P1's own balance checks (temporal distance vs comorbidity count, AMI at admission, SNF discharge) were null but are balance checks, not placebos.
5. Skill is the mechanism — explicitly NOT presupposed.
6. A credentialing body could act on a recency parameter — ASSUMPTION, and now weakly contradicted by eleven years of non-response to P1 (see Significance — audience).

What would count as an answer: the V2 estimation above, pre-registered, with (i) the recency coefficient and its CI conditional on volume, (ii) a pre-declared negative-control procedure and a falsification test on unrelated-procedure recency whose expected-null status is defensible *given* P1's result, and (iii) inversion of the fitted curve into the gap length at which expected outcome degrades by the pre-stated margin, with CI — or a demonstration that no such gap exists within observed support. A CI excluding a clinically material recency effect answers negatively and is directly usable: it tells privileging committees not to write recency rules.

Nearest prior formulations (three, all opened):

P1. Hockenberry JM, Helmchen LA. "The nature of surgeon human capital depreciation." *Journal of Health Economics* 2014;37:70-80, doi:10.1016/j.jhealeco.2014.06.001. Read in full as NBER WP 20017 (https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20017/w20017.pdf); published record confirmed at https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jhecon/v37y2014icp70-80.html.
- UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the patient discharge (56,315 CABG discharges, 188 surgeons, 62 hospitals, PHC4 Pennsylvania, 2006Q3-2010Q4), with exposure assigned at the operating surgeon (licence number).
- EXPOSURE: days since the surgeon's last CABG, and days since the surgeon's last inpatient procedure of any type — continuous, linear in days.
- ENDPOINT: 1-day mortality; in-hospital mortality; log total cost.
- REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: within-surgeon variation in days away, net of surgeon and hospital fixed effects, year fixed effects, trailing-365-day surgeon and hospital CABG volume, and patient covariates (age, race, payer, Elixhauser, vessels bypassed, admission type, AMI, bypass, valve).
- ESTIMAND: the within-surgeon conditional association between one additional day away from the operating room and patient outcome (linear probability model, SEs clustered by surgeon); not a randomised effect, and the authors flag endogenous scheduling.
- RESULT: in-hospital mortality +0.067 pp/day (SE 0.025) for any-procedure recency and +0.032 pp/day (SE 0.015) for CABG recency; 1-day mortality +0.046 pp (SE 0.014) and +0.010 pp (SE 0.006, n.s.); emergent patients of high-volume surgeons +0.398 pp (SE 0.101) in-hospital, an 11.4% relative effect; costs fall with days away. No CIs reported, only SEs.
- WHAT IT DOES NOT DO: no 30- or 90-day windowed counts; no non-linear recency curve and so no half-life; no pre-declared negative-control procedure; no inversion into any interval or threshold; no mention of aviation currency; no credentialing or privileging recommendation (its policy discussion is checklists, team reliance and scheduling).

P2. Huesch MD. "The impact of short breaks from cardiac surgery on mortality and stay length in California." *Journal for Healthcare Quality* 2014;36(5):42-49, doi:10.1111/jhq.12018. Read via https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-impact-of-short-breaks-from-cardiac-surgery-on-mortality-and-/ (abstract and study details).
- UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the isolated-CABG patient (56,956 patients, California 2003-2005), exposure assigned at surgeon level.
- EXPOSURE: binary — surgeon performed no isolated CABG in the calendar month preceding the month of the index operation (sensitivity variant: below the surgeon's own typical monthly workload). This is the candidate's "count in the preceding 30 days" limb, dichotomised.
- ENDPOINT: length of stay; in-hospital mortality; 30-day mortality.
- REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: patients of surgeons who did operate in the prior calendar month.
- ESTIMAND: adjusted mean difference in outcome between break-exposed and unexposed patients, adjusting for patient risk and the surgeon's long-run average monthly volume, with skew and within-surgeon clustering corrections (surgeon fixed effects not stated).
- RESULT: +0.5 day LOS (p=.037), not robust to skew and clustering corrections; no significant difference in hospital or 30-day mortality. Framed explicitly as "forgetting after not doing" and as hypothesis-screening, with a call for more granular data.

P3. de Andres Crespo M, Lykoudis PM, Myint F, Berlingieri P. "Surgery and technical skill decay." *International Journal of Surgery* 2025;111:3399-3413. Read via https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10210186/1/Surgery%20and%20technical%20skill%20decay.pdf.
- UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the study (narrative review of 41 studies); within those, the individual operator — predominantly students, trainees and simulator sessions, 34/41 with n<50.
- EXPOSURE: a retention interval (time out of training or away from operating), typically weeks to 2 years.
- ENDPOINT: technical-performance scores, operative time, error rate, self-reported confidence — not patient outcomes.
- REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: the same operator's pre-break performance, or non-break controls.
- ESTIMAND: presence and timing of decay in measured technical performance after a retention interval. Shortest reported decay 11 weeks (difficult laparoscopic tasks, medical students); longest 2 years for fine psychomotor skill; conclusion: "no precise timeframe for skill decay has been found".
- WHAT IT ESTABLISHES FOR THIS CANDIDATE: the target field's own 2025 review finds no study linking practising-surgeon skill decay to patient outcomes, proposes no interval, does not mention aviation, and does not discuss privileging on recency — and it cites neither P1 nor P2. Its stated gap is therefore evidence about the surgical-education literature's contents, not evidence that the measurement is absent.

## Gate 3 — Significance (argued)

Consequence — if yes: a recency term enters privileging. The lever is already built: privileges are volume-conditioned, Ahpra already runs a generic recency standard, and state boards already key re-entry requirements to months away, so the marginal regulatory act is to make an existing instrument procedure-specific and outcome-calibrated rather than to invent one. It would also change rota design, locum and part-time practice, leave policy, and the defensibility of low-frequency privileges held by otherwise high-volume operators — and it would partially reinterpret the volume-outcome literature, since annual volume is a smeared proxy for a rate whose recency structure nobody adjusts for. If no: a pre-stated CI excluding a material effect retires the transplant and tells privileging committees not to write recency rules — a usable negative, and the cheaper of the two answers to act on. If the true answer is P1's (general-procedure recency matters more than procedure-specific recency), the implication inverts awkwardly: currency would have to be defined over a bundle of activity rather than per privilege, which is not a rule any privileging form can currently express.

Audience — and an honest deflation: the named audience is hospital credentialing and privileging committees, specialty boards and societies that set volume thresholds, Joint Commission/Ahpra-type regulators, payers operating volume requirements, and registry custodians (NCDR, STS, NJR, GIQuIC). But P1 has been in the *Journal of Health Economics* since 2014 with an 11.4% relative mortality effect in its sharpest subsample, and the target field's own 2025 review does not cite it. Either the audience does not read health economics (the boundary mechanism below) or it has seen the finding and judged it unactionable. Both readings cut the claimed action-readiness: the audience is real, but the evidence that it would act is weaker than the candidate assumes, and this is the strongest single argument against the significance score.

Fecundity: what functional form does procedural competence decay in (the half-life question P3 says is unanswered for any operator population)? Is the decaying asset procedure-specific or general — P1's finding, if it survives replication, reframes the whole construct? Does team recency (the team's days since this operation together) dominate operator recency, which would relocate the intervention from licensing to rostering? Does recency mediate part of the measured volume-outcome gradient? Is there a recency analogue for cognitive specialties? And methodologically: how should a regulator set a threshold on a continuous exposure whose placebo is non-null?

Anchoring: directly on the volume-outcome literature and its unexplained heterogeneity, including the dissenting findings the candidate cites (case volume as a poor surrogate for laparoscopic skill; annual volume without outcome impact); on minimum-volume credentialing and payer volume requirements; on skill decay and retraining-interval research in resuscitation and simulation (P3); on the organizational-forgetting and interruption literature in operations management (query 6), which owns the method; and on physician re-entry regulation, which is a recency rule already in force with no empirical calibration.

## Reason for silence

For the core limb: there is no silence to explain — it was asked and answered twice in 2014. What needs explaining is why the *target* field does not know, and why the inversion limb was never attempted.

Mechanism, primary — disciplinary boundary with a vocabulary mismatch at every hop. The finding is filed under "human capital depreciation" in health economics and "organizational forgetting" in operations management; the construct is filed under "skill decay" in surgical education; the instrument that would act on it is filed under "recency of practice" and "currency" in credentialing and licensing. Query 6 returns P1 at the top of the method-owning field's vocabulary and query 8 does not return it at all in the target field's. P3, the 2025 target-field review, cites neither P1 nor P2 — a documented, dated instance of the boundary holding.

Mechanism, secondary — an incentive and liability gap specific to limb (b). A published recency interval becomes a discoverable standard of care: it converts every rota gap into an auditable exposure and every adverse outcome after a long gap into a documented departure from a published threshold, with the hospital rather than the operator holding the scheduling decision. That is a reason for societies and registry custodians to prefer a continuous association in an economics journal over a number in a privileging manual, and it explains the asymmetry between the two limbs — the association is published and the threshold is not.

Mechanism, declined: "no one thought of the aviation analogy" is not available. Aviation currency is the standard rhetorical furniture of patient-safety writing, and query 3 shows the credentialing trade press discusses privileging criteria constantly. The analogy is not what was missing; the per-operator dated case history linked to outcomes was expensive (P1 needed a restricted PHC4 extract to get a days-since-last-procedure field), and the threshold nobody wants to publish is a different obstacle again.

## Scores

Well-posedness: **2/3.** V2 states the registry requirement, the exposure, the adjustment set, the reference stratum, the margin requirement and the support condition, and both a positive and a negative answer are stated and usable — so it is not vague and not unfalsifiable. It falls short of 3 because the pre-declared negative control the design leans on has a published non-null value in P1 (presupposition 2), so the design as written cannot interpret its own falsification test; and because limb (b)'s answer is only defined relative to a margin the question still does not fix, leaving the "no such interval exists" branch dependent on observed support that varies by specialty.

Significance: **2/3.** Consequence is argued in both directions with an existing regulatory lever to act on, the negative answer is as usable as the positive, and the anchoring to volume-outcome is real. Held off 3 by the audience deflation above: an on-point positive finding has sat unactioned for eleven years, and limb (b)'s reason for silence is partly that the audience does not want the number.

Strongest remaining objection (for the adversary to press): the candidate is P1 with pre-registration and an extra exposure window, in a different registry — and P1's unrelated-procedure result suggests that what the candidate would measure is not procedure-specific currency at all, so even the design increments may be aimed at a quantity that does not exist in the form the transplant assumes.
