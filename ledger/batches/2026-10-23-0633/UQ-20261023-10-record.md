## Gate 0 — Intake

Question (canonical, V1 as submitted):
Among hospitalised patients whose dose of a renally-eliminated drug was reduced during an episode of reduced kidney function, what is the distribution of time from documented renal recovery to dose restoration, what share is never restored before the drug is stopped or the patient is discharged, and what cumulative fraction of the intended exposure is lost?

Question (canonical, V2 — the one Gate 1 reformulation, scored as canonical):
In a cohort of hospital admissions with linked timestamped medication-administration and laboratory records, restricted to a pre-declared drug list whose labels state a renal dose threshold AND a single full dose per indication (titrated drugs excluded by pre-declared rule), and restricted to episodes in which the administered dose fell below the labelled full dose while measured clearance was below that drug's label threshold:
 (a) taking renal recovery as measured clearance returning above that same label threshold and remaining above it for a declared interval (primary rule: 24 h; sensitivity arms at 12 h and 48 h, and an arm using a cystatin-C or measured-clearance estimate where available), estimate the cumulative incidence of dose restoration to the labelled full dose as a function of time since recovery, with discontinuation of the drug, discharge and death as competing risks;
 (b) report the share of episodes never restored before the first of those competing events;
 (c) report the exposure deficit as administered cumulative dose over the labelled full dose integrated across the post-recovery, pre-competing-event window, per drug class;
 (d) classify each non-restoration as documented-deliberate or undocumented from the chart's recorded rationale; and
 (e) report, for each contributing site, whether its deployed renal dosing rule set contains any rule that fires on a dose below the labelled full dose at a clearance above the threshold.

Reason for reformulation (well-posedness only): V1 leaves three things unfixed that decide the numbers — the recovery clock, the denominator of "intended exposure", and the window over which the deficit is integrated. V2 fixes each by stipulation and moves the CDS-directionality limb from the motivating prose into a reported output. No limb was added or dropped.

## Gate 3 — Significance (argued)

Consequence — if yes (a large deficit dominated by undocumented omission): the deficit is a measured, named, previously uncounted exposure loss in exactly the drug classes where exposure-response is steep, and the remedy is a one-line addition to an existing rule set rather than a new programme — add the mirror branch to the renal dosing rule, trigger on the eGFR crossing rather than on the order. Boussadi shows the branch is buildable and that humans miss under-doses even when it fires, so the finding would bear on alert placement and on who the alert goes to, not merely on whether to build it. If no (a small deficit concentrated in documented deliberate decisions): that is an equally publishable result and it closes a worry that currently circulates as folklore in stewardship rounds without a number attached; it also licenses stewardship teams to stop looking, which is a real resource decision.
Audience — four communities that would act, named concretely: (1) hospital antimicrobial stewardship teams, whose prospective-audit-and-feedback worklists are built from rule outputs and currently surface over-dosing and spectrum, not under-dosing at recovered clearance; (2) clinical informaticists who own the renal dosing rule configuration — the action is a rule, which is cheap, auditable and testable; (3) anticoagulation stewardship and AF services, for whom DOAC under-dosing is already a live quality measure but is measured cross-sectionally against label criteria rather than as a failure to revert; (4) AKI follow-up and nephrology pharmacy services, who own the recovery event but not the medication ledger. The disciplinary split between (4) and (1)-(2) is itself part of the reason for silence.
Fecundity — new questions that become askable: does the restoration hazard depend on who is on service (pharmacist-staffed vs not), on whether the reduction was made by a pharmacist or a prescriber, or on handover boundaries; is the deficit visible in outcomes (treatment failure, recurrence, thromboembolism) once it can be quantified as an exposure rather than as an error count; do the same ratchets exist for non-renal dose reductions (hepatic, cytopenia, weight-based dosing after oedema resolves), which is the obvious generalisation and the first thing a positive result would provoke; and does the existence of an under-dose rule branch (Boussadi-style) measurably change the restoration hazard at sites that have one — a natural comparison limb (e) sets up.
Anchoring — existing open problems and anomalies it touches: the augmented-renal-clearance underdosing literature (which asks the same exposure question from the other end of the clearance distribution and never meets the recovery event); the AKI drug-restart literature (RAS blockers after AKI), which has established that stop/restart decisions after renal events are poorly made and measurable, but treats the agent as binary and never the dose as continuous; the DOAC inappropriate-dosing quality-measure literature, which counts label discordance at a point in time; and the therapeutic-inertia family, which owns the method for "a change that should have happened and did not" but has applied it to initiation and up-titration of guideline therapy, never to reversion after a transient contraindication.
Why this is not novel trivia: the action implied by a positive answer is a rule change in a system that already exists, by a team that already exists, at a cost close to zero — and the question's negative answer is equally informative. The weak point is honest: the clinical-benefit limb is deliberately out of scope, so a large deficit is an exposure finding and not yet a harm finding, and an audience member could reasonably wait for the harm study.

Reason for silence (candidate mechanism, stated as a mechanism and not as an oversight):
Three mechanisms compound, and they are all structural rather than psychological.
(1) THE HARM MODEL IS ASYMMETRIC AND SO IS THE ERROR TAXONOMY. Renal dosing entered the patient-safety literature through overdose toxicity, which produces a named adverse event attached to an identifiable patient. An un-restored low dose produces no event, no incident report and no adverse-drug-event code — so the denominator is invisible to the incident-reporting and ADE-detection systems from which medication-safety research agendas are generated. The same asymmetry is visible in the titles the sweep returned: the Br J Clin Pharmacol paper is "Failure of prescribers to adjust antibiotic dose to IMPAIRED renal function"; the EAHP abstract is "dose adjustment of ceftolozane/tazobactam IN RENAL FAILURE". The vocabulary itself has only one direction in it.
(2) THE DOMINANT STUDY DESIGN CANNOT SEE THE QUANTITY. The field's instrument for renal dosing quality is the cross-sectional appropriateness audit — point-prevalence concordance of a prescription with a dosing rule. Donnelly (2,472 prescription lines, 81.1% concordant) and Rodriguez-Reyes (46 patients, 63.0% inappropriate) are both that design, and a design with no clock structurally cannot produce a time-to-restoration distribution or an integrated exposure deficit, however many times it is repeated. The transition is invisible not because nobody looked but because the standing instrument has no time axis.
(3) A DISCIPLINARY BOUNDARY RUNS THROUGH THE EVENT. Renal recovery is owned by nephrology and critical care, which track it as an outcome; the medication ledger is owned by pharmacy and informatics, which track orders. Eppenga is the clearest illustration — a nephrology-journal protocol that measures the renal transition in both directions and then asks only whether a medication change WOULD be needed by a rule, never whether anyone made one. The recovery event and the restoration event sit in two literatures, and the quantity that joins them belongs to neither.
What I am NOT claiming as a mechanism: that rule sets could not fire on recovery. They can, and one does (Boussadi, 962 rules with an under-dose branch). The silence is in the measurement and the taxonomy, not in the tooling's capability — which is a weaker and better-evidenced mechanism than the one the intake proposed.

Strongest remaining objection (for Gate 4 to press): that the never-restored share is largely an artefact of the competing risks themselves. Courses of renally-dosed anti-infectives are short, and if recovery typically occurs at or after the end of a course, a large never-restored share would be arithmetic about course length rather than a finding about clinical behaviour — and the exposure deficit over a window that closes on the next day would be small by construction. The candidate's competing-risks estimator exposes this rather than hiding it, but the entry needs the distribution of (time of recovery) minus (time of drug stop) to know whether there is any window to measure at all.
