Question (canonical) — Gate 1 REFORMULATED version (this is the one that goes forward):
On long-horizon agentic benchmarks run with greedy decoding, recorded-and-replayed tool responses, fixed seeds and a fixed harness, (i) how large is the residual run-to-run variance in task success attributable to batch-composition-dependent floating-point nondeterminism in the serving stack — measured as the gap between replicate variance under default kernels with batch composition deliberately varied and replicate variance under batch-invariant kernels — and (ii) is the default-kernel mean success rate equal to the mean over pinned numeric paths, or systematically lower, with any gap scaling in rollout horizon?

Question (canonical) — Gate 0 ORIGINAL version (recorded, superseded):
In long-horizon agentic LLM evaluations run greedily (temperature 0) with every non-model source of nondeterminism pinned, what share of run-to-run variance in task success is attributable to batch-composition-dependent floating-point nondeterminism in the serving stack, and does removing it with batch-invariant kernels shift the mean score as well as the variance?

Reason for the one permitted Gate 1 reformulation (well-posedness only, no change of subject):
The original's first limb is degenerate as stated. If *every* non-model source of nondeterminism is genuinely pinned and decoding is greedy, serving-stack numerics is the only source left, so its "share of variance" is 1 by construction — the quantity carries no information, and any value below 1 only shows the pinning failed. The informative estimand is the *magnitude* of that residual variance and the *gap* between a jittered-batch arm and a batch-invariant arm, not a share of a total that has been defined down to one term. The reformulation also fixes the reference problem in the second limb: batch-invariant kernels are not a ground truth (see P1), so "shift the mean" has to be stated against a distribution over pinned numeric paths rather than against one privileged path. Subject matter, mechanism and both limbs are unchanged.

Alternative formulations:
1. Is serving-stack numerical nondeterminism a zero-mean noise source for agent benchmark scores, or a biased one whose bias grows with rollout horizon?
2. When two otherwise identical greedy agent rollouts of the same task diverge because kernel reduction order depended on what else was in the serving batch, how often does the divergence change the final outcome, and in which direction?

Motivating gap (source method: anomaly mining):
Practitioners report that identical temperature-0 requests to the same served model return different completions. The mechanism was identified and patched in open-source kernels by Thinking Machines Lab's "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference" (Sept 2025): attention and matmul reductions are not batch-invariant, so a request's numerics depend on its batch neighbours; that post draws out the consequence for RL (sampler/trainer mismatch) and stops there. Separately, run-to-run variance in agentic benchmarks is now reported as a validity problem in its own right — Bjarnason, Silva & Monperrus find pass@1 swings of 2.2-6.0 pp on SWE-Bench-Verified with SDs above 1.5 pp "even at temperature 0" (arXiv:2602.07150), and Masoudian et al. attribute ~39% of observed variability to the inference backend (arXiv:2608.04714). The anomaly is reported and its mechanism is explained, but the second-order question is not posed: whether this channel is *noise* (zero-mean, widens intervals) or *bias* (shifts the point estimate, plausibly downward, because failure states in long rollouts are more absorbing than recovery states are). The distinction decides whether published pass@1 numbers are merely imprecise or systematically understated, and it is newly testable only because batch-invariant kernels now exist as a shipped, controlled counterfactual (vLLM exposes batch invariance as a documented feature).

The bias mechanism the question is actually testing, stated so it can be refuted:
Under default kernels a rollout is not a draw from a fixed numeric path — it is a walk *across* numeric paths, because batch composition changes from step to step over hundreds of steps. Under batch invariance the rollout follows one consistent path. If an outcome-changing token flip anywhere along the horizon can drop the agent into a state it does not recover from, the jittered walk gets many more chances to touch such a token than any single pinned path does, and the asymmetry between absorbing failure and non-absorbing success converts extra chances into a lower mean. This predicts bias that grows in horizon, which is the testable content; a flat or zero gap refutes it.

Presuppositions:
P1. Batch-invariant kernels compute the same mathematical function as the default ones up to a fixed reduction order — CONTESTED in the sense that matters: their numerics are not bit-identical to the default path, so "which arm is unbiased" cannot be assumed and must be established against either a high-precision reference or, more practically, a *set* of pinned paths (several fixed batch sizes / tensor-parallel degrees) whose spread bounds how much of any measured gap is just path choice.
P2. An agent harness can be made deterministic apart from model numerics — CONTESTED: tool latency, network, sandbox state, server-side retries and provider-side routing are independent nondeterminism sources. The design must record and replay tool responses, and must report a leakage check (replicates under a single pinned path should collapse to one trajectory; if they do not, P2 failed and the experiment is uninterpretable).
P3. Long-horizon agent tasks have asymmetric outcome dynamics, failure states being more absorbing than success states are — CONTESTED, and deliberately so: this is part of what the question asks, not an input. It enters only as the predicted *direction* of any gap.
P4. Evaluation harnesses actually use greedy decoding where they report deterministic scores — varies by harness, must be audited rather than assumed. (The question as reformulated imposes greedy decoding on its own arms, so P4 bears on external validity, not on internal validity.)
P5. Run-to-run variance under fixed configuration is currently reported or recoverable from released logs — PARTLY CONTESTED; arXiv:2602.07150 supplies ~60,000 trajectories' worth for one benchmark, but the numerics-specific decomposition is not recoverable from any released log, so this limb must be generated, not re-analysed.
P6. A rollout's divergence point (first differing token) is identifiable — established for self-run experiments, contested for third-party reported results. The experiment is self-run, so this holds where it is needed.
P7. Batch composition is manipulable by the experimenter — ESTABLISHED for self-hosted open-weight serving, FALSE for hosted APIs, where co-tenant traffic sets it. This bounds the question to self-hosted arms and makes the hosted-API case an extrapolation, not a measurement.

What would count as an answer:
A paired experiment on at least two agentic benchmarks (one software-engineering, one computer-use or terminal, to vary horizon length and failure structure) and at least two model scales, in which the same task set is run K times under (a) default kernels with batch composition deliberately randomised across replicates, (b) batch-invariant kernels, and (c) at least two distinct pinned configurations within arm (b), with tool responses recorded and replayed and seeds fixed. It must report: a leakage check showing arm (b) replicates collapse to identical trajectories; the residual variance in arm (a) and the variance gap (a)-(b), alongside the sampling and environment components measured by separately unpinning each; the paired difference in mean success between (a) and the (b)-path distribution, with confidence intervals; the rate at which a first-divergence event propagates to an outcome change and its direction; and the interaction of the mean gap with horizon (steps or tokens to completion), tested as a pre-registered trend, not a post-hoc split.
- A mean difference indistinguishable from zero, with an interval tight relative to typical reported model gaps (~1-3 pp), and no horizon trend → "noise": published scores are imprecise, and existing multi-run advice already covers it.
- A consistent deficit under default kernels that grows with horizon → "bias": reported scores are configuration-dependent point estimates, and batching/kernel configuration becomes part of what a benchmark must specify.
- A null on both limbs (residual variance negligible *and* no mean shift) is a complete answer and closes the question — it would also show that the temp-0 variance others report comes from somewhere else, which is itself informative.

Novelty — how this differs from each:
vs A: A measures the *size* of agentic run-to-run variance and establishes it survives temperature 0; it does not ask where that variance comes from. It runs no kernel arm, performs no decomposition into numerics/sampling/environment, does not replay tool responses to pin the environment, and — decisively — never tests whether the mean moves. A's recommendation (report pass@k, run K times) is the correct response to *noise* and is silent on *bias*: if the mean is shifted, running K times estimates a shifted mean more precisely. A is the closest prior on limb (i) and does not touch limb (ii).
vs B: B establishes the mechanism and the fix at the kernel level and draws the consequence for RL training, where the harm is a distribution mismatch between sampler and trainer. It does not treat evaluation scores as the affected quantity, does not run agent rollouts, and does not ask the direction-of-effect question. The candidate takes B's artifact (shipped batch-invariant kernels) and uses it as an experimental control on a question B does not pose.
vs C: C compares *across* backends and attributes a variance share to backend identity — a between-configuration contrast, where the candidate's contrast is within one serving stack, between jittered and pinned batch composition. C's benchmarks are single-turn factual and social-bias tasks with no horizon, so the horizon-interaction limb cannot arise; C uses no batch-invariant kernel arm, names no numerical mechanism, and reports no mean bias attributable to numerics.
Cross-field note bearing on Disguise: the candidate's exact logical form — "do bit-level reduction-order differences merely add spread, or do they produce a statistically distinguishable distribution?" — is a solved, named problem in climate and ocean modelling (ensemble-consistency testing; query 7). That is the strongest reason to suspect the question is a renamed known one, and also the reason it is answerable: the statistical machinery exists. What does not exist is any transplant of it to LLM evaluation, and the LLM case differs structurally in a way that is not a mere relabelling — a climate model has no absorbing failure state and no discrete pass/fail readout, which is precisely where the asymmetry argument (P3) lives.

Significance — consequence:
If the answer is "bias" — a deficit under default kernels that grows with horizon — then every published agentic pass@1 is a point estimate conditional on an unreported variable (batch composition and kernel configuration), and the direction of the error is known: scores understate the model. Leaderboard comparisons between models served under different batching regimes become non-comparable in a way that extra replicates cannot fix, because averaging over a biased distribution converges to the biased mean. Benchmarks would have to specify batching/kernel configuration the way they already specify prompts and scaffolds, and the gap would scale into exactly the long-horizon regime that current evaluation is moving toward. If the answer is "noise", the consequence is narrower but still real: it retires a live hypothesis, bounds how much of the 2.2-6.0 pp variance prior A reports can be blamed on the serving stack, and redirects attention to scaffold and environment sources. The asymmetry between the branches is honest to state: the "noise" branch mostly confirms advice already given, which is why this scores 2 and not 3.

Significance — audience:
(a) Benchmark maintainers and third-party evaluation bodies (SWE-bench, terminal/computer-use harness maintainers, UK AISI-style evaluators): would act by adding batching/kernel configuration to the reported configuration surface and, under "bias", by re-running or re-qualifying headline numbers. Prior A's existence shows this community is already acting on variance findings, which is evidence the audience is real rather than hypothetical. (b) RL practitioners: B already tells them mismatch matters for training; a horizon-scaling bias result tells them the reward signal itself is biased in the same direction as the eval, which bears on whether batch-invariant kernels are worth their throughput cost in the rollout path. (c) Procurement and third-party evaluators comparing vendors on agentic scores: under "bias", a vendor's score depends on serving configuration, so a contractual score becomes meaningless without specifying it. (d) Serving-engine maintainers (vLLM, SGLang): a measured eval-score consequence changes the cost-benefit of making batch invariance a default rather than an opt-in feature.

Significance — fecundity:
Answering it makes askable: whether the same channel biases LLM-as-judge scoring in the same direction; whether quantisation, speculative decoding, prefix-cache hits and tensor-parallel degree form a family of batch-composition-like channels with a common bias signature (the Zhang et al. tensor-parallel work suggests the family is real); whether a "numeric-path ensemble" is a cheap uncertainty estimator for agent tasks, flipping the nuisance into an instrument; whether the absorbing-failure asymmetry (P3), once measured, predicts which tasks are fragile independently of numerics; and whether published horizon-scaling laws for agent success are partly measurement artifacts of this channel.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches (i) the agentic-eval validity problem as posed by prior A and arXiv:2605.23950 on harness disclosure; (ii) the RL sampler/trainer mismatch problem named by prior B and attacked by Zhang et al. (arXiv:2511.17826); (iii) the reproducibility-of-ML-results literature, where nondeterminism is a known confound but always as variance, never as bias; (iv) the ensemble-consistency-testing tradition in climate modelling, which supplies the statistical form of the answer; (v) the open question of whether agent failure is compounding-error-driven (arXiv:2609.01660, arXiv:2608.06663), to which limb (ii) contributes a measurement.

Reason for silence:
Primarily new-instrument. Before batch-invariant kernels shipped (TML Sept 2025; vLLM batch-invariance as a documented feature; tensor-parallel-invariant variants in 2026), there was no way to construct the pinned arm, so the question could be speculated about but not posed as an experiment — and a question with no available counterfactual does not get written down as a measurement. Second, a disciplinary boundary that runs exactly through this question: the people who understand that reductions are batch-variant are kernel and serving engineers, and the people who run, report and audit agent benchmarks are evaluation-methodology researchers. Prior B stops at the consequence its own community owns (RL training) and does not cross into score validity; prior A arrives at the phenomenon from the evaluation side, observes that it persists at temperature 0, and does not reach down to the kernel. Each side's natural account of the anomaly is complete within its own frame, which is how a question falls into the seam. Third, a mild incentive gap: establishing that agentic scores are configuration-dependent point estimates is costly to whoever publishes the scores, and the organisations with the deepest kernel expertise are largely the organisations publishing the scores. Not claimed: that nobody thought of it — practitioner posts gesture at it repeatedly, which is why the silence needs the instrument-plus-boundary explanation rather than an oversight story.
