## Restatement in my own words

Take a population where every CT scan's delivered dose was recorded by the machine itself and is tied to a named hospital, a named scanner unit and a named protocol, and where those people can be followed in a cancer registry. Most of the variation in how many milligray a given child gets for "the same" scan is not about the child — it is about which building they walked into. So: use the dose that *other* patients got for the same examination class on the same scanner-protocol in the same quarter as a stand-in (instrument) for this patient's own dose, run the dose–cancer regression that way, run the ordinary dose–cancer regression on exactly the same person-years, and report the gap between the two numbers with an interval and a pre-declared equivalence margin, plus a first stage and a falsification battery. The implicit promise: if the two numbers agree, the long-standing complaint that the paediatric-CT dose–response is really "sicker children get scanned more" loses its footing; if they disagree, the field finally has a size for that bias.

My reading of what the candidate is actually doing statistically: projecting individual dose onto facility-scanner-protocol-quarter cell means. That is a grouping estimator. Which raises the question of what the gap in limb (c) is a gap *of*.

## What the two decisive reads established

**(A) The record's flagged presupposition 7 is TRUE, and stronger than flagged.** From the IARC EPI-CT dosimetry document, quoted as served (each fragment short and verbatim from the fetch):
- "The primary source of technical information was metadata from a sample of images stored on PACS."
- "PACS dataset was not used at the individual patient level."
- "Linkage between PACS and RIS data was only possible for a relatively small subset of CT scans (30,000)."
- "Each missing dosimetric parameter was derived from a probability density function."
- "Values of parameters shared among members of a subgroup (for example, the CT machine...) were sampled once."
- "unshared dosimetric parameters (for example, anatomical area explored) were sampled from the relevant PDFs on an individual basis."
- Questionnaire participation 21%; "Only 23% of the participating hospitals provided information on machine settings."
- CTDIvol was unavailable for "the vast majority of examinations"; RDSR is not mentioned in the document at all.

So in the comparator literature the per-examination dose a patient is assigned is a facility/machine/period quantity drawn once per subgroup, individually linked for roughly 30,000 of some 1.43 million examinations. The patient-level contrast in EPI-CT-class cumulative dose comes overwhelmingly from how many scans, of what type, of what body region — not from individual milligray.

**(B) The record's one unread on-point own-field methods paper contains no instrument.** Smoll, Mathews & Scurrah 2020: latent-class finite mixture model over scan-to-diagnosis interval; "Instrumental variable: None"; no facility-, hospital-, scanner- or protocol-level variation; exposure is scan occurrence and counts, not dose, with no mGy or mSv anywhere; and "Clinical indication for individual CT scans are not included in Medicare records." This **removes** the record's principal stated ground for grading novelty Medium rather than High. Novelty holds and is, if anything, understated.

Note the sting in (B) for the candidate's comparator: the cohort that supplies the ERR 0.056/mSv number does not record indication, so V2's "same person-years" conventional comparator cannot be computed in that cohort — it would have to be a different cohort, which is consistent with V2 but not with the record's framing of differencing against the published number.
