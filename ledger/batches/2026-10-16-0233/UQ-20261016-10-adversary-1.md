# Adversary #1 log — UQ-20261016-10

## Restatement (my own words, before reading the generator's framing back)

Pick one pathogen and one stretch of calendar time. Enumerate every nucleic-acid
amplification test that held a regulatory authorisation somewhere in that window.
For each test, find out which stretch of the pathogen's genome it amplifies — exact
oligo coordinates if public, otherwise the gene, otherwise "unknown". Weight each
test not by existing but by how many tests of that kind were actually run worldwide.
Then ask two things:

(a) How bunched together are those targets on the genome? Report it as a
market-concentration index (HHI) and as its reciprocal, read as "how many genuinely
independent targets does world testing effectively have".

(b) Given a model of where on the genome mutations arrive, and a model of when a
mismatch actually makes a test stop working, what is the chance that one single
circulating mutational or recombination event knocks out at least 10/25/50% of
world testing throughput simultaneously?

In one line: **is the world's molecular testing for a pathogen a monoculture, and
what is the one-event systemic risk that follows?**

## Single strongest killing point

The record's own retrospective falsification test defeats it. S-gene target failure — named as the positive control the estimator must assign non-negligible probability to — is the textbook case of a three-target assay losing one target, **remaining diagnostically positive**, and being repurposed as a free surveillance signal: roughly zero capacity lost. The second supplied instance is also a *dual*-target assay merely *degraded*. So the quantity the question is built to estimate (volume-weighted capacity destroyed by one event) is systematically overstated by a target-region HHI whenever assays are multiplex with any-target calling, which is the normal configuration; and the record specifies no rule for splitting a multiplex assay's volume across its targets, leaving part (a) undefined for that normal case. The question is not merely unverified — as written, its headline statistic measures something other than what it claims, and its own validation test would calibrate it against non-events.
