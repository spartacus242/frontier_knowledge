## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Take every building in some EGMS-covered region, declared in advance. From the free Copernicus ground-motion service, compute for each building how much faster one part of its footprint is sinking than another (a differential *vertical velocity rate* over the ~2015-onward service window, not the settlement accumulated since the building was built). Compare that rate against the limiting values the geotechnical textbooks give — Skempton–MacDonald's angular distortion limit and Burland–Wroth's deflection-ratio limit — treating "exceeds the limit" as a positive screening test. Then check the test against an independent record of which buildings actually show cracking at Burland grade 2 or worse: how many damaged buildings does it catch (sensitivity), how many undamaged it wrongly flags (specificity, ROC), and, on a random verified sub-sample that fixes how common damage really is, what share of flagged buildings are truly damaged (PPV). Boscardin–Cording is parked as not evaluable because it also needs horizontal strain. Finally, on the buildings that also have manual levelling, estimate how much of any poor performance is just because a recent rate was used in place of cumulative distortion.

In short: *is "exceeds the settlement-damage threshold, as measured by satellite rate" a useful screening test for real building damage across a population, and if not, is that the criterion's fault or the instrument's?*
