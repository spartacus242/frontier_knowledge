Verification (post-batch, fresh-context checker, log UQ-20261016-53-verification.md): the kill's evidence is CONFIRMED as quoted, and its reach is narrower than the adversary implied.
Confirmed: both fragments are in the Deep Learning Tuning Playbook (github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook), in "Identifying scientific, nuisance, and fixed hyperparameters" under "Designing the next round of experiments" — "need to be optimized over in order to fairly compare" is the definition of a nuisance hyperparameter, and "would not be very insightful" is the weight-decay example, that comparing model sizes at one fixed weight-decay value tells you little. It does prescribe re-optimising nuisance hyperparameters separately for each scientific option, running one tuning study per option and comparing the best trial from each, and it frames the risk it is guarding against as under-tuning producing unfair comparisons.
Narrower than claimed: the playbook says NOTHING about confounding, estimability, or bias of the scientific factor's main effect under per-arm re-tuning — the checker searched the page text and found none of those terms in that connection. And no published position in machine learning was found that treats per-arm re-tuning as a confound rather than as fair comparison; the nearest paper opened, Choi et al. 2019 (arXiv:1910.05446), argues for fuller per-optimiser tuning, i.e. the same way. So the objection is not "the field has already settled the aliasing question against you"; it is that the field has never adopted the separable factorial main effect as its estimand, and states a different comparison target in its own guidance. That is still a presupposition failure — the candidate's headline share is fixed by whichever estimand the coder imposes rather than measured from the literature — but it rests on one guidance document plus a bounded absence, which is recorded here rather than dressed up as a proof.
