Domain(s): AI (large-scale training systems; compute governance and verification); STEM — power systems engineering and measurement (synchrophasors/PMU, point-on-wave recording, electrical-network-frequency forensics); neighbours: non-intrusive load monitoring, hardware power side channels, media forensics

Question (canonical) — V0 (intake, Gate 0): Do the second-to-subsecond synchronised power swings of a large AI training job leave a signature detectable in measurements made outside the facility — distribution- or transmission-level PMU data, or wide-area frequency and electrical-network-frequency recordings — and if so, what is the detection threshold in job megawatts and local grid strength, and which job attributes (training vs idle, checkpoint/restart cadence, collective-communication oscillation frequency, scale-up events) are recoverable from that external signal?

Question (canonical) — R1 (one reformulation, adopted at Gate 1, for well-posedness only): For a single-cluster AI training campaign of known power profile and known phase schedule, measured simultaneously inside the facility and outside its fence: (a) in the 0.05–55 Hz band, what is the ROC for discriminating "training active" from "facility energised but not training" using (i) utility PMU and point-on-wave records at the point of interconnection and at buses one, two and more than three electrical nodes away, and (ii) a publicly recordable electrical-network-frequency trace taken from a wall socket elsewhere on the same interconnection — expressed as a function of job size in MW and of local grid strength (short-circuit ratio at the PCC, local inertia), with a stated floor below which the test is at chance; (b) which of four pre-named job attributes — training/idle state, checkpoint-restart cadence, dominant collective-communication oscillation frequency, scale-up/scale-down events — are estimable from each measurement class on held-out jobs, with per-attribute error bars; and (c) what is the confusion rate against a comparison set of other large flexible loads (electrolyser, electric arc furnace, crypto mining, pumped load) and against the same facility running its own power-smoothing hardware?

Alternative formulations:
- Can an observer outside a datacentre tell from grid measurements alone whether a large model is being trained inside it, and at what scale does that become possible?
- Is there an off-site electrical side channel that distinguishes AI training load from other large industrial load, and what does it disclose about the job?

Motivating gap: The forward problem is documented and the mitigation is shipping: "Power Stabilization for AI Training Datacenters" (arXiv:2508.14318) describes synchronised multi-megawatt oscillations driven by collective-communication phases, and current rack platforms ship power-smoothing for exactly this reason. On the grid side, PNNL/NASPI have published on whether existing measurement systems can even represent these oscillations. Compute-governance verification proposals, by contrast, rest on on-site, cooperation-dependent mechanisms (hardware attestation, chip telemetry, inspection, billing-level energy accounting). An externally measurable, non-cooperative physical channel with a stated detection floor would be a different instrument, and a negative answer (mitigation closes the channel) would retire a class of proposed mechanisms. Gate 2 found that this inverse problem is no longer untouched: a simulation-only utility-side detector and a chip-level workload classifier both appeared in 2026 (see nearest priors).

Presuppositions:
P1 Training-induced oscillations survive aggregation at the point of common coupling and remain above background at the nearest external measurement point. CONTESTED, and part of what is asked. Two findings bear on it: utility-proposed oscillation limits are set at 3.5–25 MW peak-to-peak (PNNL-39191 lists ATC 25 MW p-p above 0.1 Hz; ERCOT 10 MW per 5 s; LIPA 3.5 MW in 5–55 Hz; AESO ±160 kW sub-synchronous), which implies oscillations at those amplitudes are expected to be visible at the interconnection; and on-site batteries, supercapacitors and firmware smoothing exist to remove them.
P2 External measurements at adequate sample rate are obtainable lawfully and non-intrusively. PARTLY CONTESTED, and now sharpened: PNNL-39191 finds PMUs have an IEC/IEEE validated bandwidth of 2 Hz (P-class) or 5 Hz (M-class) regardless of reporting rate, that "oscillation frequencies that exceed half of the reporting rate cannot be faithfully represented", and gives a case where a 4.8 MW 20 Hz oscillation was missed by a 120 fps P-class PMU but recovered in point-on-wave data. So the measurement class, not only access, bounds which attributes are recoverable; PMU feeds are access-restricted, ENF is recordable by anyone on the interconnection, and the access asymmetry between researchers, states and the public must be stated, not assumed away.
P3 Other large flexible loads do not produce confusable signatures. UNKNOWN; a required arm of the study, not a premise. CIGRE C4-10564 (2026), "Load characteristics of emerging bulk loads: electrolysers and data centres in the renewable energy era" (publication page seen in search results, not opened), indicates the comparison material exists.
P4 A facility's load at the relevant timescale is dominated by one coherent job. CONTESTED: mixed training-plus-inference fleets and multi-tenant colocation blur or destroy coherence; scope is therefore restricted to large single-cluster runs. Note that arXiv:2609.18997's detection statistic is explicitly a synchronisation/coherence measure, which makes this presupposition the load-bearing one for the whole channel.
P5 Ground truth about a real job (MW, phase schedule, parallelism strategy) can be obtained for at least one instrumented campaign. FEASIBILITY presupposition; requires an operator partner or a scaled surrogate cluster whose extrapolation must be argued. The two 2026 priors did not meet it (one simulated, one single-GPU bench).
P6 Someone would act on the answer. Grid reliability planners clearly would. The governance/verification use is CONTESTED on legal and ethical grounds and must be argued, not asserted.
Not presupposed: that the channel is open, that any attribute is recoverable, or that mitigation is incomplete.

What would count as an answer: (1) An instrumented ground-truth study: a training job of known megawatt profile and known phase schedule, measured simultaneously inside the facility and at one or more external buses, yielding a detection ROC as a function of job MW and local grid strength (SCR/inertia), with a stated floor below which nothing is recoverable, and with each measurement class reported separately (PMU at P- and M-class reporting rates, point-on-wave, and wall-socket ENF). (2) A classification and estimation result on held-out jobs for the four named attributes, with confusion analysis against other industrial loads and against the facility's own mitigation hardware operating. (3) An explicit statement of which measurement classes support which inferences. Pre-committed outcomes, all settled by data: channel open with quantified threshold and attribute leakage; channel open only for reliability-relevant episode detection but carrying no job-level information (the outcome N2 below makes most likely); channel closed by mitigation, which would retire a class of proposed verification mechanisms.

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, with sources actually opened):
N1. Gargiulo, S. & Kulp, G., "Workload Identification with Physical Side Channels for AI Governance", arXiv:2609.00309, dated 31 Aug 2026 (opened: https://www.roboticscenter.ai/research/papers/workload-identification-with-physical-side-channels-for-ai-governance-2609; abstract/metadata page only, full PDF not opened). Classifies training vs inference vs non-AI workloads from NVIDIA H200 power draw — about 930 five-second traces at roughly 10 MHz — at 97% accuracy / 0.955 macro-F1 on unseen model families; states that "an external observer can identify the class of the workload running on an NVIDIA H200 from its power draw" and that "such a physical channel can in principle be observed independently of operator cooperation"; reports that "training is particularly recognizable through the memory-bound optimizer update", spectral content mostly below about 20 kHz, and four tested evasion strategies with a hardened detector.
N2. Chaudhary, C., Tiwari, A., Pei, Y., Ben-Idris, M. & Mitra, J., "Detection of Synchronized AI Data Center Load Episodes Using SCADA Telemetry", arXiv:2609.18997v1, eess.SY/cs.SY (opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18997; the fetch reported a submission date of 2026-07-17, which is inconsistent with a 2609 arXiv identifier — the date is recorded as unverified). Detects synchronised AI data-centre load episodes from standard substation active-power telemetry with no new instrumentation, trained classifiers or labels: a Synchronization Index (dominant-eigenvalue fraction of a sliding sample covariance across substations), a CUSUM delay-bounded episode alarm, and eigenvector-based attribution to contributing substations. Validation is simulation only — an RTDS model of the IEEE 39-bus system with three AI data-centre buses — and performance is stated qualitatively as a "wide margin over chance".
N3. Chatterjee, K., Follum, J., Varghese, A., Biswas, S., Farantatos, E. & Zhu, L., "Measurement Adequacy for Monitoring Data Center Oscillations", PNNL-39191, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory for DOE Office of Electricity, April 2026 (opened: https://naspi.org/sites/default/files/reference_documents/Measurement%20Adequacy%20for%20Monitoring%20Data%20Center%20Oscillations.pdf; circulated via the NASPI webinar of 18 Mar 2026). Significance — consequence: If the channel is open with a quantified floor, two things change. Grid planners gain a non-cooperative way to attribute an observed oscillation to a datacentre rather than to another large load, which is the attribution step their present compliance limits (ATC 25 MW p-p, ERCOT 10 MW/5 s, LIPA 3.5 MW in 5–55 Hz) presuppose but do not provide. Compute governance gains a verification primitive that does not require the operator's cooperation — and simultaneously a confidentiality problem, since the same channel leaks job-level information to any party with PMU access, and, if ENF carries it, to the public. If the channel is open only for episode detection and carries no job-level information (the outcome N2's coherence statistic makes most plausible), then the reliability use stands and the governance use is refuted, which is itself the decision-relevant result: it removes an argument that grid monitoring could substitute for on-site attestation. If mitigation closes the channel, a class of proposed non-cooperative verification mechanisms is retired, and the value of mitigation hardware acquires a second, previously unpriced dimension — signature suppression — which operators and regulators would both have to reason about.
Significance — audience: (1) Reliability bodies and RCs already acting on this: NASPI/DOE-OE and the PNNL group (PNNL-39191), ERCOT large-load modelling, ESIG's 2026 large-loads work, and the utilities setting MW oscillation limits. A detection floor as a function of grid strength tells them where to site POW recorders and whether a PMU fleet can be relied on for compliance at all. (2) Compute-governance verification researchers — the arXiv:2604.04712 / arXiv:2506.15867 / N1 line — who currently enumerate on-site mechanisms; a negative answer prunes their menu, a positive one changes which treaty designs are feasible. (3) Datacentre operators and their counsel, for whom the answer is a disclosure question about load data and an argument for or against smoothing hardware beyond reliability compliance. (4) The ENF forensics community (UMD MAST line), for whom a new class of load-driven ENF content would be a new signal and a new interference source.
Significance — fecundity: whether mitigation hardware's own switching introduces a distinct marker (a signature of suppression); how coherence, and therefore detectability, degrades with fleet mixing and multi-tenancy (the scaling question behind P4); whether ENF-based public auditing of large-load behaviour is possible at all, and what asymmetry between restricted and public measurement classes implies for governance design; whether oscillation source-location methods, built for generators and inverter-based resources, transfer to loads; whether load-side signature sanitisation becomes a standard the way interconnection oscillation limits did; and whether the same approach distinguishes inference fleets from training fleets at facility scale, which N1 can do at device scale.
Significance — anchoring: arXiv:2508.14318 (synchronised multi-megawatt oscillations from collective communication); PNNL-39191's unresolved measurement adequacy above 2–5 Hz and its 4.8 MW/20 Hz missed-oscillation case; the heterogeneous and unexplained spread of utility oscillation limits (AESO ±160 kW vs ATC 25 MW); N2's simulation-only validation; N1's device-level channel and its evasion arms; the open interconnection-study problem of modelling large flexible loads (CIGRE C4-10564, ESIG 2026).

Reason for silence: Candidate mechanism — an instrument-and-institution mismatch with a short fuse, now partly expired. The band in question straddles an instrument boundary that was itself only documented in 2026: PMUs, the only widely deployed wide-area instrument, are validated to 2–5 Hz and alias above half their reporting rate, while the forcing of interest sits from sub-Hz to tens of Hz, so the data that would answer the question are mostly point-on-wave records that are not continuously archived. The institution that holds the data (utilities and reliability coordinators) is chartered for reliability compliance, is contractually and often legally barred from publishing customer-attributable load data, and has no reason to pose an inference question about its customer; the institution that wants the inference (compute governance) comes from the chip-telemetry and attestation tradition, has no PMU access, and had not, until 2026, acquired power-systems measurement literacy. Add recency: the phenomenon was published in Aug 2025, mitigation shipped across 2025-26, and the measurement-adequacy report is Apr 2026. Honest qualification, which Gate 4 should press: this silence story is weakening rather than holding, because the silence has partly ended — N2 (2026) crossed exactly this boundary from the power-systems side and N1 (2026) from the security side. That pattern fits "a question newly asked and barely answered" better than "un-asked", and it is the main reason the Gate 2 verdict is Low rather than Medium.
