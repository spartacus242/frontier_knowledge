Question (canonical) — V2, the one Gate 1 reformulation, scored as canonical:
In a jurisdiction holding a retrievable register of performance-based ("alternative solution" / "performance solution" / equivalency) fire-safety approvals that can be joined to individual buildings, and with a frame, covariate set and non-inferiority margin declared before any data pull: (A) for what fraction of buildings in the frame can design pathway be recovered, and how many code-relevant fire incidents accrue to the performance-based stratum, and (B) conditional on (A) clearing a declared power threshold, among buildings in which a fire actually occurred, does the performance-based stratum differ from otherwise-comparable prescriptively designed buildings — matched or weighted on occupancy class, height, construction era and suppression provision — in realized engineering outcomes, with flame and smoke spread beyond the compartment of origin as the primary endpoint and structural damage and casualties secondary?

Question (V1, as intaken; recorded per Gate 1):
Among buildings in which a fire has actually occurred, do those designed by performance-based fire engineering differ from otherwise-comparable prescriptively designed buildings in realized engineering outcomes — fire and smoke spread beyond the compartment of origin, and structural damage — once occupancy, height, era and suppression provision are matched?

Why the reformulation (well-posedness only, no change of subject): V1 leaves three things unfixed that decide whether any evidence could settle it — the frame (which jurisdiction's register supplies the exposure), the decision rule (a regime claiming *equivalent* safety is tested by a declared non-inferiority margin, not by a two-sided difference test, or V1 can be answered either way), and the fact that the feasibility limb is itself an answer rather than a precondition. V2 fixes the frame, makes the margin a pre-declaration requirement, and promotes recoverability to limb (A) so the question is answerable whichever way the records fall.

Alternative formulations:
- Has performance-based fire design ever been evaluated against prescriptive design on field outcomes, rather than on design-stage modelling and cost?
- Treating design pathway as the exposure and the fire incident as the outcome, what is the effect of performance-based design on compartmentation failure in real fires, and can the regime's claim of equivalent safety be tested rather than assumed?

Motivating gap:
Performance-based fire design has been permitted in many jurisdictions for roughly thirty years on an explicit claim of equivalent safety at lower cost. The question routinely asked in comparative-effectiveness research of any alternative regime — does it deliver equivalent outcomes in the field, in the population that actually experiences the event? — appears never to have been asked of it with incident data at building level. The literature's evidential culture is the verification calculation, the full-scale test and the life-cycle cost model; the one field-data study opened for this batch evaluates the prescriptive code against its own protection objectives from 900 firefighter-inspected incidents and never stratifies by design pathway, offering a performance-based code as a suggestion rather than a tested result. The Johns Hopkins CASE release flagged at intake as potentially fatal was opened in this Gate 2 and is a simulation plus life-cycle-cost study, not an incident-outcome comparison.

Presuppositions:
1. Design pathway (performance-based / alternative solution / equivalency approval vs deemed-to-satisfy) is recoverable for individual buildings at scale, from registers of alternative-solution approvals, building-consent records or consultancy project records. CONTESTED — the principal feasibility risk, and plausibly the reason for the silence. Gate 2 found jurisdictional guidance on alternative solutions (MBIE New Zealand) and Australian regulatory-process documents, but no evidence that any jurisdiction publishes or holds a building-linked, queryable register of performance solutions; nothing opened establishes that the join is possible at scale.
2. Fire incident records can be linked to the building at address or parcel level and carry outcome fields for flame and smoke spread extent and damage. PARTLY ESTABLISHED — NFIRS and several national equivalents record spread extent; the German study demonstrates per-incident, inspector-assessed smoke- and fire-spread grading is collectable at scale (900 incidents, 123 departments, ~25% of population).
3. Enough incidents occur in performance-based-designed buildings to estimate an effect. CONTESTED — such buildings are few, large, unusual and often heavily suppressed, so fire frequency per building is low; a power problem that may dominate. This is why limb (A) carries a power threshold rather than an assumption.
4. Confounding is addressable with recorded covariates. CONTESTED, and the deepest objection: performance-based design is chosen BECAUSE the building is atypical, so pathway is confounded with hazard by construction (confounding by indication). V2 requires a quantified sensitivity analysis (E-value or equivalent) for unmeasured confounding and a pre-declared covariate set; it does not claim identification.
5. A defensible estimand exists. FLAGGED and addressed in V2 — because the regime claims equivalent safety at lower cost, a non-inferiority margin on the primary engineering endpoint must be declared before any data pull; without it the question is answerable either way.
6. Outcome fields are not themselves recorded differently in performance-based buildings (differential measurement via more detailed post-incident investigation of large or unusual buildings). CONTESTED — a large engineered building is more likely to draw a fire-investigation team, so spread extent may be ascertained more finely in the exposed arm; needs a measurement-equivalence check or a negative-control endpoint.

What would count as an answer:
A matched or weighted comparison on a frame and non-inferiority margin declared before any data pull, with flame/smoke spread beyond the compartment of origin as the primary endpoint, casualties secondary, structural damage secondary, and a quantified sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding plus a measurement-equivalence check; OR, failing that, a documented demonstration that design pathway cannot be recovered for enough buildings to support any comparison — which is itself an answer with consequences, since it would establish that a thirty-year regulatory regime has been unauditable on outcomes by construction, and would tell code bodies (ICC, NFPA, ABCB, MBIE and the Nordic authorities that went furthest down this route) which record-keeping change would make it auditable.

Significance — consequence:
If the comparison can be run and non-inferiority holds within the declared margin, a thirty-year regime acquires its first field evidence for the equivalency claim it has been asserting from models — which matters most where the claim is contested after incidents, and would justify extending the pathway. If non-inferiority fails, the consequence is immediate and specific: a quantified excess in compartmentation failure, attached to a design pathway, in a population of buildings that are large, occupied and still being approved that way. If limb (A) fails — pathway cannot be recovered for enough buildings — that is not a null result but a governance finding: the regime has been unauditable on outcomes by construction, meaning no incident, inquiry or statistical series since the 1990s could ever have detected a systematic difference, and the fix is a recorded field rather than a research programme.

Significance — audience:
Code bodies and regulators with a live decision on pathway scope: ICC and NFPA in the US, ABCB in Australia (which maintains the performance-requirement/performance-solution architecture and its Fire Safety Verification Method), MBIE in New Zealand (which publishes alternative-solution guidance for Clause C), and the Nordic authorities that went furthest. They would act on limb (A) alone, by adding a design-pathway field to consent and incident records — cheap, and the only change that makes any later outcome audit possible. Fire services and incident-data custodians (NFIRS, national equivalents, the German departments behind P1) decide what gets recorded post-incident. SFPE and the research community that supplies verification methods would gain, or lose, a field anchor for the acceptance criteria P3 found missing. Property insurers and reinsurers price engineered buildings today without a pathway-stratified loss series.

Significance — fecundity:
Once pathway is an exposure that can be joined to incidents, a family of questions becomes askable that currently cannot be: does the design-stage margin of safety in a submitted verification predict realized spread; do particular alternative solutions (atrium smoke exhaust, extended travel distances, reduced fire-resistance ratings traded against suppression) carry distinct field signatures; do realized fires in engineered buildings fall inside the design fire scenarios that approved them, which is the empirical calibration fire safety engineering has never had; does as-built and in-service drift away from the approved performance basis show up in outcomes; and does the differential-investigation effect in presupposition 6 bias every existing spread statistic by building size.

Significance — anchoring:
Touches the equivalency assumption that licenses the whole performance-based regime; P3's finding that no unambiguous acceptance criteria were ever stated; P1's result that spread is controlled while smoke remains the threat, which makes smoke spread the discriminating endpoint rather than flame spread; the ICC/Meacham programme to put a risk basis under performance objectives; the post-Grenfell question of design-pathway accountability and traceability; and the general unresolved problem of ex-post evaluation of performance-based regulation, where the weathertightness precedent in New Zealand is the cautionary case.

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary boundary reinforced by a records gap and a liability incentive. Fire safety engineering's evidential culture is the verification calculation, the full-scale test and the life-cycle model — P2 and P3 are both that, three decades apart — so the field validates designs rather than cohorts, and nobody in it is trained to treat design pathway as an exposure. The two halves of the needed dataset sit on opposite sides of an institutional line: fire services hold the incidents and record no design-pathway field, while councils, certifiers and consultants hold the pathway in unlinked, often commercially confidential project files, with liability exposure that makes a retrospective outcome audit of one's own approvals actively unattractive. No actor owns the join, and because the regime never declared the quantity that would constitute equivalence (P3), there is no custodian of the question either — which is why P1 could inspect 900 fires, ask whether the code works, and never think to ask which code pathway the buildings were approved under.
