## Restatement (my own words, before reading the generator's framing back)

Ambient scribes now leave two artefacts for the same visit: a verbatim transcript of what was said, and a signed note written from it. Take visits where both are kept and where you can later establish, independently of that visit's own coding, what the patient turned out to have. Build predictors of that later diagnosis from the transcript and from the note, and ask whether the note-fed predictor does worse — in discrimination, calibration and decision-curve net benefit — with the inputs matched for length and summary form, with a human-transcribed subsample to separate speech-recognition loss from writing loss. Then ask the second question: is the note's shortfall bigger for some patients than others — interpreter users, people with limited English proficiency, dysarthric or cognitively impaired speakers, fast talkers, particular accent/first-language groups — and does the whole pattern also hold for human-written notes from encounters recorded before scribes existed.

In short: **does the record predict the patient's future worse than the conversation did, and is it worse for some patients than for others.**

### Fetches (4 of 4), all via publisher/repository/arXiv; no PMC or Europe PMC route attempted

1. `https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/55399367-9355-42a1-a844-96414b842a39` — **READ.** Khan, Naqi Ali, *Clinical Encounter Information Flow: Applications In Evaluating Medical Documentation Tools*, **2012 master's thesis** (not a recent dissertation). Tests whether "clinical information flow, defined as the transfer of concepts from patient to note, can be traced." Reference artefact = **standardized-patient scenario descriptions (PSDs)**, not transcripts. 256 unique PSD concepts, 122 shared with notes from both tools; "both computer-based and dictation-based tools are subject to clinical concept loss"; and — load-bearing below — "Dictation-based and computer-based notes also shared **103 concepts not found in the PSDs**." No downstream diagnosis or outcome endpoint; no patient-attribute stratification.
2. `https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.00019` — **READ.** 66,297 paired note sections (of 98,331; 32,034 dropped for empty final text) from outpatient clinics across two ambient vendors. Compares **AI draft vs clinician-finalized note only — transcripts are neither compared nor said to be retained.** Endpoint: stigmatizing-language prevalence/density. Explicit limitations: "did not evaluate how SL varies across patient social and demographic factors"; "Future work should trace SL across the end-to-end pipeline."
3. `https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31017` — **READ (abstract page).** Prior 2 is real: Fox, Markham, Lail, Karotsieris, "One note in three: a verified census of three deployed AI scribes, and the instrument that counted it", submitted 2026-08-31. "One note in three (31.3% [27.0, 35.6])", "565 notes from recorded UK primary-care and US ambulatory encounters", 142 consultations — all three figures confirmed as the record states them. No outcome prediction; breakdowns by failure type and setting (incl. telephone), **not by patient attributes**.
4. `https://arxiv.org/html/2502.10388v1` — **READ.** 30-day psychiatric readmission from discharge notes. SVM-BOW trained on **full notes vs LLM summaries** (Table 2 AUROC, as the page reports them): McLean 0.5770 vs 0.5853; MGH 0.5923 vs 0.6233; BWH 0.5420 vs 0.5518; FH 0.5750 vs 0.5646. Authors' own words: "These results, with AUROC values ranging from 0.54 to 0.59, indicate that the task is highly challenging". No full-note transformer comparator; no subgroup stratification; no spoken transcripts.

## The eight attacks

| # | Attack | Objection | Best response | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Triviality** | Answerable in a page: a note is a summary, so the transcript trivially contains more; Fox et al. have already published that 31.3% of notes carry a verified failure and that omissions dominate error type. The sign of the predictive gap is therefore foreordained and only its size is at issue — a measurement, not a question. | The sign is **not** determined, and two opened sources show why. (a) The note is not a subset of the encounter: Khan 2012 found "103 concepts not found in the PSDs" present in notes from both tools, and Weiner et al. report nearly half of notes containing content with no transcript counterpart — notes add examination findings, prior-record context and clinician inference. (b) Summarisation can *raise* downstream performance by shortening and denoising: in the only clinical outcome-anchored source-vs-summary comparison I found (arXiv:2502.10388, Table 2 as published), merged LLM summaries matched or exceeded full discharge notes at three of four sites and lost at the fourth. Scope caveat I must state: those are point estimates in a 0.54–0.59 AUROC band the authors themselves call "highly challenging", with no CIs on the page I read and a different regime (written note → LLM summary, psychiatric readmission), so they establish *direction is open*, not that summaries win. A question whose answer is not sign-determined, and whose subgroup-interaction limb nobody has even estimated, is not a one-pager. | **Resolved** |

## Recommended verdict

**Provisional** — nothing Unresolved, so not Rejected; but Medium novelty with a very close and very recent neighbour family (Fox et al. ×2), a false unlisted presupposition in the framing, and an unevidenced P1 cap the entry below Validated. Promotion conditions: (i) demonstrate one site with retained verbatim transcripts linkable to outcomes under governance — no such corpus was found in nine queries, and the field's largest paired ambient corpus lacks exactly that artefact; (ii) restate the estimand without the "lossy channel / sufficient statistic" framing, which Khan 2012 and Weiner et al. falsify; (iii) add a multiplicity and minimum-effect plan for the five-stratifier interaction; (iv) strike reason-for-silence mechanism #1 and substitute the endpoint-boundary mechanism, which I corroborated across five studies.
