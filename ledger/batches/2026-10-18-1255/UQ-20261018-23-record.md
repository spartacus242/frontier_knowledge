Domain(s): Medicine — clinical informatics; real-world-evidence methods; deployed clinical decision support. Source method: cross-field transplant (migration-fidelity auditing from digital preservation / archival science into medicine).

Question (canonical):
V1 (as intaken): When a health system replaces its electronic health record with another vendor's, what fraction of each class of pre-migration clinical data element survives into the new system as a queryable element, and what does the resulting patterned loss do to the look-back windows of real-world-evidence studies and to deployed risk models that silently assume continuity across the migration date?

V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, the one permitted — well-posedness only; this is the canonical form going forward):
For a pre-declared frame of K health-system EHR replacements, under an element-class taxonomy declared before any data pull (structured observation, free-text note, waveform, image metadata, order, medication-administration record, scanned/imported document, device or interface feed) and with "queryable" pre-defined as retrievable through the receiving system's own research query surface (its clinical data warehouse or OMOP/i2b2-style extract) WITHOUT recourse to a read-only legacy viewer or document store:
 (i) what is the per-class survival fraction — elements retrievable post-migration divided by elements existing in the legacy primary system as of that department's go-live date;
 (ii) in >= 2 systems, does an empirical before/after element-density audit, with the departmental go-live as breakpoint and including a negative-control class expected to be unaffected and a positive-control class known from the conversion specification to have been dropped, reproduce the specification-derived fractions;
 (iii) for one pre-declared published EHR-based analysis and one deployed risk model whose look-back windows cross that boundary, what is the sign and size of the induced change in feature availability and in realised calibration?

Alternative formulations:
- What does a hospital lose, as evidence, when it changes EHR vendor — and has anyone audited it?
- Treating an EHR replacement as an archival migration, what is its fidelity by data class, and how far back is the record actually usable afterwards?

Motivating gap:
Digital preservation treats a format or system migration as an epistemic event: significant properties are declared in advance, fidelity is audited after conversion, and losses are published. Medicine performs the same operation constantly — EHR replacements were near-universal across the 2015-2026 consolidation — and treats it as an IT and cost decision. A declared subset converts (demographics, problems, allergies, medications, some discrete labs); notes, waveforms, order and administration detail, device feeds and older results stay in a read-only legacy viewer or a scanned-document store. Meanwhile RWE studies claim decade-long look-back and deployed models carry 1-5 year features across those same dates, and no reporting checklist (STROBE/RECORD/RECORD-PE, TRIPOD+AI, CODE-EHR) requires a statement of migration history. Query 10 of the Gate 2 sweep confirmed the operational pattern in vendors' own conversion and archival literature (declared-subset conversion; legacy documents delivered as TIFF/scanned images with metadata); what no source produced was a measured per-class survival fraction or any analytic consequence.

Presuppositions (each marked):
1. CONTESTED and likely binding — conversion specifications are documented and retrievable (public-sector systems by information request, academic systems by cooperation, or reconstructable from inside a sample). If not, limb (i) must be done empirically via limb (ii)'s density design. Gate 2 evidence that this is genuinely contested: the only specification-level artefacts surfaced were a patient-facing "Legacy System Data Crosswalk" tip sheet and vendor service descriptions, not retrievable per-transition specifications.
2. ESTABLISHED by construction — "survival as a queryable element" is definable per class under the pre-declared taxonomy. V2 pins the definition to the research query surface, which is what makes the denominator and numerator countable.
3. CONTESTED, and part of the question — loss is patterned by element class rather than idiosyncratic per site. If idiosyncratic, the answer is a distribution rather than a table, which is still an answer.
4. CONTESTED, must be demonstrated on a named sample, not assumed — there exist published RWE analyses and deployed risk models whose windows actually cross migration dates.
5. DEFENSIBLE but must be stated — a read-only legacy archive is not equivalent to migrated data for research or decision support. Stated because a system may argue the data "is still there"; V2 answers it by definition rather than by assertion, and the archival literature (NIST long-term EHR preservation; vendor EMR-archival whitepapers) supplies the retention-versus-usability distinction.
6. CONTESTED but tractable — migration dates are retrievable and sharp enough to serve as a breakpoint; staggered departmental go-lives must be modelled as per-department breakpoints rather than one system date.
7. FLAGGED, newly explicit after Gate 2 — the legacy denominator is countable at all. It lives in a decommissioned or archived system, so limb (i) presupposes read access to the source-side counts, not only to the receiving system.

What would count as an answer:
Three separately reportable limbs, exactly as V2 states them: (i) a per-element-class survival table over >= K transitions whose conversion specifications can be obtained; (ii) an empirical before/after density audit inside >= 2 systems with go-live as breakpoint, with one negative control and one positive control; (iii) a demonstration limb re-running one pre-declared published EHR-based analysis and one deployed risk model across the boundary, reporting change in feature availability and in realised calibration. A real answer is a per-class survival table plus the sign and size of the induced analytic change. "Nothing is lost that matters" is a legitimate answer and would close the question; a large patterned loss implies a concrete deliverable — a migration-provenance statement as a required reporting item, and a model-gating rule at migration dates.

Significance — consequence:
If a large patterned loss exists: every EHR-based study whose look-back crosses a migration date has an unreported, class-specific truncation in its exposure and covariate ascertainment, and the direction is not random — the classes most often left behind (notes, administration detail, waveforms, device feeds, older results) are exactly the ones carrying the confounders and the outcome adjudication that structured codes do not. That converts an unmeasured nuisance into a correctable, declarable design feature, and it makes a migration-provenance statement a reporting item with the same standing as a data-source description. For deployed models the consequence is sharper and dated: a model with 1-5 year features silently loses inputs on a known day, which is a gating condition, not a monitoring curiosity — current practice would detect it, if at all, months later as unexplained calibration drift. If the answer is "nothing is lost that matters" — survival near 1 in the classes models and studies actually use — that also changes something: it retires a standing worry, licenses decade-long look-back across migrations explicitly, and tells health systems that legacy-archive spend buys retention compliance rather than analytic capability.
Significance — audience:
Four communities, each with a specific action. (1) RWE methodologists and regulators — FDA's RWD/RWE guidance program, ENCePP/ISPE checklists, the RECORD-PE and CODE-EHR authors — would add a migration-provenance item and a look-back validity bound. (2) OHDSI and clinical-data-warehouse teams, who already argue in their own forums about how to represent legacy and pre-EHR periods (query 3) and currently resolve it by local convention: a per-class survival table turns observation_period construction at a migration boundary from convention into a measured adjustment. (3) Model-governance and clinical-AI oversight committees, who would gain a dated gating rule rather than post-hoc drift detection. (4) Health-system CMIOs and their vendors, who would face an explicit, auditable statement of what conversion scope costs analytically — the quantity their current scope decisions trade against cost without ever pricing.
Significance — fecundity:
It opens: what conversion scope is analytically optimal (the first study that prices conversion scope against evidence loss); whether loss is predictable from vendor pair and contract type, which would let a study adjust without site cooperation; whether a read-only legacy archive can be made queryable enough to restore a look-back window, and at what cost; whether multi-site networks are silently heterogeneous in look-back depth by site migration history, which would recast between-site heterogeneity in distributed RWE studies; and whether the same audit applies to the other silent system boundaries — laboratory information system swaps, PACS migrations, device-integration changes, payer-system conversions.
Significance — anchoring:
It attaches to four live things rather than to nothing: the Sentinel/EHR-continuity "data discontinuity" literature (P4), which has a name for an adjacent mechanism and no name for this one; the temporal-dataset-shift and silent-model-failure literature, which has the detection machinery but calendar-time breakpoints; the OHDSI observation-period problem, where this is the missing measurement behind an unsettled convention; and NIST's long-term-EHR-preservation line plus the archival-science migration-fidelity tradition, which owns the METHOD but has only ever applied it to retention and admissibility. The JGIM 2023 editorial (P1) is the anchor inside medicine: it lists both "migration, syndication, and validity of data" and "the feasibility and conduct of research" as affected, which is the question named as an agenda and left unposed as a measurement.

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary boundary reinforced by an incentive and liability gap, with a measurement-access problem on top. (1) The migration is owned by IT, revenue cycle and vendor professional services, under contracts and NDAs, and is evaluated on go-live stability, billing continuity and cost; the loss is realised years later by researchers and model owners, who in most cases do not know the go-live dates and never see the conversion specification. The people holding the specification and the people who would feel the loss are in different reporting lines, and neither owns the question. (2) No reporting checklist requires a migration-provenance statement, so the fact has no slot in the paper where it would become visible and therefore questionable — the standard silence mechanism of "absent from the form, absent from the literature". (3) Incentive and exposure: a published per-class loss table is an institutional admission touching record-retention obligations, accreditation and medico-legal discovery, so the organisation best placed to measure it is the one least able to publish it, and the vendor whose conversion scope produced it has no reason to. (4) The denominator is in a decommissioned system: measuring survival requires source-side counts from a platform that has been contractually and often physically retired, which makes this one of the rare questions that gets harder to answer with time rather than easier. The archival profession, which owns the method and has no such exposure, has applied it to EHRs only for retention and admissibility (query 6) — the transplant has not been made because the two fields meet over the storage decision, not over the evidence.
