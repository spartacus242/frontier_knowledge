Question (canonical) — V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, the one permitted; V1 below):
For released sparse-MoE checkpoints run at inference under a DECLARED drop rule (R1 arrival-order overflow as in GShard/Switch; R2 lowest-gating-score drop as in Capacity-Aware Token Drop) at C >= 3 capacity factors on M >= 2 checkpoints, with strata fixed before any run (language/script by detector, document domain by corpus label, token frequency by decile of the tokenizer's own corpus counts, position by index bucket): (a) does the joint distribution of drop events over strata depart from the marginal input distribution over the same strata, tested by a pre-declared exchangeability/permutation test with a per-stratum drop-rate interval; and (b) in a PAIRED contrast against a capacity-free run on identical inputs with identical weights and identical decoding, is the per-stratum loss/accuracy delta a positive function of that stratum's measured drop rate — i.e. is there a slope, estimated against an evaluation-and-seed noise floor measured in the same harness, that lets an operator forecast the incidence of a chosen capacity factor before shipping it?

Question (canonical) — V1 (as intake, superseded for well-posedness):
In capacity-limited sparse mixture-of-experts models, is the set of tokens discarded at the expert-capacity limit a biased sample of the input distribution — concentrated by language/script, document domain, token frequency and position in sequence — and does a stratum's drop rate predict that stratum's loss/accuracy penalty relative to a capacity-free run on identical inputs?

Why V2: V1 left the drop rule unspecified, and the rule determines the answer — arrival-order overflow produces a position-concentrated incidence by construction, while score-priority dropping (the anchor paper's default) produces a gating-score-concentrated one; an answer to V1 is therefore not well defined without the rule as a declared factor. V2 also fixes the noise floor (without which a ~0.9%-magnitude aggregate cannot be resolved per stratum), the pre-declaration of strata, and the token-frequency proxy.

Alternative formulations:
- When an MoE discards tokens to meet an expert-capacity limit, who pays — is the reported aggregate degradation spread evenly, or is it a tax with an identifiable incidence?
- Is MoE token dropping distributionally neutral, or can the per-stratum accuracy penalty be read off the per-stratum drop rate?

BUT the intake claim "no hit on the identity or incidence of the dropped set" is FALSE in the candidate's own literature. OpenMoE (arXiv:2402.01739, ICML 2024) names the phenomenon "Drop-towards-the-End", measures it — "we visualize the ratio of tokens dropped at different position IDs" — and reports it is non-uniform across data sources: "only having a small proportion of tokens dropped, even for the Position ID after 1500" for pretraining data, while "for multi-lingual and instruction-following datasets, a large ratio of tokens is dropped", "more severe in instruction-tuning datasets". Limb (a) of the candidate was therefore asked and descriptively answered for the position and dataset-type strata in 2024. What OpenMoE does NOT have: token-frequency strata, per-language quantification (dataset-level only), any number in text (results live in Figure 12), any capacity-free paired reference, and any measured penalty — its causal claim is a conjecture, "We found that this probably be caused by the token drop of a long sequence."
The anomaly that remains live is narrower: nothing opened measures a per-stratum penalty against a capacity-free run, or any slope of penalty on drop rate. Dropless training (MegaBlocks, arXiv:2211.15841) removed drops from the training path, but capacity limits returned at serving under expert parallelism and capacity factors remain in shipped training stacks.

FLAGGED: for a checkpoint trained WITH a capacity factor, the capacity-free run is off its training distribution, so the contrast is not symmetric between the two populations.

6. This removes the "no bias" null's main support for the language stratum — which cuts against the candidate's novelty, not for it.
7. (NEW) The drop rule is a factor, not a nuisance — ESTABLISHED by the anchor's own Order/Score ablation.

What would count as an answer:
A measured joint distribution of drop events over pre-declared strata against the marginal input distribution over the same strata, with a pre-declared exchangeability/permutation test and per-stratum drop-rate intervals; plus a PAIRED per-stratum loss/accuracy delta (capacity-limited vs capacity-free, same inputs, weights and decoding) and a regression of that delta on the stratum's drop rate, crossed over M >= 2 released MoE checkpoints x C >= 3 capacity factors x R = 2 drop rules, with the slope compared against an evaluation-and-seed noise floor measured in the same harness. "No" = drop rates flat across strata within intervals and no slope distinguishable from the noise floor. "Yes" = a named stratum ordering plus a slope with a usable forecast interval.

Significance — consequence: If YES (ordered incidence plus a usable slope), the capacity factor stops being purely a throughput knob and becomes a distributional policy: an operator could forecast, before shipping, that a chosen capacity factor costs (say) non-Latin-script traffic several times the headline average, and single-average degradation claims of the "0.9%" form would be disqualified as release evidence for multilingual deployments. It would also make the drop rule (order vs score) a reportable configuration item, since the two rules would have different incidences. If NO (flat drop rates, no slope above the noise floor), the aggregate number is licensed, OpenMoE's dataset-level asymmetry is shown to be a position artefact rather than a language one, and a plausible fairness concern about MoE serving is retired on evidence rather than left as folklore — a cheap and genuinely useful negative.
Significance — audience: (i) MoE serving-engine maintainers choosing capacity-factor defaults under expert parallelism, who currently have no distributional acceptance test; (ii) pretraining teams still shipping capacity factors, for whom the position/instruction-data asymmetry OpenMoE found is a known bug with no measured cost; (iii) multilingual-evaluation groups (the Aya/Command and NLLB lineages) who already stratify quantization damage by script and would add capacity settings to the same audit; (iv) authors and reviewers of inference-efficiency papers, whose reporting convention is the single average this question tests.

Reason for silence: a disciplinary boundary crossed with an incentive gap, and the evidence for it is in the near-duplicate itself. The capacity factor is owned by systems engineers whose reporting unit is throughput and whose quality gate is one average against an accuracy budget; the people who stratify by language and script are evaluation researchers who do not instrument the router. OpenMoE sits exactly on that line: it measured the drop rate by position and noticed the multilingual/instruction asymmetry, but published it as the diagnosis of a defect to be engineered away, not as an incidence measurement, and left the penalty as a one-clause conjecture with no capacity-free arm — because its goal was a better MoE, not a distributional audit. The dropless turn then retired the question as a training concern in 2022, just before expert-parallel serving reintroduced capacity limits at inference, so no community inherited it as its own. This mechanism also explains the shape of the finding here: the question was half-asked by the people with the logs and never asked by the people with the strata.
