## 1. Restatement in my own words

Run an asthma trial that randomises patients to label doses of an inhaled steroid (say 100 / 250 / 500 / 1000 µg/d). For each patient, separately work out roughly how much drug actually reached their lungs, using their recorded breath profile through the inhaler plus a bench curve of "dose emitted at that flow" for that device, with puff counts to handle missed doses. Now fit the response curve twice: once with label dose on the x-axis, once with estimated delivered dose. Question: how much steeper is the second curve (max slope), and how far does its half-effect dose move? Then split the difference between three causes: (a) patients differ in what fraction of the label dose lands in the lung, (b) the drug genuinely runs out of effect (saturation), (c) patients differ in how much steroid helps them at all. Plus: check whether the delivery fraction is worse in sicker patients, because if it is, the whole correction changes sign.

Mechanically: the candidate's claim is that the famous flat ICS curve is partly an artefact of x-axis choice. The record's own Gate 1 correctly re-specifies the mechanism — randomised label dose makes this Berkson-type, so the flattening cannot be regression dilution; it must be convolution (smearing) of individually log-shifted sigmoids. I checked that correction and it is right: for a response curve f and a shift density p, the group curve is f*p and max|(f*p)'| = max|f'*p| ≤ max|f'| (Young's inequality, ‖f'*p‖∞ ≤ ‖f'‖∞‖p‖₁ with ‖p‖₁=1). So smearing can only broaden the transition, never narrow it. The candidate's mechanism is therefore *available*; the live issue is its **magnitude**, which is where I attacked.
