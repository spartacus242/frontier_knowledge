## 1. Restatement in my own words (before any attack)

Two things are on the table and I grade both, because Gate 1 spent its one permitted reformulation here.

**V1 (canonical, as submitted).** When a crystal and its own melt meet along a *curved* boundary, is the
free energy per unit area of that boundary different from its value at a flat boundary? Written as an
expansion in 1/R, is the leading coefficient — the solid–liquid Tolman length δ_sl — nonzero, which way
does it point, and how big is it compared with the thickness of the interface itself? The declared

**V2 (the repair).** For at least two real elemental solids melted as size-selected nanoparticles across
2–20 nm, what number (sign in a stated convention, magnitude in nm) comes out when you fit δ_sl to the
1/R² part of Tm(R), after the premelting-shell and substrate contributions to that same 1/R² term have
been pinned down separately by comparing freestanding with supported particles?

Plainly: *does curving a crystal–melt interface cost or save free energy, by how much, and can you read
that off the melting curve of small particles?*

## 4. The eight attacks

### Attack 1 — Triviality
**Objection.** V1's first two limbs are answerable in one sentence from a paper I read at full text, so
most of the submitted question needs no research at all. Cheng & Ceriotti's abstract, reconstructed from
11 consecutive segments: *"The curvature dependence of interfacial free energy, which is crucial in
quantitatively predicting nucleation kinetics and the stability of bubbles and droplets, can be described
in terms of the Tolman length δ. For solid-liquid interfaces, however, δ has never been computed directly
due to various theoretical and practical challenges. Here we present a general method that enables the
direct evaluation of the Tolman length from atomistic simulations of a solid-liquid planar interface in
out-of-equilibrium conditions. … For benchmark purposes, we computed δ for a model potential, and
compared the results to less rigorous indirect approaches."* And in the body: *"The negative value of δ
suggest that the equimolar dividing surface is closer to the bulk solid than the surface of tension, and
that the interfacial energy of a curved equimolar surface γ^V(R) has a positive curvature dependence."*
(sic, as served.) Opening words "The negative value of δ suggest" scope this to their computed δ; the
clause **ASSERTS** a negative δ and a positive curvature dependence of γ. So "does γ_sl depend on
curvature / is δ_sl nonzero / which way does it point" is answerable by citation.
**Best response.** The material-specific magnitude for real elemental metals, and any experimental number
at all, is not answerable in a page: no document I opened supplies one, and the 2024 review's
experimental-methods list (contact angle, nucleation rates, groove morphology, sessile-drop accelerometry
— my read of it was truncated and I say so) does not present a curvature-resolved experimental route.
**Status: Partial.** Two of V1's three limbs are page-answerable; V2's number is not.

### Attack 4 — Presupposition failure *(run as instructed: numbered clauses first, then each tested
against a document I opened; graded TRUE / FALSE / CONTESTED / UNVERIFIED)*

**Measurand exemption — DECLARED and respected.** The record's presupposition 2 (premelting/support
inseparability) is reported as the obstacle the experiment must overcome, so it is a measurand statement
and I do not grade it as presupposition failure. The exemption does **not** reach clauses 1, 3 or 4 below,
because V2 nowhere reports those as primary results — it flags premelting and support only.

**The clauses the question needs to be true before it can be asked at all**, read literally off V2:

1. **"the" solid-liquid Tolman length δ_sl of a material is a single scalar** — one number per
   elemental solid, so that "its sign and its magnitude in nm" has a referent for a faceted particle.
2. **δ_sl is well defined without naming a Gibbs dividing surface**, so that an experimentally fitted
   coefficient is the same object the method field calls δ.
3. **the 1/R² coefficient of Tm(R) decomposes into terms of which only premelting and support need
   separate constraint.**
4. **γ_sl is recoverable from nanoscale metal melting with enough determinacy that a sub-leading 1/R²
   coefficient is constrained at the pre-declared 0.02 nm level.**
5. Tm resolvable to ≤1 K and R to ≤0.2 nm across 2–20 nm on size-selected particles.
6. The curvature expansion of γ_sl converges at R ≥ ~2 nm.
7. A freestanding-versus-supported contrast is achievable for the same material and size distribution.

**Clause 1 — CONTESTED (sourced, full-text read).** Cheng & Ceriotti, body text: *"There is significant
anisotropy in δ among the three crystal orientations, and little temperature dependence."* Opening words
"There is significant anisotropy in δ" scope the claim to δ itself, not to γ; the clause **ASSERTS**
orientation dependence of δ. This is the **only direct computation of a solid–liquid Tolman length I
opened**, and in it δ is not a scalar but an orientation-indexed set. **My own reasoning, labelled:** a
melting experiment on a faceted particle returns one effective coefficient, which would be a
facet-weighted average over orientations whose weights are set by the particle's equilibrium shape — so
"δ_sl of tin, in nm" would be a shape-dependent effective number rather than a material constant. *The
search that would have grounded that averaging step — a paper deriving the effective curvature
coefficient of a Wulff-shaped particle from orientation-resolved δ — I did not run; the anisotropy itself
is sourced, the averaging consequence is my inference.* V2 does not repair this clause; the record states
that V2 "inherits" it.
**Clause 2 — CONTESTED (sourced, with my own inference flagged).** δ is *defined* in the paper I opened
as "the free energy difference between the surface of tension and the equimolar dividing surface", and the
same paper says *"Unsurprisingly, the value, temperature dependence, and anisotropy among the principal
orientations of γ vary considerably with the choice of the Gibbs dividing surface"* — note that this
sentence is about **γ, not δ**, and I do not stretch it. **My own reasoning:** a Tm(R) curve carries no
information about where either dividing surface sits, so an "experimentally fitted δ_sl" is the
coefficient of a chosen parameterisation of Tm(R) and is not demonstrably the same quantity. V1 and V2
pin no convention; the record concedes this.
**Clause 3 — CONTESTED (sourced, with a flagged regime gap).** Chang, Johnson, Sakai & Saka, in-situ TEM
melting of real Al, abstract: *"the depressed melting temperature is not controlled solely by the inverse
radius"*; Discussion: *"the melting process is governed by the surface area to molten volume ratio"*. Both
clauses **ASSERT** a departure from 1/R attributed to **geometry**, a term V2 does not flag at all. Their
Table 1 prints Ω(α) = **0.23 to 0.35 nm** in the relation ε = Ω/R — a size-dependence coefficient in
units of length, in the same decade as the δ values at issue, **explicitly not labelled a Tolman length**,
and I do not relabel it. **Regime gap, stated plainly:** their unit is a carbon-coated cone-shaped needle
of 20–30 nm point diameter, not a size-selected 2–20 nm sphere, so this sources the *existence* of an
unflagged geometry term in real-metal nanoscale melting and not its magnitude in the candidate's geometry.
**Clause 4 — CONTESTED (sourced; the arithmetic is mine).** The same paper reports **γ_sl = 55 mJ/m²**
(Discussion) and notes that about **180 mJ/m²** would fit the same data equally well under a different
assumption about the carbon coat's interface energies, and that earlier experimental estimates (Turnbull
1950; Falkenhagen & Hoffman 1957; Lai et al. 1998 — as cited there; I did not open those) were about
twice as large. **My own arithmetic, labelled:** 180/55 ≈ **3.3**, i.e. within one experiment the
*leading* 1/R coefficient is indeterminate by a factor of roughly three depending on an assumption about
the support. Carry that as an order of magnitude on their stated alternatives, not as a quoted
uncertainty, since the paper prints no error bar I read. **My own reasoning:** a 1/R² sub-coefficient
fitted on top of a leading term that loose is not constrained at the candidate's own 0.02 nm falsification
threshold.
**Clauses 5, 6, 7 — UNVERIFIED.** I opened nothing bearing on instrument resolution, on convergence of
the expansion at 2 nm, or on achieving the freestanding/supported contrast. These kill nothing.

**The leg this verdict stands on if the others fall: clause 1.** Without a scalar δ_sl the question "what
is δ_sl, in nm, for at least two elemental solids" has no object to be about, and the contesting sentence
is in the only paper that has ever directly computed the quantity, read by me at full text. Clauses 3 and
4 are the second and third legs and rest on a different document in a neighbouring geometry; clause 2 is
part source, part my inference.
**Best response available to the candidate.** Report δ_sl orientation-resolved, or report an effective
scalar with a dividing-surface convention pinned and the facet distribution measured, and convert the
degeneracy into a reported quantity the way the record converts premelting/support. That response is
plausible and the record does not take it — V2 asks for "the" δ_sl with no convention and no facet term.
**Status: Unresolved.**
