## Restatement (my words, not the generator's)

The candidate wants to read each discretionary inpatient lab order as if the ordering doctor had written down a probability that the patient will crash (RRT call, unplanned ICU transfer or death) in the next 24-48 h, and then check whether that number is right. The mechanics are borrowed from Abaluck et al.: fit the ordering decision as "order iff risk exceeds my personal threshold", recover each clinician's threshold from the realised event rate of the least-likely-to-be-ordered patients who were nonetheless ordered, then report calibration intercept/slope of the implied forecast against realised deterioration, overall and by night/day, staffing, service, age, language, race, weekday. A protocol-mandated-order arm is supposed to show no calibration as a negative control. Three advertised outcomes: signed miscalibration by stratum, calibration (so "leakage" features are actually human information), or non-estimability.

Stripped of framing, the question is: **does the realised near-term deterioration rate of ordered ward patients, at and above the ordering margin, line up with a probability one can impute to the clinician from the fact of ordering?**

## Pages opened (4 fetches)

1. **Djulbegovic B, Elqayam S, Reljic T, et al. "How do physicians decide to treat: an empirical evaluation of the threshold model." BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2014;14:47** — https://bmcmedinformdecismak.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1472-6947-14-47 (publisher route, opened first call; record's recalled citation verified). Web survey of **41 physicians**, **six hypothetical vignettes** (3 PE, 3 AML) varying the benefit/harm ratio; outcome = proportion who would treat. Abstract (as served): "We provide the first empirical evidence that physicians' decision-making can be explained by the threshold model." Dual-processing variant fit best, sample "too small" for conclusive support. **Stated hypothetical decisions, not observed orders.** Consequence: the record's P2 fear ("threshold model already inverted on observed orders") is NOT realised; but neither does the paper establish that the threshold model describes real-world ordering — it establishes it for 41 doctors' vignette answers.
2. **Alur R, Laine L, Li DK, Raghavan M, Shah D, Shung D. "Auditing for Human Expertise." NeurIPS 2023** — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01646. 3,617 acute-GI-bleed ED presentations, three hospitals, 2014-2018. "we interpret the emergency room physician's decision to admit or discharge a patient as a prediction that one of these adverse outcomes will occur" (urgent haemostatic intervention, 30-day death, 30-day readmission). Tests whether the decision carries information beyond Glasgow-Blatchford features (conditional-independence test); **does not measure calibration**; **no lab-ordering signal**. Finding: "strong evidence that physicians are incorporating additional information which is not captured in the construction of the GBS." So the conceptual move the candidate presents as its content — a binary clinical decision read as a prediction and tested against realised outcomes — is already published, in ML/CS, for an ED decision. It is not the candidate's design (no threshold recovery, no calibration, no ward, no 24-48 h deterioration), but it is a close conceptual neighbour the record did not find.
3. **Alur R, Raghavan M, Shah D. "Human Expertise in Algorithmic Prediction." arXiv 2402.00793 (NeurIPS 2024)** — https://arxiv.org/html/2402.00793v3. Applications are chest-X-ray reading and a non-clinical task; no test-ordering, no inpatient deterioration. **Relevant for P3**: as served by the summariser, the paper's stated scope "excludes outcomes that human decisions causally affect" with the justification that a radiologist's read "does not affect the contemporaneous presence or absence of a disease." That is the same group that built the decision-as-prediction bridge deliberately steering away from exactly the outcome class the candidate chose (deterioration after an order that triggers treatment). Summariser paraphrase; exact quoted fragment only the latter.
4. **Churpek MM et al. "Causes, Diagnostic Testing, and Treatments Related to Clinical Deterioration Events among High-Risk Ward Patients." medRxiv 2024.02.05.24301960** — https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.02.05.24301960v1.full.pdf. 4,000 eCART-flagged ward admissions, four Midwest systems; descriptive frequencies of tests ordered *after* a deterioration flag ("complete blood counts in 47% of events"). ## Recommended verdict

**Rejected** — Unresolved on attack 4 (and 8). Grounds: V1(i) calibrates a decision against the consequences of that same decision, with two of three outcome components being further decisions by the same team; the record concedes identification fails, Alur et al. 2024 (opened) scope such outcomes out explicitly, and the identified repair (events among the unordered margin against a clinical threshold) is N2's design moved to the ward. Djulbegovic (opened, publisher route) does NOT kill P2 — it supports the threshold model on vignettes only — so this is not a P2 rejection. Novelty: no renamed prior found in 9 Disguise queries; the conceptual bridge (decision read as a prediction, tested against outcomes) is already published (Alur 2023), so the record's Medium should read Medium-Low. Routing if rejected as already-asked: not applicable — rejected on presupposition, not duplication; the surviving discrimination limb belongs with UQ-20261017-39.

Single strongest killing point: the marginal ordered patient's realised deterioration rate is downstream of the order (treatment) and its outcome components (RRT call, ICU transfer) are themselves clinician decisions, so the "calibration" of the revealed forecast is unidentified and partly circular; the record concedes this and the only identified repair is N2's design.
