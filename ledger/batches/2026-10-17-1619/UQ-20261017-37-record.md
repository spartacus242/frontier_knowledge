Question (canonical):
V1 (as intake): In pragmatic cluster-randomised and stepped-wedge trials run inside health systems, what fraction of randomised clusters share treating clinicians with clusters in the opposite arm or period, and how much does that clinician-level overlap move the published intervention effect and its confidence interval?
V2 (Gate 1 reformulation, well-posedness only — the version scored): Restricted to clinician-mediated interventions (training, decision support, ordering/prescribing behaviour) and defining overlap CONTEMPORANEOUSLY — a clinician who, in the same calendar window, delivers care in two clusters that are in different treatment states — (a) what is the distribution across a pre-declared frame of health-system cluster-randomised and stepped-wedge trials of (i) the share of clusters with at least one such clinician and (ii) the share of patient-contacts delivered by such clinicians, measured from trial rosters or payer/EHR provider identifiers rather than from the trial report's prose; and (b) in trials whose provider identifiers are retrievable, how far does re-analysis under a crossed clinician random effect or a bipartite-interference-aware estimator move the point estimate and interval against the published analysis?

Alternative formulations:
- Is the cluster the right randomisation unit when the agents who deliver the intervention practise in several clusters at once?
- Measured on real trials rather than assumed away as "contamination", how large is the bias from clinicians who work on both sides of a cluster randomisation?

Motivating gap (as intake, now partly falsified by Gate 2):
Cluster and stepped-wedge designs are justified by between-cluster independence; standard analyses model correlation only within clusters. Shared staff were believed to appear in these papers only as a listed limitation and "essentially never as a measured quantity", while clinician practice is demonstrably multi-site and statistics now has estimators for bipartite delivery (treatment assigned to units, delivered through shared agents). GATE 2 CORRECTION: the phenomenon is in fact named ("therapist contamination") and has been counted twice in the candidate's own literature — over 234 trial articles (Magill et al. 2019) and over 100 trial protocols (Jacobsen & Wood 2020) — and the bias limb has one published, indeterminate answer at the allocation-level-comparison resolution. What remains unmeasured is the instrument and the denominator (provider identifiers rather than text; clusters and patient-contacts rather than articles), the frame (health-system pragmatic/stepped-wedge rather than mental-health complex interventions), and the within-trial re-analysis.

Presuppositions:
1. Clinician-to-cluster affiliation during the trial period is recoverable from trial rosters or administrative affiliation data. CONTESTED, and Gate 2 weakened it further: Jacobsen & Wood (2020) found only 57% of 100 psychological-therapy trial protocols clearly described WHO delivered the intervention, so for a literature-assembled frame the quantity is not recoverable at all; it is recoverable only inside trials, by their own teams, from site HR rosters or payer/EHR provider files.
2. Intervention exposure can travel with a clinician between clusters — plausible for training/decision-support/ordering interventions, doubtful for ward-level process or equipment change. CONTESTED; V2 restricts the question to clinician-mediated interventions to repair this.
3. Published effect estimates were computed under assumptions excluding cross-cluster agent sharing. ESTABLISHED for the standard cluster/stepped-wedge analysis (cluster or cluster-period random effect only).
4. At least some trials permit re-analysis under crossed clinician random effects or an interference-aware estimator. CONTESTED on two counts: data access, and identifiability of agent-mediated interference from a single realised cluster-level assignment (a crossed random effect is estimable; a causal interference contrast generally is not without an exposure mapping assumed rather than observed).
5. Overlap biases rather than merely inflating variance. CONTESTED, and now with a published prior pointing the other way: Magill et al. (2019) report "little evidence for a relationship between cluster randomisation to avoid contamination and size of treatment effect estimate".
6. NEW, flagged at Gate 1: in a stepped-wedge trial the same clinicians serve their own cluster before and after its step by construction, so V1's "or period" limb is ~100% by design and is the already-named quantity CARRY-OVER, which the stepped-wedge methodology literature does treat. V2 drops it.

What would count as an answer:
A measured overlap distribution — share of clusters with any contemporaneous cross-state clinician, and share of patient-contacts delivered by them — over a pre-declared frame of health-system cluster and stepped-wedge trials of clinician-mediated interventions, computed from provider identifiers; plus at least one re-analysis of a trial holding those identifiers under a crossed-clinician or interference-aware model, reporting the change in point estimate and interval against the published analysis. Overlap near zero answers it in the negative and retires the listed limitation for that frame. Overlap common but re-analysis moving nothing answers the bias limb in the negative, which is a different and also useful answer. NOTE: the frame-level limb is executable only by trialists or a consortium with site-level provider data, not by an outside reviewer — this is what caps well-posedness below 3.

Significance — consequence:
If overlap is common and re-analysis moves estimates: the cluster is the wrong randomisation unit for clinician-mediated interventions, CONSORT's cluster extension and the SW-CRT reporting conventions need a provider-affiliation item, crossed-clinician sensitivity analysis becomes an expected row in the analysis plan, and a slice of the implementation-science effect literature is biased by an amount nobody has priced. If overlap is common but re-analysis moves nothing (the direction Magill et al.'s weaker test already points), a routinely listed limitation is retired with a number instead of a caveat and reviewers stop demanding contamination narratives. If overlap is near zero in the frame, the same, more cheaply.

Significance — audience:
Stepped-wedge and cluster-trial methodologists (the Hemming/Taljaard/Kasza group), the NIH Pragmatic Trials Collaboratory and comparable embedded-trial programmes, CONSORT cluster-extension and SPIRIT authors, trial statisticians at CTUs writing SAPs for health-system trials, and the bipartite-interference statisticians who currently lack a health application. Concretely: a SAP gains a pre-specified crossed-provider sensitivity model; a reporting guideline gains an item; an ethics/feasibility review gains a reason to collect provider identifiers prospectively.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens: what estimand is even defined when delivery agents are shared (the bipartite analogue of the informative-cluster-size estimand debate)? Should clinician-intervention combinations be randomised instead of clusters, extending Walwyn et al. 2026 from individual to cluster designs? Does provider overlap explain part of the known attenuation of pragmatic-trial effects relative to efficacy trials? What is the design-efficiency cost of restricting a trial to non-overlapping clinicians?

Significance — anchoring:
The contamination literature's own unresolved bias limb (P1's negative); carry-over and time-varying treatment effects in stepped-wedge designs; identifiability of interference effects from a single cluster-level assignment; informative cluster size and the estimand debate in CRTs; the health-workforce fact of multi-site practice (P3).

Reason for silence:
Not "no one thought of it" — the concept is named and twice counted. The silence is specifically on the MEASURED version, and the mechanism is a measurement-access gap crossed with a disciplinary boundary: realised clinician-to-cluster affiliation lives in site HR rosters and payer/EHR provider files that trial reports never publish (P2 found 57% of protocols do not even name who delivered the intervention), so the reviewers who own the question can only read prose, while the trialists who hold the identifiers have no incentive to quantify a limitation that can only weaken their own estimate; and the estimator family that would use the quantity (bipartite interference) matured inside platform experimentation, which has no route to health-system trial microdata. That is why the question exists in qualitative form in medicine and in quantitative form in statistics, and in measured form nowhere.
