Question (canonical) — TIGHTENED AT GATE 1 (reformulation 1 of 1, well-posedness only):
For a pre-declared set of automated phenotyping pipelines (>=2 pose estimators, e.g. DeepLabCut and SLEAP; >=2 behaviour classifiers, e.g. a supervised ethogram classifier and an unsupervised MoSeq-class model) each applied to >=3 manipulations that visibly change the animal's image (sedation/ataxia, diet-induced obesity, peripheral nerve or spinal injury, a coat-colour- or body-size-divergent mutant line), with per-group ground truth from blinded, multi-rater human labelling at pre-registered per-group label counts and restricted to behaviour categories with documented inter-rater reliability: (a) does per-group sensitivity and specificity (for discrete behaviour calls) or the per-keypoint error distribution (for pose) differ between treated and control animals under a pre-specified test of equality; and (b) where it does, how large is the bias in the pipeline's effect estimate after standard differential-misclassification correction, expressed relative to that estimate's published confidence interval and to the assay's typical effect size?

Question (original intake version, recorded per Gate 1):
In automated animal behaviour phenotyping, is pose and behaviour classification error independent of experimental group, and how large is the differential-measurement-error bias it imposes on reported effect sizes?

Alternative formulations:
1. Does the accuracy of an automated behaviour classifier depend on the treatment the animal received, and if so, how much of a published behavioural effect is instrument error rather than behaviour?
2. What is the group-stratified error of automated phenotyping pipelines, and what bias does the field's implicit assumption of non-differential error impose on its effect estimates?

Motivating gap:
The settled claim inverted is that learned pose/behaviour pipelines (DeepLabCut, SLEAP, MoSeq-class models, commercial home-cage systems) made behavioural measurement objective, so a pipeline's validated accuracy transfers to the experiment it is used in. The presupposition inside that claim is that classification error is NON-DIFFERENTIAL — equal in treated and control animals. But the instrument is a learned function of the animal's image, and the manipulations under study routinely change the image: sedation and ataxia change posture, obesity and age change silhouette, injury changes gait, mutant lines can differ in coat colour or size. Group-dependent error is then indistinguishable in the output from a real behavioural effect and invisible in the pooled accuracy figure (overall keypoint pixel error, or per-behaviour F1 on a test set drawn across groups) that this field reports. Gate 2 confirmed the structural claim from a source opened: the field's own systematic review of 67 rodent pose-estimation studies reports no group-stratified accuracy anywhere and flags omitted keypoint accuracy as reporting bias.

Presuppositions:
1. Blinded human labels can serve as a reference standard for per-group error. [CONTESTED — human labellers are not group-blind unless deliberately blinded, and inter-rater reliability caps any error estimate; multi-rater blinded labelling is required by the design, not assumed. Note that the nearest verified analogue, Zhou et al. 2021, uses single-operator manual measurement as "ground truth" and concedes that no measurement is error-free, so this presupposition is the design's weakest joint and is handled by multi-rater labelling plus an IRR ceiling reported alongside every per-group estimate.]
2. Group-stratified ground truth is obtainable — enough frames per group can be labelled to estimate per-group sensitivity and specificity with usable precision. [Established and feasible; a power statement is required, and the tightened formulation makes per-group label counts pre-registered.]
3. Published studies do not already report group-stratified classifier error. [SUPPORTED, not asserted: Bhola & Yoo 2025, a systematic review of 67 rodent marker-less pose-estimation studies, 2016-2025, reports no stratified accuracy and notes that "some behavior classification studies omitted keypoint detection accuracy, raising concerns of reporting bias" and that "the reported performance values in their studies are not directly comparable". Residual risk: per-condition breakdowns buried in individual tool papers' supplementary tables would not surface in a review-level or query-level sweep. This is a limb of the question, and the corpus audit remains part of the work.]
4. Given per-group sensitivity and specificity, the induced bias in the effect estimate can be computed by standard measurement-error correction. [Established conditional on a reference standard. Independently corroborated by the method-owning field: Battaglia et al. 2025 supply the bias correction for ML-generated variables, and state that relaxing their orthogonality condition to allow error correlated with a covariate is "straightforward to relax" though not developed there.]
5. Training on pooled data from all groups reduces but does not guarantee equal per-group error. [CONTESTED — this is the field's informal defence; it is an empirical claim and part of what the question tests.]
6. The behaviours scored are defined well enough that a reference standard exists at all — the construct is not so soft that "error" is undefined. [CONTESTED for some ethogram categories; the tightened frame restricts to categories with documented inter-rater reliability.]

What would count as an answer:
Per-group sensitivity and specificity (discrete behaviour calls) or per-keypoint error distributions (pose) for the pre-declared pipelines and manipulations, estimated on group-stratified, blinded, multi-rater human labels; a formal pre-specified test of equality across groups; and, where error is differential, a measurement-error-corrected effect estimate placed against the published estimate and against the field's typical effect size for that assay. A null answer is informative: per-group error differences bounded below the level that would move effect estimates by more than their reported confidence intervals would justify the field's current pooled-validation practice. Reported with the inter-rater-reliability ceiling attached to every per-group figure.

Novelty — how this differs from each:
vs P1 (Zhou 2021, the closest prior by structure, and the reason this is not graded High): identical logical move — automated-instrument error that depends on the biological grouping, propagated into the estimate the field reports — but every one of the four attributes differs. Unit: plant genotype vs individual animal under an experimental manipulation. Endpoint: continuous morphological trait error vs per-group sensitivity/specificity of a discrete behaviour call and per-keypoint error distribution. Reference: one operator's manual measurement vs blinded multi-rater labelling with an IRR ceiling. Estimand: allelic effect / TAS set in an observational GWAS vs a treatment-effect estimate in a randomised intervention, corrected by differential-misclassification formulae. The grouping in P1 is a genetically controlled morphology class, not an assigned treatment, so P1 cannot answer the candidate's limb (a) for any manipulation; and it says nothing about behaviour classification, where the error is a misclassification rate rather than a continuous bias. P1's value is as proof that the mechanism is real and can be large enough to change signs — it raises the prior that the answer is "yes, differential" and lowers the novelty of the IDEA, while leaving the animal-behaviour quantity unmeasured.
vs P2 (Bhola 2025): same field, same tools, corpus-level scope — and it is the paper whose absence of this analysis establishes presupposition 3. It describes what 67 studies did; it does not estimate any error, let alone a per-group error, and performed no meta-analysis. Its one adjacent remark is about experimenter blinding (a human-observer bias), which is precisely the concern the field believes automation retired; the candidate's claim is that automation relocates that bias into the classifier rather than removing it. P2 therefore supports the candidate rather than pre-empting it.
vs P3 (Mathis 2021): the nearest ML-side prior and the field's canonical domain-shift result. It measures the right kind of quantity (error under distribution shift, against human labels) but on the wrong partition: unseen individuals, unseen species, synthetic corruptions. None of those is an experimental group inside one experiment, which is the only partition that can confound a treatment effect; and the paper stops at keypoint error, never propagating it into a behavioural estimate or a bias correction. A pipeline could pass Horse-10 handsomely and still have treated-vs-control differential error.

Significance — consequence:
If yes (error is differential and the corrected estimate moves by more than the published CI): a defined slice of the automated-phenotyping literature carries an instrument-induced component of unknown size inside its headline effects, concentrated exactly where the field most wants automation — disease models, drug sedation screens, injury recovery, obesity and ageing — because those are the manipulations that change the animal's appearance. The practical consequence is a change in validation reporting: pooled keypoint error and pooled per-behaviour F1 stop being sufficient, and per-group sensitivity/specificity with a label budget balanced across groups becomes the minimum claim a pipeline can make about an experiment. P1 shows the stakes are not theoretical: in the plant analogue, manual-vs-automated GWAS shared no trait-associated SNPs. If no (per-group differences bounded below the level that would move estimates beyond their CIs): the field's current pooled-validation norm is licensed by evidence rather than by assumption, a live objection to automated phenotyping is retired in writing, and the bound itself becomes the number reviewers cite. Both branches are publishable and both change a practice, which is what distinguishes this from novel trivia.
Significance — audience:
(i) Behavioural-neuroscience and laboratory-animal-science methodologists, who already mandate blinding against human observer bias and have no analogous requirement for machine observers; they would add a stratified-validation item. (ii) The tool groups that publish the benchmarks (DeepLabCut, SLEAP, B-SOiD, keypoint-MoSeq, BehaviorDEPOT) — they control the validation template that hundreds of downstream papers copy, and a per-group breakdown is a small addition to an existing table. (iii) Commercial home-cage and gait vendors (Noldus, TSE, CatWalk-class systems), whose accuracy claims are pooled; the CatWalk validity literature found at Q9 validates readouts in injured animals without ever reporting per-group detection error, so the gap is concrete and addressable. (iv) Reporting-guideline and preclinical-rigour bodies (ARRIVE, NC3Rs, NIH rigour policy), the plausible route from finding to practice. (v) Statistical reviewers and meta-researchers, who currently treat an automated behavioural readout as error-free; Battaglia et al. give them the correction and, by their own Assumption 2(iii), nothing to plug into it.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens: (a) whether pooled training across groups actually equalises per-group error — presupposition 5, the field's defence, becomes a testable claim with a number; (b) whether differential error can be flagged without new labels, from internal signals (per-frame confidence, likelihood under a group-conditional model, group-predictability of residuals) — a cheap screening instrument the whole field could run retrospectively; (c) design questions: group-balanced label budgets, pre-registered per-group validation, how many labels per group buy a usable bound; (d) whether the same structure bites in human digital phenotyping and wearables, and in livestock/veterinary vision, where the manipulation also changes the image; (e) whether some documented cross-lab irreproducibility of behavioural phenotypes is instrument-differential rather than environmental; (f) on the theory side, it is a concrete empirical case for the relaxation Battaglia et al. call straightforward and leave undone.
Significance — anchoring:
Touches the preclinical reproducibility and blinding literature (the machine-observer analogue of a bias the field already regulates in humans); the long-standing lab-to-lab variability problem in rodent behaviour; the "pitfalls" strand of the pose-estimation literature (the DeepLabCut primer, Horse-10/Horse-C robustness) which has the error measurements but not the group partition; the plant-phenotyping GDMB result as a worked precedent in another organism; and the ML-generated-variable inference literature, whose central orthogonality assumption is exactly the presupposition under test.

Reason for silence:
Three mechanisms, none of them "no one thought of it". (1) Disciplinary boundary in the direction that matters: the concept lives in epidemiological measurement-error theory and in plant quantitative genetics, while the data live in behavioural neuroscience, and the traffic between them is thin. (2) Inherited validation culture — an instrument novelty effect. These pipelines were validated according to the norms of the field that built them, computer vision, where the standard object is pooled performance on a held-out test set. Clinical-test evaluation, by contrast, reports stratified sensitivity and specificity as a matter of course. The animal field adopted the benchmark norm with the tool, and the pooled number became the thing everyone reports because it is the thing the tool papers reported. (3) An incentive and resource gap: human labels are the field's scarcest input, so label budget flows to improving the model rather than to spending it symmetrically across groups to audit it; a per-group breakdown can only make a tool's headline accuracy look worse; and the manipulations that would make the error differential — ataxic, obese, injured, oddly coloured animals — are precisely the ones a tool paper leaves out of its training and test sets. Corroborating trace from an opened source: Bhola et al. 2025 report that some behaviour-classification studies omitted keypoint-detection accuracy altogether and that reported performance values across studies "are not directly comparable", i.e. the field does not yet reliably report the pooled number, let alone a stratified one.

Strongest remaining objection:
That the answer is a bounded and boring null for the endpoints that actually carry published claims. Most reported behavioural effects rest on coarse aggregates (total distance, time immobile, bout counts) computed from many frames, where independent per-frame error partly averages out, and these assays are powered for large manipulations; differential error could be statistically unmistakable and still move the effect estimate by far less than its confidence interval. The candidate's own null branch concedes that this outcome would vindicate current practice — which is informative but modest. Second, and the live novelty risk rather than a significance risk: per-condition accuracy breakdowns may already exist scattered in tool papers' supplementary tables and in the four unexamined near-hits listed above, in which case the question is partly asked and the contribution shrinks to aggregation plus the bias propagation.

Suggested first step toward an answer (pointer only — not taken):
Before commissioning any new labelling, re-analyse ground truth that already exists with a condition label attached: public benchmark and validation sets whose videos carry a group or condition tag (CalMS21-class behaviour-classification benchmarks, the DeepLabCut/SLEAP benchmark suites, published B-SOiD and keypoint-MoSeq validation sets, and the supplementary label sets of gait and home-cage validation studies). Stratify the existing human labels by condition and test equality of error. That establishes whether the effect exists at zero labelling cost, and only then is a pre-registered, blinded, multi-rater, group-balanced labelling study worth its price. In parallel, open the four unexamined near-hits named under residual novelty risk.
