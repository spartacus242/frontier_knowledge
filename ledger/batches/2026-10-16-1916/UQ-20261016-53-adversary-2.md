## Restatement (my own words, before engaging the record's framing)

Take the published papers that say "we changed X in our pretraining recipe or architecture and it helped." For each such headline claim, write down which knobs actually differed between the two runs being compared — not just X, but learning rate, schedule, batch size, token budget, sequence length, warmup, data mix, tokenizer, precision, sharding, parameter count, depth/width. Then ask, in the bookkeeping sense of industrial experimental design: given only the runs the paper actually did, could the effect of X have been separated from the effect of whatever else moved with it, or are the two columns the same column (or nearly so) so that no amount of analysis of those runs can tell them apart? Limb (i): what fraction of headline claims fail that estimability test, how many are simply undecidable because the paper did not say enough, and do two independent coders agree. Limb (ii): for the handful of cases where someone can still run the machines, add the missing runs so X and its travelling companion are no longer the same column, and see whether the reported improvement survives a seed-noise-sized tolerance band.

Why the question looks appealing: compute budgets force co-movement (raise sequence length, drop batch size to hold tokens/step; swap a block, adjust depth to hold parameter count), and that is the textbook setting where a design cannot separate effects.
