## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Commercial drug-information publishers each attach a severity label to every drug pair they list, and hospitals wire those labels directly into their prescribing software: the label decides which combinations stop a prescriber mid-order. Nobody has ever checked the labels against what happens to patients. The candidate proposes treating each publisher's severity scale as a diagnostic classifier and scoring it: take a few hundred pairs, deliberately including the ones the publishers argue about, estimate in linked health data how much extra serious harm (hospitalisation or death within 90 days) is caused by *being on both drugs rather than one of them*, and then ask three things — how much of the pair-to-pair variation in harm is explained by which severity box a pair sits in; whether the boxes rank pairs better than chance and whether rival publishers rank them the same way; and, in real alert logs, how many fewer interruptions would fire if the boxes were drawn from measured harm instead of editorial judgement, holding constant how much harm you still catch. Pairs where the data cannot support a causal estimate are reported as a named "cannot tell" group rather than quietly dropped.

In short: *is the thing that fires the alert correlated with anyone getting hurt, and what would it cost in alert volume to redraw it from outcomes?*
