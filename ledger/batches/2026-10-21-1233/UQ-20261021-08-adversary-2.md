## Restatement in my own words

Take a task engineers do every day — look at a broken part and say what broke it (fatigue? overload? stress-corrosion cracking?) — and measure it the way forensic fingerprint and firearms examiners were forced to measure their own work after 2009. Break specimens on purpose so you know the true mechanism, hand them to practising examiners who cannot see the answer or each other's answers, give them a closed list of mechanism labels plus an explicit "inconclusive", and randomise two kinds of side information: (a) who hired you / which side you are on, which has nothing to do with metallurgy, and (b) a service-history story that is true, false, or simply absent, which DOES legitimately bear on the metallurgy but whose truthfulness is the thing being varied. Then report three numbers with intervals — how often examiners name the wrong mechanism, how often they say inconclusive, and how much they agree with each other — plus how much each side-information arm moves those numbers against a no-context baseline.

Two things to keep straight, because they drive my grading. First, the thing being claimed as never asked is not "does context bias experts" (asked and answered one discipline over, repeatedly) but "has anyone ever measured accuracy and reproducibility of MECHANISM attribution in engineering failure analysis against ground truth built by construction". Second, the primary quantity is ambiguous between an absolute rate and a within-set contrast, and that ambiguity is where I attack hardest.

## Single strongest killing point (one leg, the one I would stake the verdict on if the others fell)

The absolute false-attribution rate is a property of the specimen set, not of the discipline — the designer who chooses how clean, how mixed-mode and how sequence-contaminated the specimens are chooses the number, so the headline figure the named audiences would actually cite ("the error rate of failure analysis") is not an estimand about examiners at all. Only the within-set contrasts survive: the context-arm difference and inter-examiner reproducibility. This leg needs no external source — it is structural — and it is corroborated in direction by the method field's own retreat from false positive/negative error rates to variance-decomposed "failure rates" in arXiv:2507.02240, which I opened. It does not kill the candidate, but it shrinks it: the question that survives is "does context shift attribution, and do examiners agree", not "what is the discipline's error rate".
