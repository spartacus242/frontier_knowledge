### 2b. What does it do to a Poisson deviance analysis? (sourced where I could; my own reasoning marked)

Harrison 2014, PeerJ 2:e616, full text — REGIME: simulated and ecological count data, Poisson GLMMs; not heap data; the statistical statements are generic. Cause: "'real overdispersion' can arise when there is clustering in the count data" / "meaning observations are not truly independent of one another"; also "can occur due to missing covariates, non-independent (aggregated) data" and zero-inflation. Consequence: "Overdispersion is a serious problem because it can bias both the means and standard errors of parameter estimates"; "For all scenarios, ignoring overdispersion lead to greatly reduced standard errors of parameter estimates"; "the standard errors for all estimates were smaller by up to a factor of 5 in the Naive models"; "ignoring overdispersion during model selection can result in the retention of overly complex models" / "and thus erroneous conclusions regarding which predictors significantly influence the outcome variable of interest"; "to minimize the risk of Type I error". Point estimates in its simulations: "Accurate estimates of the slope parameter were recovered from both the Naive and OLRE models"; "the Naive model recovered the correct parameter estimates irrespective of the degree of overdispersion". Diagnostic: "the residual deviance of a candidate model should be roughly equal to the residual degrees of freedom" / "The ratio of these two values is referred to as the dispersion parameter, and values >1 indicate overdispersion." Remedies named: negative binomial ("ν = μ + μ2/k"), observation-level random effects ("a random effect with a unique level for every data point"), zero-inflated Poisson; cautions: "OLRE are not an appropriate tool for modelling overdispersion when it is due to zero-inflation"; "OLRE should be used with caution when high levels of overdispersion are present". NOT mentioned: quasi-Poisson, GEE/robust standard errors, likelihood-ratio or F tests.

Forsberg, LibreTexts 17.3 (textbook page): "leads to underestimated standard errors, inflated test statistics, and invalid inference"; remedy = inflate "the standard errors by the square root of the dispersion factor" / "Maximum Quasi-Likelihood Estimation" / quasi-Poisson family in R / "The Negative Binomial family allows for both over- and under-dispersion in the model."; invariance: "Note that the coefficient estimates are the same as for the Poisson model." / "The difference is in the standard errors"; estimation: "the overdispersion measure equals the ratio of the residual deviance to the residual degrees of freedom". NOT on the page: F tests for nested models, clustering as a cause, sandwich errors, random effects.

MY OWN REASONING (standard GLM theory; the grounding sources I did not open are McCullagh & Nelder 1989, Generalized Linear Models, 2nd ed., Chs 4 and 9, and Wedderburn 1974, Biometrika 61:439–447; for cluster-robust variances Liang & Zeger 1986, Biometrika 73:13–22): under extra-Poisson variation with dispersion φ, the raw Poisson deviance difference between nested models is inflated by about φ, so comparing it to a nominal χ² makes the likelihood-ratio limb ANTI-CONSERVATIVE by that factor; the quasi-likelihood correction divides the deviance difference by φ̂ (Pearson χ²/df or deviance/df) and refers it to χ² or, with φ estimated, to an F distribution; the point estimates and hence the fitted log-rate surface and its second differences are unchanged. Clustering that is shared across age cells at one death instant is between-cell dependence that a within-cell φ does not capture; the standard remedies are a sandwich variance clustered by death instant (or period cell) or a random effect at that level — the latter, at fine period cells, is confounded with the period effect itself.

### 2c. Can the candidate's frame carry the remedy without changing the estimand? Cap or defeat?

- Quasi-Poisson or a cluster-robust (sandwich) variance: leaves the fitted surface, the curvature point estimates of limb (i), and the raw deviance reductions of limb (iii) numerically unchanged (sourced at textbook level: "the coefficient estimates are the same as for the Poisson model"; Harrison: naive estimates correct, SEs too small by up to 5x). It changes only limb (i)'s interval widths (x√φ̂ or sandwich) and limb (ii)'s calibration (scaled deviance / F in place of nominal χ²). Estimand untouched; inference re-specified in one line (variance structure and cluster unit — Veroy & Guyer's garbage cluster or the death instant — declared).
- Negative binomial or a frailty / random-effect model: changes the likelihood, so limb (iii)'s deviance numbers change (they are model-relative, as the entry already concedes) while the hazard surface being estimated does not.
- Verdict on the kind of defect, for the judge: this is a CAP, not a defeat of the deviance limb. The deviance reductions remain computable and unique as descriptive quantities under any ordering; the dispersion implied by the only printed cluster-size table is modest (about 1.1–1.9) for fourteen of fifteen benchmarks, and the one heavy-tailed program (javac) is a known, declared-in-print exception rather than a hidden one; and the clustering is partly the period signal the question is after, so it cannot be dismissed as pure nuisance. What the clustering DOES defeat is the scored wording's plain-Poisson confidence intervals and nominal-χ² LRT as written — a defect of the declared inference that the canonical text does not repair (Gate 1's reformulation is spent), consistent with the entry's 2/3 well-posedness. Independently of clustering, limb (ii) is near-certain to reject at 10^6–10^8 deaths — a sample-size point the adversaries made and label their own; I agree and it is unsourced here too.
