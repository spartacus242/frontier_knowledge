## Restatement (my own words, before attacking)

Inside one human body, if you sort every cell into octave-wide bins by its individual mass, each bin turns out to hold about the same total grams of cell material — equivalently, the number of cells per bin falls off as roughly one over cell mass. The candidate asks two things. (1) Is that flatness a property of animal bodies in general, or a quirk of how a human happens to be built? (2) Does the exponent of that within-body count-versus-size relation move systematically as you go from a mouse-sized animal to an elephant-sized one? V2 adds a counting rule for the awkward cases (erythrocytes, myofibres, adipocytes, neurons), a pre-declared spectrum-fitting method, a slope-equality test against -1 and against the human -0.97, a hold-out check that drops each body's two or three dominant tissues, and a check of whether equal-biomass bins are also equal-energy bins.

Note on the unit of analysis, which matters below: the object is a *within-one-body* distribution over size classes, not a cross-species comparison of mean cell size. A prior about mean cell size per cell type versus body mass is a different object and must not be allowed to stand in for it.

## My own algebra, labelled as mine
Given F1's method — total count per octave class regressed on that class's geometric mean mass — the two facts the candidate treats as separate are one fact. For class k, total biomass B_k = N_k × (arithmetic mean cell mass in class k), so log N_k = log B_k − log m_k exactly, up to a within-bin correction bounded by one octave (≤ log 2 in the bin's own units) from the arithmetic-versus-geometric mean. Hence "slope −0.97 (CI −1.09 to −0.87)" and "roughly equal biomass per logarithmic class" are the same measurement stated twice: the slope of log B_k on log m_k is +0.03 with the same CI half-width of ~0.11, carried through from Hatton's stated interval. The candidate's limbs (a) and (b) are therefore one quantity, not two, and a species whose slope is −0.97 cannot have a non-flat biomass spectrum. This does not kill the question; it shrinks it.
