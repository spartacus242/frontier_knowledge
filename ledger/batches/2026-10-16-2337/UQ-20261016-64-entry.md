Domain(s): Medicine — clinical documentation and diagnostic error; primary care and clinical informatics; health equity. Method-owning neighbours: summarization evaluation (NLP), clinical risk prediction, measurement-bias/fairness methodology, ASR evaluation.
Question (canonical): In a corpus of ambulatory encounters retaining both the verbatim transcript and the final signed note, with a pre-specified set of index diagnoses/outcomes established independently from the EHR at a pre-specified lag L (adjudication rule fixed in advance; the index encounter's own coding excluded), what is the difference in out-of-sample discrimination, calibration and decision-curve net benefit between a transcript-input predictor and a note-input predictor — under (a) length- and summary-matched input controls, (b) a human-transcribed reference subsample isolating ASR loss from documentation loss, and (c) pre-specified subgroup-by-artefact interactions (interpreter use, limited language proficiency, dysarthria/cognitive impairment, speech rate, accent/first-language group) — and does the difference persist on pre-ambient, human-written notes from recorded encounters?
Alternative formulations: (A2, lay) "Now that what was actually said in consultations is recorded at scale, which artefact predicts what turned out to be wrong with the patient better: the transcript or the note written from it?" · (Intake version) "How much clinically predictive information does the record discard relative to what was said, and is that gap distributed unevenly across patient groups?" (graded at 2/3 well-posedness; estimand, lag and predictor class unfixed) · (A1, dropped at Gate 1) "…content that no downstream reader, human or algorithmic, can recover" — quantifies over an unbounded reader class, not settleable.
Motivating gap: The instrument that first makes the spoken encounter auditable at scale is used almost only to ask whether the machine writes as well as the clinician (Foo et al., Aust J Gen Pract 2026;55(4):226–232 — PDQI-9-style rater scores, omissions as one Likert item, no outcome). Gate 2 narrowed the gap materially: the omission-against-transcript limb is now closed by Fox et al. (arXiv:2608.31016, 2608.31017). What remains open is outcome-anchoring of documentation loss and its stratification by patient attributes.
Presuppositions: P1 paired transcript–note corpora exist at research-usable scale under permissive governance — CONTESTED and LIMITING (Fox et al. recorded 142 consultations themselves; a 98,331-pair production ambient corpus, arXiv:2606.00019, holds drafts and finals but no transcripts; adversary 2 found no retained-transcript corpus linked to outcomes in 9 queries). P2 an independently established later outcome exists at lag L — CONTESTED on a ground the record does not raise (see attack 4). P3 predictive performance proxies clinically relevant information — CONTESTED (paralinguistic/style channel; vocal-biomarker work is live), so the content/style ablation is load-bearing. P4 the note, not the encounter, is what downstream decisions and note-trained models consume — plausible, partly checkable. P5 loss is non-random across patient attributes — the hypothesis, not an assumption; presupposes stratifiers are recorded, weak for accent/first language and interpreter use. P6 the comparison is not an artefact of input length or predictor capacity — requires the matched controls. P7 transcription is not the dominant error source — CONTESTED; requires the human-transcribed subsample. P8 clinicians/vendors/standard-setters would act — plausible. P9 (added by adversary 2, unlisted in the record) the note is a lossy compression of the transcript — FALSE as stated: Khan 2012 found 103 concepts in notes absent from the reference, Weiner et al. report nearly half of notes carrying content with no transcript counterpart. The graded estimand (a signed difference between two inputs) survives P9; the Gate 3 "sufficient statistic"/"quantified ceiling" framing does not.
What would count as an answer: On a paired corpus under a pre-registered protocol — (i) the signed predictive gap (discrimination, calibration, net benefit) for pre-specified later-established diagnoses, transcript vs note, with length/summary-matched controls, a human-transcribed subsample isolating ASR loss, and human adjudication of discordant cases establishing content rather than style; (ii) an inventory of recoverable content — largely pre-empted by Fox et al. 2608.31016, so replication plus outcome-anchoring, not discovery; (iii) the gap's interaction with pre-specified patient attributes; (iv) the same analysis on human-written notes from pre-ambient recorded encounters. A null is publishable in advance as a defence of documentation practice and of note-trained models.

Significance — consequence: If the gap is large, the note is not predictively sufficient for outcomes clinicians act on, with three attached decisions: transcript retention becomes a governance and medico-legal question; documentation targets shift from "faithful and readable" to a scorable retained-signal objective that does not move by a factor of eight when the review instruction changes (per P2); note-trained models acquire a measured rather than speculative input ceiling. If null: a currently unavailable defence of documentation practice, and the retention argument retires. If large and differential by patient group: documentation becomes an identified mechanism of inequity upstream of every model-fairness intervention in the field. Scope discipline required: a single-site, single-predictor-family result bounds nothing about other models, sites or tasks, and the "quantified ceiling for every note-trained model" claim overreaches (adversary 2, attack 6).
Significance — audience: Scribe purchasers and health-system informatics teams; documentation standard-setters and specialty bodies; clinical-AI developers training on notes; health-equity and language-access programmes; medico-legal reviewers and regulators. Evidenced rather than asserted: health systems and vendors already fund and publish paired-artefact audits of live scribes (Fox et al. on three deployed products; arXiv:2606.00019, 66,297 paired sections across two vendors), and deployers are reported to have built proprietary incomparable evaluation pipelines and to be asking for a shared benchmark (arXiv:2609.17355).
Significance — fecundity: What is a predictively sufficient note, and can a documentation target be optimized against retained signal rather than rater score?
