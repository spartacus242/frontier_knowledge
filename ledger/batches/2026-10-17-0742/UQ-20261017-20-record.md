Question (canonical, V1 as intake): What happens to the patient whose specimen is rejected: among laboratory orders rejected for pre-analytical reasons, what fraction never yields a usable result at all, what is the delay to the first usable result, and what downstream clinical consequences follow, estimated with a design that exploits the operator- and draw-route-driven component of rejection?

Question (V2, the one Gate 1 reformulation, for well-posedness only — frame, estimand and comparator made explicit): In a linked LIS–EHR frame at >=1 site with documented recollection policy, taking the pre-analytically rejected laboratory order as the exposed unit and a non-rejected order for the same test, same calendar window and same acuity stratum as the comparator: (i) what proportion of rejected orders has no usable result within pre-specified windows (6 h, 24 h) and never; (ii) what is the distribution of order-to-first-usable-result interval in exposed versus matched comparator orders; and (iii) what is the effect of rejection on pre-specified downstream endpoints (time to recognition of a critical result, escalation or ICU transfer, length of stay, number of additional venipunctures) under a declared identification strategy — phlebotomist identity, draw route, or shift-level haemolysis propensity as instrument — with the exclusion restriction tested by pre-specified falsification outcomes?
(Both versions recorded as required. V2 does not change the object; it only names the comparator and estimand that V1 left implicit. V2 is what Gate 2 was graded against.)

Alternative formulations:
- When the laboratory rejects a haemolysed, clotted or insufficient sample, how often is the test simply never repeated, and does anyone come to harm?
- Treating pre-analytical specimen rejection as an exposure rather than as a quality indicator, what is its causal effect on time-to-result, test abandonment and patient outcome?

Presuppositions:
1. Rejections are recorded with reason, order identifier and timestamp in the LIS and are linkable to the clinical record — ESTABLISHED (modern LIS; the priors found here were built from exactly such records).
2. Rejection has a substantial operator- and draw-route-driven component plausibly independent of prognosis given acuity, usable as an instrument — CONTESTED, and the central methodological threat: sicker patients have harder draws and more line-drawn samples, so the exclusion restriction must be argued and tested, not assumed. Unaffected by the Gate 2 finding (no prior tried this).
3. "Never re-collected" is distinguishable in the record from "deliberately abandoned because no longer clinically needed" — CONTESTED; requires an explicit coding rule validated against chart review. NOTE: the CAP Q-Probes prior resolved this pragmatically rather than validly, by counting abandonment as lab-did-not-request OR team-did-not-comply within an "arbitrary six-hour window" (its own word), so the presupposition remains live and is the sharpest surviving methodological point in the candidate.
4. There is a counterfactual usable result — rejection is a correct refusal whose cost is delay and abandonment, not a wrong result — ESTABLISHED as to the analytical point.
5. Downstream endpoints are ascertainable at the needed time granularity in linked records — ESTABLISHED.
6. Rejection is not already compensated by a reflex auto-recollection protocol making the delay trivial — CONTESTED and site-dependent; the frame must record recollection policy. Gate 2 evidence bears on this: published abandonment is 21% (CAP, 78 institutions) and 48.3% (single South African tertiary site), and published repeat delay ranges from a 65-min median to a 121-h mean, so the presupposition holds but with enormous between-site spread, which makes any single-site estimate of limb (iii) hard to generalise.

What would count as an answer: (i) the proportion of rejected orders with no usable result within pre-specified windows and never; (ii) the order-to-first-usable-result distribution against matched non-rejected orders of the same test and acuity; (iii) effect estimates on pre-specified downstream endpoints under a declared identification strategy, with the exclusion restriction tested by falsification outcomes. A null — rejected specimens are promptly re-collected and nothing downstream follows — is a clean answer and would strip the patient-safety justification from rejection-reduction programmes, leaving a cost and throughput argument. (Gate 1 passes: answer criteria are statable, falsifiable and pre-specifiable. But note that criteria (i) and (ii) have already been met by published work, which is a Gate 2 matter, not a Gate 1 one.)

Novelty — nearest prior formulations (3, with sources):

P1. CAP Q-Probes, "Clinical Consequences of Specimen Rejection" (CAP Quality Practices Committee; coauthors Donald S. Karcher MD and Christopher M. Lehman MD), 78 clinical laboratories — as reported in detail in "Specimen rejection: What price for patient care?", CAP TODAY, July 2012, https://www.captodayonline.com/Archives/0712/0712d_specimen_rejection.html (opened).
  - UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the rejected specimen, aggregated to the institution (Q-Probes benchmarking design).
  - ENDPOINT: abandonment — rejected specimen for which no corrected/relabelled specimen was requested or none was supplied; and specimen processing delay to the usable result. Stratified by stat/non-stat, specimen type (blood vs urine) and institutional bed size.
  - REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: none against non-rejected orders; the comparison is institution-to-institution (percentile benchmarking) and stratum-to-stratum within rejections.
  - ESTIMAND: descriptive cross-institutional proportions and medians. No patient outcome and no causal estimand.
  - NUMBERS IT PUBLISHES: 21% of rejected specimens abandoned; median delay 65 min overall, 90 min for non-stat, ~60 min for stat, 91 min at >450-bed institutions. Named-but-unquantified downstream harms: repeat venipuncture, haematoma, phlebotomy-associated anaemia, cutaneous nerve injury, repeat catheterisation for urine, and delayed clinical decisions.

P2. Jacobsz LA, Zemlin AE, Roos MJ, Erasmus RT. "Chemistry and haematology sample rejection and clinical impact in a tertiary laboratory in Cape Town." Clin Chem Lab Med 2011;49(12):2047-2050. DOI 10.1515/CCLM.2011.743. https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/CCLM.2011.743/pdf (publisher page opened and figures quoted) and https://scholar.sun.ac.za/handle/10019.1/20532 (opened).
  - UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the specimen (481 rejected of 32,910 received over 2 weeks, one tertiary laboratory), plus the patient file (n = 30, randomly selected) for the clinical-impact limb.
  - ENDPOINT: proportion of rejected samples repeated; time for a repeat sample to reach the laboratory; proportion of repeats returning critical-range results; proportion of reviewed files in which rejection "had an impact on patient care".
  - REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: none. No non-rejected comparison group; no acuity matching.
  - ESTIMAND: descriptive single-site proportions and one mean, plus an unblinded, undefined chart-review judgement of care impact.
  - NUMBERS IT PUBLISHES: rejection rate 1.46%; 51.7% repeated (so 48.3% not repeated — my arithmetic from their reported figure); mean 121 h (~5 days) for a repeat to reach the lab; 5.1% of repeats in the critical range; 40% of 30 files judged affected (~12 files — my arithmetic).

P3. Phelan MP, Hustey FM, Good DM, Reineks EZ. "Seeing Red: Blood Sample Hemolysis Is Associated with Prolonged Emergency Department Throughput." J Appl Lab Med 2020;5(4):732-737. DOI 10.1093/jalm/jfaa073. https://academic.oup.com/jalm/article/5/4/732/5865362 (opened).
  - UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the ED visit (11,228 visits with a core-lab potassium result, Jan–Mar 2014, one urban academic tertiary ED).
  - ENDPOINT: ED length of stay / arrival-to-disposition interval. NOT time to first usable result; NOT redraw occurrence (the authors state their data could not determine how often a haemolysed sample led to a redraw); NO clinical outcomes.
  - REFERENCE/COMPARATOR: visits whose potassium sample was not haemolysed — i.e. this prior DOES have the comparator the other two lack.
  - ESTIMAND: unadjusted mean difference in LOS, stratified by disposition and ESI level, two-tailed t-tests; the authors explicitly disclaim causality and name age, illness complexity and extent of testing as unmeasured confounders.
  - NUMBERS IT PUBLISHES: 349 vs 287 min (difference 62 min); discharged 429 vs 337; admitted 292 vs 264; medians 215 vs 184.

ROUTING: limb (iii) belongs in ledger/routed-open-problems.md as an open problem — "identified effect of pre-analytical specimen rejection on downstream clinical endpoints in a linked LIS-EHR frame" — with P1 and the CAP Today review as the sources showing it is open, and tractability limited mainly by data access rather than by method.

Significance — consequence: If limb (iii) is positive, rejection-reduction programmes acquire the measured patient-safety warrant that P1 and the PA advisory currently assert and do not have, and the quantity becomes a legitimate patient-safety indicator rather than a lab process metric. If null, the justification collapses to cost and throughput — a real deflation, since cost has separately been studied (the ISPOR 2012 abstract found at query 5) and would then be the whole case. But the consequence is weaker than the intake assumed: because P1 has already published 21% abandonment and a 65-min median delay, the decision-relevant uncertainty that remains is only about the final link from delay/abandonment to clinical events, not about whether delay and abandonment occur.
Significance — audience: laboratory quality programmes and accreditation bodies that set rejection-rate targets (CAP, IFCC/EFLM working groups on the extra-analytical phase), hospital patient-safety committees, and ED operations groups who already act on the delay finding (P3 is cited in exactly that setting). They would act by re-grounding or withdrawing the safety claim and by changing recollection policy — the between-site spread found here (21% vs 48.3% abandoned; 65 min vs 121 h) is itself an actionable target.
Significance — fecundity: opens the policy question of whether reflex auto-recollection standing orders are justified; opens the measurement question of how to validate a never-recollected code against intent; and opens whether haemolysis-tolerant reporting with an interpretive comment beats rejection on net patient outcome — a comparison nobody can currently make because the rejection arm's patient cost is unmeasured.
Significance — anchoring: touches the EFLM/IFCC extra-analytical quality-indicator programme (which uses rejection rate as a numerator without a patient-side denominator), the diagnostic-error literature on test follow-up failure (query 9 — which covers results that returned and were ignored, and has no cell for specimens that never produced a result), and iatrogenic/phlebotomy-associated anaemia (named by P1, unquantified).
Scores: Well-posedness 3/3 (V2 states frame, exposure, comparator, endpoints, identification strategy and a falsification test; a null is a clean answer) · Significance 2/3 (real audience and a genuine deflation available, but the two quantitative limbs are already published, so the marginal consequence is confined to limb (iii)).

Reason for silence: NOT a silence, which is itself the Gate 2 finding. For limbs (i) and (ii) the question was asked and answered (P1, P2) and the intake's silence was an artefact of vocabulary — the field files this under "specimen rejection" and "recollection", not under "what happens to the patient". For the residual limb (iii) the credible mechanism is a disciplinary-ownership and incentive split, not an absence of ideas: the exposure data sit in the LIS and are owned by laboratory medicine, which is accredited on rejection rate as a process indicator and so has no need to establish the downstream harm it is already funded to prevent; outcome ascertainment and instrumental-variable identification sit with clinical epidemiology and health-services research, which do not see the LIS rejection field. P1's own title shows the question was thought of in 2012 and stopped at the laboratory's data boundary — the hallmark of a boundary gap rather than an unasked question. A secondary incentive: the asserted harm has been rhetorically sufficient for rejection-reduction business cases, so measuring it carries downside risk for the programmes that would have to fund the measurement.
