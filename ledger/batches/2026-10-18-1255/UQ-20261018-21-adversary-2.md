## Restatement in my own words

Take a hospital ward patient. A doctor decides, without a protocol forcing it, to draw a lactate (or a troponin, a blood culture, a repeat CBC). The candidate proposes to read that decision as a *bet*: by ordering, the clinician is implicitly asserting that this patient's chance of crashing in the next day or two (rapid-response call, unplanned ICU transfer, death) is above some action threshold. The question is then a verification question of the kind weather forecasters ask of themselves: among the patients who only just got ordered — the ones the clinician was nearly indifferent about — does the *actual* rate of crashing match the probability the ordering decision implies? And if it does not, is the mismatch bigger at night, at low staffing, on some services, for older patients, for patients who do not speak English? Plus two scaffolds: recover the threshold clinician-by-clinician rather than assuming one number for everybody, and check the whole apparatus against protocol-mandated orders, where nothing should calibrate because no judgement went into them.

Said shortly: **is a doctor's ordering behaviour a probabilistic forecast, and is that forecast calibrated?** The record's own fallback, if the probability scale will not hold, is the weaker ordinal claim: does ordering more rank-order risk correctly?

## Spot-check of the record's cited priors

- **N1 (Abaluck et al., NBER w19956) — real, and the record's numbers are right.** Mean threshold 0.056, SD 0.054 after empirical-Bayes correction; 84% of doctors over-testing at their margin; ~1.9M ED evaluations, 20% Medicare Part B sample 2000–2009, >6,600 physicians with ≥7 chest CTs. Two quotations I rely on below, from the fetched PDF: "*physicians test if and only if the probability of a positive test q_id exceeds a physician-specific threshold τ_d*", and, of marginal patients, "*the observed probability of a positive test reveals the threshold at which doctors are willing to test*".
- **N3 (Romero-Brufau et al., JAMIA Open 2019;2(4):465–470) — real, and the record's characterisation is right.** 31,159 patient-shifts, 3,185 patients, 3,551 hospitalisations; 169 events (86 RRT, 76 ICU transfers, 7 codes); ICU transfer within 24 h; Worry Factor is a nurse-**stated** 0–4 rating; **no calibration plot, intercept/slope or observed-vs-expected anywhere**; baseline 0.15% ICU risk per patient-day rising to 5.8% at WF 4; ordering and monitoring behaviour not analysed (only whether a provider was called and came). One discrepancy worth flagging: the abstract gives AUROC 0.92 while Results give 0.964 (5-level) and 0.920 (3-level), without saying which the abstract reports.
- **Djulbegovic 2014, the paper the record told me to open first, does not do what the record feared and does not do what the candidate needs.** It is a **web survey of 41 physicians on six hypothetical vignettes** (PE and AML). Thresholds were **not** recovered from behaviour: T_EUT came from literature benefit/harm ratios, T_RG from the physician's own dual-visual-analogue-scale ratings, T_DP from both; observed vignette decisions were then checked for agreement. The authors' claim is "*We provide the first empirical evidence that physicians' decision-making can be explained by the threshold model*", and treatment rates moved as predicted (AML 66%→20% at high threshold, p<0.0001; PE 98%→39%, p<0.0001). **Two consequences.** (a) The record's P2 worry is partly relieved — the threshold model is at least directionally descriptive — so this unread neighbour was not hiding a kill. (b) But the model that fits *best* is dual-processing, not expected utility: DP vs EUT OR 1.75, p<0.001; DP vs regret OR 2.61, p=0.018; regret vs EUT OR 1.49, p≈0.056. **The cost-loss/EUT form the candidate's limb (i)(b) uses is the worst-fitting of the three descriptive models tested.** Scope limits I must state: n=41, hypothetical vignettes, *treatment* decisions in PE/AML rather than test ordering, no inpatients, no deterioration endpoint, and the authors themselves say the sample was too small for scenario-specific model support — so this constrains the candidate's presupposition weakly, and cannot be read as a general refutation of cost-loss thresholds.
