## Restatement (my own words)

Take patients who already carry a coded chronic-disease label in routine care. At some later point a result lands in the same record that, under an agreed rule, says the label is wrong (COPD label + post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC >= 0.7; hypothyroidism label + normal TSH some weeks after the last levothyroxine supply; coeliac label + negative serology plus negative biopsy while eating gluten). The question asks two things about what the RECORD then does, not about what is clinically true: (1) how fast and how often does a dated un-labelling event appear — problem-list deletion, "resolved"/inactivated status, or an exclusion code — in the 24 months after that first disconfirming result, expressed as a cause-specific hazard and cumulative incidence against matched person-time in labelled patients who never got such a result; and (2) what share of each group is still being prescribed the disease-specific drug at 12 and 24 months. Asthma and heart failure are secondary pairs, penicillin allergy is the positive control, and "no disease-specific prescription for 180 days" is a pre-declared fallback outcome where no removal event is emitted. So: is a diagnosis in a chart a falsifiable claim, measured as a time-to-event property of the chart.

## What Jones et al. 2024 actually establishes (the load-bearing new evidence)

Opened at https://bjgp.org/content/74/743/e364. Unit: patients in CPRD GOLD (English practices, 2010-2019) with at least one "resolved asthma" Read code, index date = first such code; 16,023 per cohort after matching. Comparators: 1:1 matched active-asthma patients (asthma code + >=1 asthma prescription, no resolved code) matched on practice, sex, age within 1 year; and a CPRD-generated non-asthma cohort matched on practice, sex, year of birth. Endpoint: hospital admissions and GP consultations for asthma/RTI. Estimand: adjusted incidence rate ratios from mixed-effects negative binomial regression.

Three facts from it bear directly on this candidate:
1. **Dated retraction events are retrievable at national scale in the candidate's own named data source.** A 16,023-patient cohort was built on the first "resolved asthma" code in CPRD GOLD, with index dating. P1 — the record's self-declared "single weakest point", and the one the Gate 2 informatics sweep said it could find no evidence for — is substantially rescued, by evidence the record does not have.
2. **The retraction event is already an object of study in the candidate's own field and database — with the arrow pointing the other way.** Jones treats resolution as the EXPOSURE and asks what happens afterwards. The candidate treats it as the OUTCOME and asks what brings it about. So the candidate is the unasked direction of an asked relation, not an unasked relation.
3. **The candidate's two declared endpoints are not independent and are probably ordered the opposite way round.** Via my fetch of the page, the Discussion states that resolution "required a period of >12 months without any asthma-related medications", and the Methods censor resolved-cohort follow-up at "the earliest date where an asthma medication was prescribed". A sensitivity analysis excluded 5,333 of the resolved cohort for having had asthma medication in the 12 months before index (my arithmetic: 5,333/16,023 = 33.3%, matching the paper's stated 33.3%). Read together: in UK practice the resolved code typically TRAILS cessation of prescribing by about a year, while in a third of cases it arrives while prescribing is still live. The candidate declares prescribing-cessation-for-180-days as a *fallback measure of the same construct* as the removal event; in the one dataset where both are observable they are sequentially dependent, with the fallback as the likely antecedent of the primary. A design that reads "retraction -> therapy change" is then measuring a lagged administrative confirmation of a prescribing decision already made.
