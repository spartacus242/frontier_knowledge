Question (canonical, Gate 0 as intaken):
Treating the magnitude an authoritative agency has published for an earthquake at a fixed settlement latency as a forecast of the magnitude it will finally publish, (a) is that forecast efficient — is the revision unbiased, serially unpredictable, and uncorrelated with information available at the settlement instant (depth, region, network density, magnitude type, time of day, event size) — and (b) over an enumerated population of parametric contracts and automated response thresholds keyed to that published value, what share of trigger decisions would have come out differently under the final magnitude, and with what sign?

Question (Gate 1 reformulation — ONE used, for well-posedness only; both versions recorded):
For earthquakes with M >= M0 in a declared region and window, using agency A's own versioned event history (or, failing that, an independently archived snapshot series or A's dated printed bulletins) to read back the magnitude m_tau that A had published at latency tau after origin time, and fixing "final" as A's value m_D as of a declared date D: (a) is the revision r = m_D - m_tau mean-zero (bias stated in magnitude units, with CI) and orthogonal to the settlement-instant information set (depth, region, station count/azimuthal gap, magnitude type, local time, m_tau itself), reported as a news-versus-noise decomposition; and (b) over the PUBLICLY DOCUMENTED population of parametric contracts and automated response thresholds keyed to A's published magnitude, how many trigger decisions flip under m_D rather than m_tau, with the sign of each flip and the money or action at stake?
Reason for the reformulation: the intake version left tau, D, A and the contract frame unpinned, which made the estimand unstated rather than ill-posed. Nothing about the substance changed.

Alternative formulations:
1. Are earthquake magnitude revisions news or noise?
2. Is the magnitude that settles a parametric earthquake contract a biased estimate of the magnitude the catalogue ends up with, and does the bias run towards or away from payout?

Motivating gap:
Parametric earthquake instruments settle on a named agency's reported magnitude (plus depth and location box) as published at a named time, while the agency keeps revising that magnitude for days to weeks. A revision process designed for scientific accuracy is therefore load-bearing for binary financial and emergency-response decisions. One intake query had already been spent at the intersection; it returned trade press and a blog and no study of revision efficiency or of flipped triggers. Gate 2 opened the trade-press item the intake flagged and found the mechanism documented in a real case: for the 18 April 2014 Guerrero event, Artemis reports that "The USGS initially recorded it as a M7.5 quake event" and that it "was subsequently downgraded to a M7.2 magnitude", and states that had the quake fallen inside Zone 3 and "had the initial magnitude of 7.5 been maintained", the MultiCat Mexico 2012 bond would have been triggered. So the stake is real and documented; what is absent is any measurement of it.

What would count as an answer:
A regression of r = m_D - m_tau on the settlement-instant information set, reported as a news-versus-noise decomposition with confidence intervals and with the bias term stated in magnitude units; plus a tabulated count, over the declared contract and threshold population, of decisions that flip under m_D, with the sign of each flip and the money or action at stake. A clean negative — revisions centred on zero, unpredictable from settlement-instant information, and no flipped decisions — is a full answer and is the outcome that retires the worry. A positive answer is actionable in three named places: whether contracts should settle on a later vintage or carry a revision clause; whether the agency should publish an uncertainty or a "provisional" flag alongside the value it knows will be used for settlement; and how the ILS market should price this component of basis risk.

Significance — consequence:
If YES (revisions biased and/or predictable at the settlement instant, with flips): the published magnitude is a biased input to binary decisions, and the bias has a sign, so three things change concretely — contracts move settlement to a later vintage or add a revision clause; the agency publishes a provisional flag or an uncertainty alongside the value it knows will be used for settlement; and the ILS market gets a priceable component of basis risk that is currently invisible because it is attributed to the hazard rather than to the measurement. If NO (revisions centred on zero, unpredictable, no flips): current practice is licensed, and the near-miss anecdotes (the documented 7.5 -> 7.2 downgrade) are shown to be tail noise rather than a systematic exposure — which is itself worth having, because it closes an argument the ILS press reopens after every large event.
Significance — audience:
(i) The reporting agency: a bias term with a sign is an operational argument for a publication-status flag or an uncertainty estimate on the value at a named latency, which is a product decision the NEIC/JMA/GeoNet class of agency can take. (ii) Tsunami-warning and automated-response operators, whose thresholds are keyed to the same published value and whose flip count is the same arithmetic on a non-financial decision set. (iii) ILS issuers, calculation agents, cat modellers and rating agencies, who currently treat the reported value as a contractual fact; a signed bias changes expected payout, not just variance. (iv) Sovereign-risk desks at the World Bank and IDB, who are both the buyers and, per Collich et al., co-designers of the triggers.
Significance — fecundity:
Opens: the same test on depth and location (the other two contractual parameters, where revision is larger); whether the revision's sign depends on whether the event is near a contractual threshold (a political-economy question about agency incentives once the agency knows a value is load-bearing); the optimal settlement latency as a decision problem trading revision risk against liquidity; whether multi-agency disagreement at a fixed instant is a better uncertainty proxy than any single agency's own revision; and the same apparatus on other official physical statistics that settle contracts (reanalysed hurricane intensity, river gauge stages, degree-days).
Significance — anchoring:
Touches the ILS basis-risk literature, which names the gap between an index and the loss but not the gap between an index's first and final published values; the catalogue-homogenisation literature in seismology, which corrects period-level inhomogeneity and has no per-event vintage object; the operational-seismology debate on rapid Mw underestimation that followed 2011; and the macroeconometric real-time data programme, whose entire apparatus sits unused on this data type.

Reason for silence:
A disciplinary boundary plus an incentive gap, with a new-data component. The method lives in macroeconometrics, whose practitioners have no reason to look at seismic bulletins and no access to the contract side. Seismologists frame revision as accuracy improvement rather than as a forecast whose rationality is testable, and have no incentive to audit their own published value as a financial index — the framing requires treating one's own later work as the realisation of one's own earlier forecast, which is not how a catalogue is conceived. The ILS side has the opposite incentive: the appeal of parametric cover is a value that is FINAL BY DEFINITION at the settlement date, so revision is a nuisance to be contracted around rather than a quantity to measure, and measuring it would surface a priced risk that neither issuer nor sponsor benefits from naming. The new-data component: per-event versioned event histories with machine-readable publication timestamps are a recent agency product, so until recently the vintage series would have had to be reconstructed from dated paper bulletins.
