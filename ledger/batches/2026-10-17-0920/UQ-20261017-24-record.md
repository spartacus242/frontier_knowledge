Question (canonical, V1 as intaken):
Now that spacecraft-reentry metal input to the stratosphere is comparable to or larger than the cosmic-dust input for several elements, by how much does it bias the tracer-based constraints on cosmic-dust influx to Earth, and can meteoric and spacecraft material still be separated per particle and per snow/ice layer well enough to preserve those constraints as reentry mass grows?

Question (canonical, V2 — the one Gate 1 reformulation, used for Gate 2 and Gate 3):
For each published method that constrains extraterrestrial mass influx to Earth from metal abundances (per-particle stratospheric aerosol composition; mesospheric metal layers; meteoric smoke; metals in polar snow/firn/ice), state its tracer elements and compute (a) the fractional bias in its inferred influx implied by the published per-element anthropogenic reentry flux and that flux's uncertainty, and (b) the reentry-mass level or calendar date at which that bias exceeds the method's own quoted precision — with the meteoric/spacecraft separation rule declared in advance and its misattribution rate estimated on pre-space-age archived material.
(Reformulation was for well-posedness only: it names the estimand per method, fixes the precision criterion that V1 left in the words "well enough", and separates the three deliverables. Both versions are recorded; V2 does not widen scope.)

Alternative formulations:
- Lay: Has space hardware broken the instrument we use to weigh cosmic dust?
- Technical: For each published method that constrains extraterrestrial mass influx from metal abundances in stratospheric aerosol, mesospheric layers or polar snow, what is the anthropogenic reentry component in the post-1990 record, and what bias does it impose on the inferred influx?

Motivating gap:
Murphy et al., "Metals from spacecraft reentry in stratospheric aerosol particles", PNAS 120(43), 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2313374120 — READ IN FULL this gate (see Access routing) — identifies >20 elements in spacecraft-alloy proportions inside stratospheric sulfuric-acid particles; ~10% (+/-7%) of particles >120 nm contain Al and other reentry elements against ~20% containing meteoric iron; projects the reentry-bearing share toward the ~50% that now contain meteoric metals; and reports that reentry Li, Al, Cu and Pb already exceed the cosmic-dust influx of those metals (Al: ~210 t/yr ablating from spacecraft vs ~20 t/yr of the ~130 t/yr meteoric Al that ablates; ">90% of the lithium, copper, and lead are in particles with signatures of spacecraft reentry"). The same class of measurement — the abundance and carrier fraction of extraterrestrial metals in stratospheric particles, mesospheric layers and polar snow — is one of the independent constraints on total cosmic-dust influx, a quantity disputed across roughly an order of magnitude by method. The intake's claim that the paper's own stated gap is only "the effect of this metal content on stratospheric aerosol properties is unknown" is CONFIRMED by the full text: the discussion's open items are aerosol properties, oxide persistence in H2SO4, ice nucleation/NAT, size distribution, optics and novel chemistry, plus a suggestion to reanalyse older spectra. The paper does NOT raise the influx-bias limb. It does say iron "cannot currently be quantified on top of the larger meteoric contribution", that "it is difficult to exclude the possibility of a second population of meteoric particles", and that reentry copper deposition "could possibly double the concentration of copper in Antarctic snow" — the nearest the source comes to the candidate, and it is framed as deposition, not as bias to a constraint.

Presuppositions (status after this gate):
1. At least one published influx constraint rests on metal abundances in stratospheric aerosol, mesospheric metal layers, meteoric smoke or polar snow. ESTABLISHED in kind (stratospheric-particle metal-fraction and mesospheric-layer methods both exist and are used; the Li natural-input figure in Wing et al. 2025 is derived from the sodium injection rate and a chondritic Li/Na ratio, i.e. from exactly such a constraint). NOT enumerated here: the full tracer-method inventory was not assembled within the query cap, and that enumeration is part of the work, not a premise.
2. Meteoric and spacecraft material are separable per particle by element ratios. ESTABLISHED in operational form, with no published error rate: Murphy et al. use Al/Fe > 0.1 (meteoric background ~0.04) with sodium as the key discriminator ("there is no corresponding population with enhanced sodium"), Mg/Fe and Na/Fe as meteoric references, and element-specific rules for Nb (m/z 93/109 NbO+), Hf (177-180 + oxides), Ag (107/109 isotope check), Cu (63Cu with m/z 61 correction), Li (7Li+ only), Pb (as Pb/Al). The NASA JSC stratospheric collection has used a coarser per-particle scheme for decades (C / TCN / TCA / AOS, AOS = aluminium-oxide spheres "mostly generated by solid rocket propellant"). Misattribution rate: not quantified in anything read; Lasue et al. call their classification "preliminary".
3. Reentry mass flux is estimable per element with usable uncertainty. ESTABLISHED and published: Schulz et al. 2025 estimate injection of 43 elements for 2015-2025 plus two future scenarios, and the methods section read here shows the composition uncertainty handled by averaging studies, renormalising to 100%, separate treatment of PCBs/batteries/solar cells/CFRP, and explicit upper-limit choices (e.g. 6.4% battery mass against alternatives of 7.5% and 9.1%) — with the authors themselves calling some source values "zero-order estimates with unlikely values". So the premise holds but the uncertainty is large, author-acknowledged and partly judgemental.
4. Pre-space-age archived material exists and is analysable. NOT VERIFIED in this gate for the specific validation the question needs. Murphy's own record starts in 1998; the JSC catalogs start in 1981; the Greenland Ir/Pt work (Gabrielli et al., Nature 432, 2004) is Holocene ice, i.e. the baseline material exists in principle but no pre/post-1960 contrast on these tracers was found.
5. FALSIFYING CONDITION: the disputed influx range is sensitive at the magnitude of the anthropogenic input for at least one tracer method. PARTLY ADVERSE. The elements where reentry now dominates are the ultratrace/alloy ones (Li, Al, Cu, Pb, Nb, Hf, Ag); the elements the main tracer methods rest on are Fe, Mg, Na, Si, Ni (layers, smoke, particle fractions) and Ir, Pt, Os (ice and sediment), where meteoric input still dominates — Murphy states explicitly that spacecraft iron "cannot currently be quantified on top of the larger meteoric contribution" and that Mg/Fe is tight "because meteors dominate the source of Mg and Fe". Schulz et al.'s headline (24 of 43 elements anthropogenic-dominated in 2024) does not, from what was read, place Mg/Fe/Si/Ni/Ir/Pt/Os in that set. So the question tends toward its own null for the dominant methods, which is an answer but a thin one.
6. Tracer abundance is attributable to an influx rate at all (transport/removal model good enough that tracer bias propagates to influx bias). NOT VERIFIED here; flagged contested.

What would count as an answer: (i) an enumerated inventory of influx-constraint methods with tracer elements and a propagated per-method bias from the per-element reentry flux and its uncertainty; (ii) a measurement of the spacecraft-attributable fraction of the tracer signal per particle or per layer under a pre-declared separation rule validated on pre-1960 archived material, with a stated misattribution rate; (iii) a projection of the reentry-mass level or date at which each method can no longer constrain the natural flux at its own quoted precision. A null — all tracer methods resting on elements where reentry input is small, plus a flat pre/post-1990 contrast — closes the question in the negative.

Significance — consequence: If the bias is large for any method, published influx numbers derived from post-1990 metal-tracer material need restriction to pre-space-age layers or an explicit anthropogenic correction, and the order-of-magnitude influx dispute acquires a systematic term that grows with the launch rate; the ~5-300 t/day spread would stop being purely a methods disagreement. If small — the likely answer on the numbers read here, since Mg, Fe, Si, Ni, Ir, Pt and Os remain meteoric-dominated while the anthropogenic-dominated elements (Li, Al, Cu, Pb, Nb, Hf, Ag) are not the tracers the main constraints use — then the constraints are safe, the null is publishable in a paragraph, and the real value is the dated projection of when each method fails. That asymmetry (a probable, cheap null) is what holds the score down.
Significance — audience: the cosmic-dust-influx/meteoric-input-function community (Plane's Leeds group, CODITA alumni, Carrillo-Sanchez-style flux modellers) would learn whether their modern-material constraints need an anthropogenic term; NASA JSC cosmic-dust curation (Zolensky) already acts on this through the TCA/AOS classes and would gain a quantified error rate; ice-core geochemists would learn whether Murphy's Antarctic-copper-doubling scenario threatens metal-tracer records in post-1990 firn; ESA CleanSpace and the space-sustainability regulators, whose own gap list (CSD 2026) contains no measurement-bias item, would gain one.
