Question (canonical): In adult inpatients transfused under a single-unit policy, what fraction of the between-episode variance in the post-transfusion haemoglobin increment is attributable to the measured haemoglobin mass of the specific red-cell unit issued, and does the probability of needing a second unit depend on which unit the patient happened to receive? Reformulation note: one reformulation was used at Gate 1 (V2), adding non-bleeding adult inpatients at a service with a documented single-unit-then-reassess policy, a declared 12-48 h sampling window, inventory issue order within the ABO/Rh- and specification-compatible pool as L2's assignment mechanism with a stated covariate-balance test, a 48-h second-unit hazard, and a third limb (c) asking the pre-declared change in the 48-h second-unit rate under mass-based issuing. V1 and V2 are both on the record; V2 is the version scored.

Alternative formulations:
- Is "one unit" a dose — how much of the variability in haemoglobin response sits in the bag rather than in the patient?
- If a blood service labelled each red-cell unit with its haemoglobin mass and issued by mass instead of by count, how many fewer second units would be needed to reach the same haemoglobin target? [This is the formulation that was killed; see Novelty.]

Motivating gap: the single-unit policy presupposes that a unit is a reproducible dose, while regulatory quality monitoring sets only a MINIMUM haemoglobin content per unit on a sampled basis, so issued units span a wide range of haemoglobin mass and the delivered dose is unmeasured at the bedside. Three limbs are graded separately because they do not share a fate: L1 variance share and slope on grams delivered; L2 which-unit dependence of the second-unit hazard under natural, un-selected variation; L3 the mass-based-issuing counterfactual.
