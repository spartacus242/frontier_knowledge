Question (canonical): [R1, adopted at Gate 1] In biobanks that link whole-genome/exome data to routine clinical laboratory results (UK Biobank primary-care and hospital extracts, All of Us EHR labs, national laboratory registries), restricted to a pre-registered analyte set fixed by a published feasibility census of how many analytes carry resolvable assay/platform metadata: (a) for each analyte, do coding variants in the gene encoding the measured analyte (single-variant plus gene-based burden, epitope-agnostic over all coding variants) associate with the immunoassay-reported clinical value, adjusted for a trans polygenic score for the analyte, ancestry, age, sex, assay era and site, and does the association replicate in a second biobank; (b) where two or more platforms are recorded for one analyte, is there a variant-by-platform interaction — a bias signature a purely biological effect does not predict; (c) in people measured for the same analyte both by immunoassay and by a variant-agnostic orthogonal method (LC-MS/MS quantified on peptides not spanning the variant site, or an activity/function-based assay), does the genotype effect fall inside a pre-specified equivalence band (the assay-bias prediction) or persist outside it (the proteoform-physiology prediction); and (d) for each effect surviving (a)–(c), what is the rate of clinical decision-threshold crossings (cardiac troponin 99th percentile, TSH reference limits, HbA1c 6.5%, analyte-specific limits) per 100,000 tests among tested carriers, with CIs, and does carriers' downstream care (repeat testing, referral, treatment initiation) differ? Pre-specified third outcome: if the census finds fewer than a pre-set number of analytes with usable platform metadata, the reportable answer is the bounded feasibility result plus limbs (a) and (d) with (b) declared not estimable.

Question (V0, intake — superseded at Gate 1, recorded per Gate 1): In biobanks that link whole-genome data to routine clinical laboratory results with assay platform recorded, how often do coding variants in the measured analyte's own gene shift the immunoassay-reported concentration far enough to move a patient across a clinical decision threshold, and does that shift vanish when the same analyte is measured in the same people by an orthogonal (mass-spectrometric or activity-based) method?

Why reformulated (well-posedness only, one reformulation used): V0 (i) gave "how often" no denominator or analyte set, so no number was defined; (ii) treated "the shift vanishes" as a null hypothesis, which absence of significance cannot establish — it needs an equivalence band; (iii) missed that an LC-MS/MS method quantifying a peptide that spans the variant site is itself variant-blind, which would make the orthogonal limb circular rather than discriminating; (iv) had no reportable outcome if the platform-metadata presupposition fails. R1 fixes all four and makes the feasibility census a deliverable either way.

Alternative formulations:
- What is the population prevalence of clinically misleading laboratory results caused by the patient's own epitope-region variants, assay by assay?
- Which routine immunoassays carry a detectable cis effect of the analyte gene that disappears under a variant-agnostic orthogonal measurement, and how many threshold crossings per 100,000 tests does each cause?

Motivating gap: The newly measurable quantity is the join itself — population-scale genomes linked to routine clinical lab results with platform metadata. The mechanism is established only case by case (HbA1c in G6PD/haemoglobin-variant carriers, which produced clinical guidance; macro-TSH; variant hCG invisible to some assays; GDF-15 H202D) and, in research affinity proteomics, as cis "epitope effects" where a coding variant changes binder affinity rather than abundance. Nobody has estimated the cross-assay, population-scale prevalence and decision impact in routine care. The two communities holding the pieces look past each other: clinical chemistry manages interference through case reports and package inserts (and through CLSI-style interference testing with spiked interferents, which has no genotype arm), while biobank GWAS of laboratory traits read cis signals as biology or discard them, without quantifying patient-level harm.

Presuppositions:
P1 (contested, and itself a reported outcome in R1): routine results in these biobanks carry enough analyzer/platform metadata to separate assay from biology. Primary-care extracts are often platform-blind, which may restrict the answerable analyte set.
P2 (contested): a variant-agnostic orthogonal method exists and is available in a subset of the same people for at least several analytes. LC-MS/MS exists for steroids, thyroid hormones and some peptides, not for all immunoassay analytes.
P3 (contested, and deliberately not relied on): antibody epitopes are mappable for the assays studied. Epitopes are proprietary for most IVDs, so the primary test must be epitope-agnostic over all coding variants in the analyte gene.
P4 (contested; the design's central inferential risk): a genotype-associated shift that attenuates under an orthogonal method implies assay bias rather than a real difference in what the two methods measure. Proteoform-specific physiology (the variant protein is genuinely less abundant, differently cleared, or differently active) is the rival explanation; R1 addresses it by direction of prediction plus the variant-by-platform interaction, not by assertion.
P5 (new at Gate 1, contested): the orthogonal method is itself variant-agnostic. A tryptic peptide spanning the variant site is mass-shifted and will be under-quantified exactly like a disrupted epitope, so peptide selection must be pre-specified; activity-based assays carry the mirror risk (a variant can alter activity without altering mass).
P6 (largely established): clinical decision thresholds are stated and stable enough to count crossings (troponin 99th percentile, TSH reference limits, HbA1c 6.5%).
P7 (contested, power): coding-variant carriers are numerous enough among people actually tested for each analyte to estimate a crossing rate with usable CIs; for rare variants this requires gene-based collapsing, which R1 specifies.
Not presupposed: that prevalence is high. A tight bound showing it is rare is equally informative, because the number is currently unknown.

What would count as an answer: A ranked list of assays, each with an estimated per-100,000-test misclassification rate and CI, built from: a replicated cis coding-variant association against the immunoassay-reported clinical value; a variant-by-platform interaction wherever platform is recorded; a within-person orthogonal comparison in a subset where assay bias predicts attenuation inside a stated equivalence band; and translation into threshold crossings plus downstream-care differences among carriers. A bounded null — "fewer than X crossings per 100,000 tests across the N analytes testable, 95% upper bound" — is an equally admissible answer. Also admissible and pre-specified: the feasibility census result, i.e. how many routine analytes can be studied this way at all.

Significance — consequence: If yes for any analyte, a reported clinical number is wrong in a direction fixed by the patient's genome, and wrongness is inherited — concentrated in families and, because coding-variant frequencies differ by ancestry, unequally distributed across groups, producing differential misclassification exactly where reference-interval equity is already contested. That converts a per-case curiosity into a population quantity with owners: an insert warning, a reflex-test rule or a calibrator/epitope redesign becomes justifiable or not by a number instead of by anecdote. If no — a tight upper bound across the testable analytes — two things follow. Laboratories can stop treating genotype-driven interference as an unquantified hazard, and, more consequentially, biobank GWAS of routine-care laboratory traits acquire a licence they currently lack: the cis signal in the analyte's own gene can be read as biology, which matters because those cis variants are the instruments used in Mendelian randomisation of biomarkers. A bias of this shape would be a violation of the exclusion restriction that no MR sensitivity analysis currently probes, so the null is load-bearing too. The third pre-specified outcome — that routine extracts are too platform-blind to answer this — is itself actionable: it names a metadata gap for the bodies that specify laboratory data standards.
Significance — audience: (i) IVD manufacturers and notified bodies, who hold the epitopes and, under IVDR post-market performance follow-up, must act on evidence of a population subgroup in which an assay misreports — their lever is insert warnings, epitope or calibrator redesign; (ii) IFCC and CLSI standardisation and interference committees, whose interference-testing framework currently has spiked-interferent arms and no genotype arm — a ranked list is exactly the input needed to add one; (iii) laboratory directors who set reflex-testing, delta-check and orthogonal-confirmation rules, who can act per analyte without any disclosure from manufacturers; (iv) guideline writers who own the crossed thresholds, with HbA1c/G6PD as the precedent that this community does change guidance on this kind of evidence; (v) biobank custodians and lab-GWAS/MR groups, whose licence to use routine labs as quantitative phenotypes depends on the answer.
Significance — fecundity: whether any polygenic score built on an immunoassay-measured lab trait partly scores assay affinity rather than physiology; whether ancestry differences in reference intervals are partly assay artifacts tracking allele frequency; whether MR studies using cis instruments for immunoassay-measured biomarkers need a bias-sensitivity analysis of this specific form; whether calibrators and commutability standards should be proteoform-aware; whether genotype-aware result reporting (flag, correct, or reflex) is net-beneficial; and, symmetrically, whether a cis signal that survives an orthogonal method identifies real proteoform physiology worth studying — the same scan that finds artifacts finds biology.
Significance — anchoring: the live epitope-effect dispute in pQTL studies (N1); the HbA1c/G6PD and haemoglobin-variant guidance precedent (N2); the macro-TSH, variant-hCG and GDF-15 case literature (N3); Lp(a) isoform-dependent immunoassay bias and its standardisation fight; the exclusion-restriction problem for cis-MR of biomarkers; IVDR post-market performance follow-up obligations; and the equity argument about ancestry-specific reference intervals.

Reason for silence: a disciplinary boundary reinforced by an unusually effective camouflage, on top of a genuinely new join. (i) Expected-signal camouflage — the single mechanism I would defend as primary: in a GWAS of a laboratory trait, a cis hit in the gene encoding the measured analyte is the most expected result there is, read as positive control and biological confirmation. The artifact's signature is identical to the field's success criterion, so the one signal that would reveal assay bias is the one signal nobody interrogates. (ii) Ontology mismatch in the owning field: clinical-chemistry interference testing is built around substances added to a sample (haemolysis, icterus, lipaemia, biotin, heterophile antibodies, drugs). The patient's genome is not an interferent in that ontology, so no standard, no insert section and no post-market protocol has a slot for it, and the case reports that do exist are filed as curiosities per analyte rather than as instances of a class. (iii) Split custody and split capability: manufacturers know the epitopes and cannot be compelled to publish a subgroup in which their assay misreports; biobanks hold the genomes and the results but not the epitopes, and until recently not the platform metadata either. Each party can ask only half the question. (iv) New instrument: population-scale WGS linked to routine-care results with any analyzer metadata is recent, and the orthogonal-method subsets (LC-MS/MS on the same people) are newer still. None of this is "no one thought of it": the per-analyte version has been thought of repeatedly (N2, N3) and acted on, which is precisely what makes the absence of the population-scale, cross-assay version a boundary effect rather than an oversight.
