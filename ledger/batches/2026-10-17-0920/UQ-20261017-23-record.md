Question (canonical) — Gate 0 version as intaken:
In modern mill test certificates for a declared steel grade and product class, how much of the observed offset above the specified minimum yield strength, and of the shape of the lower tail, is produced by the conformance decision itself — mill aim-point setting, retest rules, and re-certification of non-conforming heats into adjacent grades — as identified from the excess mass at the specification notch and from the coupled distributions of adjacent grades, and what does the de-selected latent distribution do to the characteristic value and reliability index that code calibration infers from certificate statistics?

Question (canonical) — Gate 1 REFORMULATION (one permitted; used, for well-posedness only; this is the version the record grades):
For a pre-declared heat-level mill-certificate frame stratified by grade AND by product/thickness class (because the specified minimum f_y varies within a grade by thickness, so the "notch" is a set of notches), what fraction of the mean offset (f_y,measured - f_y,specified) and of the lower-tail shape is attributable to the conformance decision rather than to production, where the conformance component is identified NOT by a single-notch density-smoothness argument but by a mass-balance condition across adjacent grades sharing a mill and product line — the mass missing below grade G's minimum must reappear as excess mass in the upper tail of grade G-1 at a rate consistent across notches — and what are the resulting latent (pre-selection) 5th percentile and the reliability index beta for one or two canonical code-calibration cases, relative to the certificate-based values?
[Why reformulated: the Gate 0 version leans on "excess mass at the specification notch", which presupposes density on both sides of the notch to fit a counterfactual. For a conforming grade the below-notch mass is largely absent by construction, so the economics estimator in its standard form has no left side to fit. The reformulation moves the identifying content to the cross-grade mass balance, which the Gate 0 version listed only as a mitigation, and makes the within-grade multi-notch stratification explicit. Both versions recorded; no change to the object of inquiry.]

Alternative formulations:
- Is the extra strength that design codes credit to real steel a property of the steel, or an artefact of which heats get called that grade?
- Applying a notch/bunching-style estimator to grade-conformance records, what is the latent pre-selection strength distribution of the material actually in structures, and how far does it differ in the 5th percentile from the certificate distribution used in reliability calibration?

Motivating gap:
The canonical probabilistic model for steel names the selection mechanism and then does not model it; two independent empirical studies in the same field each SAW the mechanism's signature and each declined to model it, one of them in so many words.
(a) JCSS Probabilistic Model Code, steel chapter (jcss-lc.org/publications/jcsspmc/steelpr.pdf, read in full at intake): states that "downgrading of material, i.e. re-classifying higher grade steel to a lower grade" "produces bi-modal distributions" with higher means and significantly higher CoVs to be expected; makes no adjustment; says nothing about truncation or selection at the specified minimum; recommends multivariate log-normal with CoV = 0.07 and mean f_y = f_y(sp)*alpha*exp(-u*v) - C with alpha = 1.05, C = 20 MPa; states "The CoV values refer to total steel production" — i.e. the statistics describe production, not the material in any particular structure. Underlying data are European studies from 1970 onward.
(b) AISC Engineering Journal, Updating Standard Shape Material Properties Database for Design and Reliability (EJ v40 n1, doi.org/10.62913/engj.v40i1.800; opened in Gate 2): "The data also imply that the distribution may be truncated at Fy = 50 ksi, or ln(Fy) = 3.91, because" the CDF is nearly vertical there — and then adopts a distribution that "conservatively, neglects any truncation at the specified yield stress value." The notch is seen, named, and set aside; the beta computation that follows (2.37-2.77 depending on phi and discretization) does not carry the truncation.
(c) Melcher, Kala, Holicky, Fajkus, Rozlivka, Design characteristics of structural steels based on statistical analysis of metallurgical products, J. Constructional Steel Research 2004 (opened in Gate 2): attributes the asymmetry of the S355 plate yield distribution to "the exclusion of values not satisfying the requirements of material standards for that tensile strength class" and states "they are reclassified to lower tensile strength classes." The mechanism is used qualitatively to explain a distribution's shape; its rate is never estimated, adjacent grades are fitted independently, and the paper's own anomaly — P(f_y < 355 MPa) about 8.6% for S355, which it calls "worse than the supposed 5%" — is left standing.
So the empirical fudges (alpha, C) that encode the above-minimum offset have never been decomposed into "mills aim high" versus "non-conformers were removed or moved", and the lower tail — the only part reliability indices depend on — is inferred from a record generated by the very decision that censors it. Public economics has an estimator aimed at exactly this object: excess mass at a threshold identifies the response to the threshold.

Presuppositions (each marked established / contested / anchor):
1. A heat-level certificate frame with grade, specified minimum and measured yield is obtainable in volume. CONTESTED on access, demonstrably assemblable: TxDOT holds 3,976 (Gr 40) / 1,587 (Gr 50) / 2,030 (A36) individual mill-certification yield values across 394 projects; the AISC/SSPC database holds about 20,000 values; Melcher et al. report n = 5,493 (S235) and 1,089 (S355) plate observations. VERIFIED this gate for existence of volume; NOT verified for heat-level linkage plus retest/re-certification flags, which is the frame the question actually needs and which nothing opened this gate demonstrates exists.
2. Downgrading / re-certification of non-conforming heats happens at a non-negligible rate. ANCHOR, not assumption — it is the central unknown. Two field sources assert the mechanism (JCSS; Melcher et al. 2004). A dedicated query for a published rate returned only vendor marketing on "off-grade reduction"; no published downgrade or re-certification rate was found.
3. The threshold is a genuine notch. True by the specification's logic; partially smoothed by retest provisions (ASTM A370/A6 and equivalents) which must be encoded explicitly. STRENGTHENED and COMPLICATED this gate: AISC reports a near-vertical CDF at 50 ksi for A992 shapes (sharp notch), while Melcher reports 8.6% of S355 plate values below 355 MPa — consistent with a thickness-graded specified minimum rather than a single notch. CONSEQUENCE: the frame must stratify by product/thickness class; the apparent "notch violation" is partly a within-grade multi-notch structure, which is identification-relevant in the candidate's favour.
4. The latent density is smooth across the threshold absent selection. CONTESTED and the real interface problem. Mills aim above the minimum, so the counterfactual density is not centred on the notch, and a single-notch bunching estimator is not identified. Confirmed as a genuine open methodological point by reading the method prior: Ito and Sallee construct the counterfactual by fitting a polynomial "excluding the data at the notches", define excess mass on a bin that STARTS at the notch, and "does not directly address a non-smooth or off-centre pre-policy density", resting instead on the observation that "there are no obvious holes in the distribution." Transplanting that reasoning to a conforming grade fails, because the hole below the minimum is exactly what the data have. Mitigation the question now stands on (moved into the canonical formulation at Gate 1): multi-threshold variation plus the cross-grade mass balance that downgrading induces.
5. The structural-reliability estimand depends on the lower tail of the in-structure distribution. ESTABLISHED: characteristic values are 5%-fractile-type (Melcher et al. compute the 5% quantile and a 0.1% design quantile; Taras's JCSS deck targets beta = 3.8 with alpha_R = 0.8, "P_non-exceedance ~ 10-3 for resistance values").
6. Codes' alpha and C factors were fitted to certificate data rather than to independently sampled coupons. NOT VERIFIED this gate. The dedicated query returned the JCSS chapter itself and off-topic JCSS meeting material; the chain from the 1970-onward European studies to alpha = 1.05 / C = 20 MPa was not traced to a primary source. Flagged as the record's weakest presupposition. Partial counter-evidence found: the AISC EJ study's own parameters come from measured coupons (207 coupons from 38 heats, 8 shapes, 3 producers), so at least one modern calibration input is coupon-based, not certificate-based.
7. Certificate values and in-structure properties differ only by the selection being studied, not by systematic test-method or location effects. PARTIALLY SUPPORTED and PARTIALLY UNDERCUT this gate. Support: AISC EJ reports observed/mill-test-certificate yield ratio mean 1.002, CoV 0.044 — certificates track coupons on the same material closely. Undercut: the same paper fixes a static-vs-A370-rate yield offset (mean static yield reduced 55.8 -> 51.4 ksi) and finds significant producer-to-producer differences; Melcher et al. note "yield strength in flanges is lower than that in web due to non-uniform cross-section cooling down", and the certificate coupon is taken at one-third of the flange. So a location/strain-rate correction must be applied BEFORE any selection component is read off, or the two will be confounded. The UT Austin work on variation in standardized yield-strength tests was surfaced by search but NOT opened (fetch budget spent); it remains an unread input that the estimand's fixing depends on.

What would count as an answer:
A fitted selection model on a pre-declared certificate frame (at least K mills; at least two grades sharing a mill and product line; heat-level yield and tensile values; retest and re-certification flags where available; stratified by product/thickness class) reporting (i) estimated conformance-attributable mass at each notch with uncertainty; (ii) an estimated downgrade/re-certification rate that is consistent across the coupled adjacent grades — the internal check that distinguishes selection from aim-point drift, and the thing that makes a null falsifiable; (iii) the implied latent distribution and its 5th percentile against the certificate 5th percentile; (iv) recomputed reliability index for one or two canonical code-calibration cases under latent versus certificate distributions. A NULL answers it: no excess mass beyond what a fitted aim-point offset explains, and adjacent-grade distributions consistent with independent production, would vindicate JCSS's unadjusted use of certificate statistics and reduce C = 20 MPa to a pure aim-point offset. A pre-registered estimator and a stated handling of the absent below-notch mass are prerequisites, not refinements.

Significance — consequence:
If YES (a material share of the offset is conformance-produced): the certificate-derived statistics that feed code calibration describe a selected population, and the direction of the error is not uniformly safe. Three concrete things move. (i) JCSS's C = 20 MPa and alpha = 1.05 stop being production parameters and become partly selection parameters, which means they are supply-chain-specific rather than material-specific — a structure fed by a downgrade-heavy line has different tail statistics from one fed by a line that scraps non-conformers, and no current model distinguishes them. (ii) The unimodal lognormal with CoV = 0.07 is the wrong shape for the sub-population that matters: JCSS itself says downgrading gives higher means AND significantly higher CoVs and bimodality, so a fitted unimodal tail understates lower-tail mass for the lower grade exactly where beta is determined. (iii) The assessment side is anti-conservative in the mean direction: TxDOT's higher recommended load-rating yields and Caltrans BDM 16-04 credit measured-above-nominal strength to existing structures, and if part of that margin is "this heat was called Grade 50 because it passed", it does not transfer to the member in front of the rater.
If NO (the null): a positive result for the field, not a non-result. It would convert C = 20 MPa into a defensible pure aim-point offset, retire the JCSS bimodality caveat as practically inert, close the AISC deferral with evidence rather than with the word "conservatively", and explain Melcher's 8.6% anomaly as thickness-class aggregation rather than selection.

Significance — audience:
Four identifiable bodies, each currently deciding something this answer bears on. (1) The JCSS PMC working group — it wrote the unadjusted bimodality sentence and is actively revising material chapters (the 2021 WG material shows the concrete group proposing joint cross-class copula models while the steel chapter still carries the 1970s-data constants). (2) CEN/TC250/SC3 and the EN 1990 Annex D/E route — Taras's WG deck proposes gamma_M0 = gamma_M1 = 1.0 on producer-supplied statistics and warns that "some of the values of gamma_M might have to be increased by ~10%" if steel properties do not match Annex E; whether those producer statistics are selection-inflated is precisely the question, and the proposal is to transfer Annex E "to steel producing QM", i.e. to institutionalise the certificate record as the calibration input. (3) The AISC Specification Committee — the phi 0.90 -> 0.95 question in P1 turns on material bias and CoV, and P1's own beta range (2.37-2.54 at phi = 0.95) sits close enough to the 2.52 original-calibration target that a tail correction could decide it. (4) Bridge owners and load-rating authorities (TxDOT, Caltrans, AASHTO MBE users) who already grant higher yields than the MBE defaults on certificate statistics. Each would act differently: a grade-and-supply-chain-conditional material model, a different fractile for assessment, or an explicit selection adjustment in the next calibration.

Significance — fecundity:
Opens the same question wherever a graded commodity's conformance record is used as a material model: reinforcing steel (Grade 60 vs 80, where TxDOT's own counts show the multi-grade frame already exists), concrete strength classes (JCSS's concrete group has already built cross-class joint models with vine copulas, so the coupling machinery exists in the same code and could carry a selection term), high-strength bolts, and welding consumables. It also makes a methodological question askable in economics: identification of a notch response when the below-threshold mass is removed from the sample rather than relabelled within it, which is the general case for conformance-certified goods.

Reason for silence:
Three mechanisms, the third of which is the specific one and is sourced.
(1) A disciplinary boundary with a vocabulary gap. Structural reliability treats a mill certificate as a measurement of a material, not as the output of a decision, and has no term for selection on the dependent variable; the estimator that exploits the threshold belongs to public economics and only became standard equipment after Saez (2010), Chetty et al. (2011) and Kleven-Waseem (2013) — well after the 1970-onward European datasets on which the JCSS steel model still rests were analysed. The Gate 2 method-field query confirms the traffic has not run the other way either: density-discontinuity/manipulation testing has no published application to product conformance data.
(2) Data custody and incentive. Aim points and downgrade flows are commercially sensitive mill information; the publicly assembled databases (SSPC, TxDOT) were built for load rating and for resistance factors, so they kept the yield value and discarded the grade-decision context (retest flags, original intended grade) that identifies the mechanism. The one search aimed at a published downgrade rate returned only vendor claims about reducing off-grade, which is the commercial framing of the same quantity.
(3) The specific mechanism: conservatism absolved the inquiry. The field's own words are available for this. P1 saw the notch and wrote that its chosen distribution "conservatively, neglects any truncation at the specified yield stress value" — i.e. ignoring the selection was understood to err on the safe side for DESIGN resistance, so modelling it offered no safety gain and carried a professional risk of liberalising phi. That is a rational stopping rule for a design-code paper and a bad one for the field, because the same neglect is not conservative in the two places it now matters: for the lower grade of a downgrading pair (higher CoV and bimodality raise lower-tail mass), and for assessment of existing structures (where the mean margin is credited upward into load ratings). A question that is only interesting where the standard conservatism argument fails will not be asked by people applying that argument.
