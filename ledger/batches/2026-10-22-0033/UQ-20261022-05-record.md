Domain(s): Biology — experimental methods and research-reagent practice (immunoassays in cell and molecular biology). Adjacent: measurement metrology, reagent reporting standards, meta-research on reporting completeness.

Question (canonical): [R1, adopted at Gate 1] For a pre-declared panel of commercial research antibodies, specified by catalogue number and datasheet-of-record date: (i) is the vendor-recommended working dilution for a named application predicted by the product's stated stock concentration, or by a measurable binding constant and the nominal abundance of the target in the recommended sample type, under a pre-declared comparison of the two models; and (ii) across a pre-declared sample of published immunoassays using antibodies from that panel, in what fraction can the mass-per-volume or molar concentration of antibody present in the incubation be computed at all from the paper plus that datasheet, and among papers quoting the same nominal dilution for the same catalogue number, what is the spread in computed concentration?

(V0, superseded at Gate 1: "is the vendor-recommended working dilution predicted by the antibody's affinity and the assay's nominal target abundance, or by the vendor's stock concentration — and, across published immunoassays using those same antibodies, in what fraction can the molar concentration of antibody actually present in the reaction be reconstructed at all from the paper plus the datasheet?" Reformulated only for well-posedness: V0 left "reconstructible" undefined, did not fix the datasheet as a dated artefact — datasheets are silently revised, so a later lookup is a different measurement — did not name the application (a product carries different recommended dilutions for WB, IF, IHC and ELISA, so "the" recommended dilution is not a single quantity), and did not state that molar reconstruction requires an assumed molecular weight, which for a polyclonal antiserum is not defined. R1 adds: per-application rows; a dated datasheet of record; "computable" defined as a mass/volume concentration in the incubation obtainable from stated stock concentration × stated dilution, with the molar step reported separately and only where an IgG molecular weight assumption is declared; and coverage of the affinity arm reported as a result rather than assumed.)

Alternative formulations:
(a) Where does "1:1000" come from — the antibody's biophysics, or the bottle's packaging?
(b) Biology's most-used reagent class is specified by a dilution rather than a concentration: how often is the dose recoverable from the published record, and is the recommended dilution a property of the antibody at all?

Motivating gap: Immunoassay methods sections specify antibodies by dilution, and a dilution is a concentration only if the stock concentration is known — which many products do not state. The dense how-to literature on titration instructs users to optimise the dilution empirically and is silent on where the vendor's recommended figure came from. If recommended dilution tracks stock concentration rather than affinity, the convention is a packaging artefact, and two papers both reporting "1:1000" can differ in antibody dose by orders of magnitude.

CONTESTED and not checked: if most purified products ship at a near-constant nominal concentration, the packaging model has little variance to explain, and what the regression would actually recover is product *format* (purified IgG vs antiserum vs supernatant vs ascites), which is already bench lore. What would count as an answer: a pre-registered two-part study. (i) A regression of recommended dilution (log scale, one row per product × application) on stated stock concentration, a measured or vendor-stated binding constant, and nominal target abundance in the recommended sample type, with variance partitioned and the packaging model tested against the affinity model under a comparison declared in advance; product format and vendor entered as covariates so that a stock-concentration effect cannot be read off format alone. (ii) A reconstructibility audit over published immunoassays reporting the share in which antibody concentration in the incubation is computable from paper plus dated datasheet, with the identifier-coverage bound reported, and, for the computable subset, the spread in realized concentration among papers quoting the same nominal dilution for the same catalogue number. A finding that dilution tracks affinity and that concentration is usually recoverable answers the question negatively and closes the concern.

## Gate 3 — Significance

**Consequence.** If dilution tracks stock concentration and concentration is usually not recoverable: the field's most-used reagent class is specified in a unit that is a property of the container, the published dose of that reagent is routinely lost, and two papers reporting the same nominal dilution may differ in antibody dose by whatever the spread in stock concentration is — which the study would measure rather than assert. That converts a reporting-checklist preference (ask for µg/mL) into a quantified defect with a size, and gives meta-researchers a reason to treat nominal dilution as non-comparable across papers. If dilution tracks a binding constant and target abundance, and concentration is usually recoverable: the convention is a compressed biophysical recommendation, the worry is wrong, and the concern closes — which is a real possible outcome and is why this is a question and not a thesis.

**Audience.** (1) Journals and reporting-standard bodies that already maintain antibody reporting items — the community that produced prior B's Box 1 and the RRID/identifier push, and whichever checklist A's recommendations feed. They would act by adding a concentration field with a defined fallback when stock concentration is unavailable, and the study tells them whether the field is satisfiable before they mandate it. (2) Vendors' technical-documentation teams: if the recommendation carries no biophysical information, printing a stock concentration and a µg/mL working range is a cheap change, and the study names which products the change would matter for. (3) Meta-researchers and systematic reviewers of immunoassay literature, who currently pool across papers on nominal dilution; limb (ii)'s spread figure is the quantity that tells them whether that pooling is defensible. (4) Quantitative immunoassay and affinity-proteomics modellers, who need an actual concentration to use a published result at all.

**Fecundity.** Once dose recoverability has a number: does reported effect size covary with reconstructed dose among papers using the same product? Does the dilution convention propagate by methods-section inheritance (papers citing papers) rather than from datasheets? Does mandating a concentration field change what gets reported, testable as a before/after at a journal that adopts one? Is the same unit-of-measurement defect present in other reagent classes specified by dilution — stains, lectins, secondary conjugates, blocking sera? And on limb (i): if format, not affinity, carries the recommendation, what *would* an affinity-aware recommendation look like, and is it better at the bench?

**Anchoring.** The research-antibody reproducibility literature, in which prior B sits; prior A's measured antibody reporting gaps, including the 1% lot-number figure and the unquantified RRID scarcity, which bound limb (ii)'s denominator; the titration how-to literature's instruction to optimise empirically, which is dense on method and empty on provenance (confirmed by queries 1, 6, 8 and 10 returning only vendor pages and forum threads); and the absence of a concentration field from the antibody reporting standards this run could read.

**Reason for silence.** A conceptual-inheritance gap plus a disciplinary boundary, both nameable. (1) In classical serology the dilution *is* the measurand — a titre is a dilution, and the reagent was an antiserum of unknown composition, so expressing dose as a dilution was not a loss of information but the only honest statement available. The convention survived the reagent's transition to purified and then recombinant protein of stated mass concentration, at which point it became a container property; conventions do not get re-examined when the thing they describe changes underneath them. (2) The boundary: the titration literature owns the dilution as an *optimisation* problem to be solved locally per lab, under which the vendor's figure is merely a starting guess and its provenance is irrelevant to the user, so that literature has no reason to ask where it came from. The reporting-standards and meta-research literature owns completeness and codes fields as present/absent — prior A's instrument is exactly that — so it cannot see a present-but-uninterpretable field. Neither owns the question "is the printed number a property of the analyte or of the packaging?". (3) An access asymmetry: limb (i) is best answered with vendors' in-house titration records, which are not public, and limb (ii) requires joining papers to dated datasheets, a cross-boundary data task with no disciplinary owner. This is not "no one thought of it".
