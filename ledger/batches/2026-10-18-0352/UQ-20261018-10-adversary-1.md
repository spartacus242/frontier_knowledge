## Restatement (my own words)

Take one country's fleet of electronic household electricity meters as it actually sits in the field. Draw a genuinely random sample from the utility's own register, stratified by meter model, firmware and year built. Put each sampled meter on a bench (or in place) and feed it a fixed, published set of distorted household current waveforms — the kind produced by dimmers, LED drivers, switch-mode supplies, inverters, variable-speed pumps — while a reference instrument that has itself been shown not to be fooled by those waveforms measures the true energy. Question 1: for each waveform mix, how wrong is the fleet — population-weighted mean error with a confidence interval, and the lower and upper tails? Question 2: given a declared national picture of what households run, what fraction of the kilowatt-hours actually billed does that error distribution imply are wrong?

The candidate contrasts this with what exists: bench demonstrations that particular meters can be very wrong on particular loads (UTwente/VSL 2016–2023), a test-method-and-waveform-library project (EMPIR 17NRM02 MeterEMI), and in-service acceptance-sampling regimes that return accept/reject under verification conditions rather than an error distribution under realistic loads.

It does not report MeterEMI final results.
