## Restatement in my own words

Take physical quantities (particle masses and lifetimes, the neutron lifetime, fundamental constants) that have each been measured many times independently. For every published measurement record the value, the quoted one-sigma uncertainty, the date, and — hand-coded from the paper — whether and how the analysis was blinded. Judge each error bar by how far the measurement lands from a later, much better reference value in units of its own quoted sigma. The question asks whether the realism of those error bars (the Birge-ratio-like factor that would make the pulls unit-variance) differs between the blinded and the unblinded measurements once quantity, era and precision are held fixed; or whether blinding is so tied to era and collaboration that the two strata cannot be told apart. The record insists that the separate, already-asked question of whether blinding changes the CENTRAL VALUES is excluded by design, and that this exclusion is what makes the candidate new.

- Henrion & Fischhoff 1986 (Am. J. Phys. 54:791), https://www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/bias/1986-henrion.pdf. Dataset: c (27 values, 1875-1958), G (14), proton magnetic moment (7), alpha^-1 (24 high-accuracy, 14 low-accuracy), ohm ratio (7), particle lifetimes (92) and masses (214), and 40 recommended values. Statistics: Birge ratio R_B (SD of normalized residuals, expected 1), interquartile index, surprise index (percent outside the assessed 98% interval, expected 2%). Values include R_B = 1.42 for c (SI = 11%), 1.38 for G (SI = 29%), 2.95 for high-accuracy alpha^-1, SI = 57% for recommended values, and R_B = 0.40 for the ohm ratio. Era split: R_B "was 1.47 for measurements up to 1941 and 1.32 since 1947." Explanatory passage (Section VII): "Having a preexisting recommended value may particularly encourage investigators to discard or adjust unexpected results", followed (fetch-tool paraphrase of the next sentence) by the prediction that this produces correlated errors across apparently independent experiments, small Birge ratios and "unduly tight confidence intervals", and "can explain what Franklin has termed the 'bandwagon effect'". Remedy: "referees might be asked to scrutinize the account of an experiment 'blind,' before knowing its outcome." Also: "Others tend to underestimate the sources of systematic error in their experiments." The paper does not stratify by blinding (blinding as a protocol barely existed in 1986); it stratifies by era and accuracy class.
- Klein & Roodman 2005 (Annu. Rev. Nucl. Part. Sci. 55:141), https://www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/bias/2005-klein.pdf. - Golub, arXiv:2311.13542, https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2311.13542. Thesis: blind analysis has little place in physics because evidence for experimenter bias is thin and the costs are real. ## Own arithmetic (labelled; Klein & Roodman state no uncertainties on these chi2 values)

From the two verified chi2 values: chi2/dof = 1.581 against the contemporaneous prior average, 3.045 against the final average; the implied uncertainty-calibration factor c = sqrt(chi2/dof) is 1.26 vs 1.75, a 39% change, from the choice of reference alone (chi2 ratio 1.90). Sample: four quantities, ~84 measurements, overwhelmingly from the unblinded era — i.e. exactly the candidate's unblinded stratum. The decomposition used below is the elementary identity E[(X-T)^2] = (E[X]-T)^2 + Var(X); the "shrinkage" form of herding (x_final = A + (1-h)(x_raw - A)) is my own model, offered to show the sign of the artifact, not a published result.
